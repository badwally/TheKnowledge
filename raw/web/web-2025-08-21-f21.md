## Abstract

Recently, we have witnessed the revolutionary development of deep learning. As the application domain of deep learning has expanded, its privacy risks have attracted attention since deep leaning methods often use private data for training. Some methods for attacking deep learning, such as membership inference attacks, increase the privacy risks of deep learning models. One risk-reducing defensive strategy with great potential is to apply some degree of random perturbation during the training (or other) phase. Therefore, differential privacy, as a privacy protection framework originally designed for publishing data, is widely used to protect the privacy of deep learning models due to its solid mathematical foundation. In this paper, we first introduce several attack methods that threaten deep learning. Then, we systematically review the cross-applications of differential privacy and deep learning to protect deep learning models. We encourage researchers to visually demonstrate the defense effects of their approaches in the literature rather than solely providing rigorous mathematical proofs. In addition to privacy, we also discuss and review the impact of differential privacy on the robustness, overfitting, and fairness of deep neural networks. Finally, we analyze some potential future research directions, highlighting the significant potential for differential privacy to make positive contributions to future deep learning systems.

## 1 Introduction

In recent decades, we have witnessed remarkable advancements in artificial intelligence, primarily due to the progress made regarding deep learning techniques (Herrmann and Kollmannsberger 2024; Archana and Jeevaraj 2024; Zhang et al. 2023b, 2022; Wang et al. 2024). The achievements of contemporary deep learning methods rely on the enhancement of computing power and the availability of large-scale data. Data for deep learning are akin to blood for the human body, providing it with a steady stream of vitality. Many excellent public datasets that are available for use have emerged with the rise of deep learning, such as MNIST (LeCun 1998) and CIFAR10 (Krizhevsky et al. 2009), and these datasets have objectively promoted the advancement of deep learning technology. It is worth noting that public datasets contain little or no personal private information. However, as the application fields of deep learning further expand, a large number of private datasets (including private information) are used to train deep learning models and even increase the demand for data sharing during the model training process. For example, if multiple medical centers want to jointly train an online medical service system based on federated learning, multiple hospitals need to coordinate their training procedures or share their own private data during the training process. This undoubtedly increases the risk of individual privacy leakage. Furthermore, even using centralized training poses privacy risks since deep learning models tend to memorize the features of the training data (Arpit et al. 2017; Meehan et al. 2020).

Currently, the main privacy risks faced by deep learning include membership inference attacks (Bai et al. 2024; Wu et al. 2024; Shokri et al. 2017), model inversion attacks (Zhang et al. 2023a; Zhu et al. 2023), and model extraction attacks (Yan et al. 2022a, 2022b). For example, membership inference attacks aim to determine whether a particular data point is part of a neural network’s training dataset. Such attacks highlight the need to develop robust privacy-preserving techniques in deep learning to safeguard sensitive data and models.

Both the widespread use of private data and the advancement of attack methods have increased the privacy risks faced by deep learning. Therefore, the privacy and security of deep learning have attracted widespread attention (Pan et al. 2024). In the field of privacy protection, numerous methods are available for protecting user information, such as *k*-anonymity (Li et al. 2022b; Sweeney 2002), homomorphic encryption (Fontaine and Galand 2007; Acar et al. 2018), *l*-diversity (Machanavajjhala et al. 2007; Li e al. 2006), and secure multiparty computation (Goldreich 1998; Du and Atallah 2001). In particular, homomorphic encryption and secure multiparty computation have also been used to train neural networks for privacy protection (Knott et al. 2021; Tran et al. 2021a; Phong et al. 2018; Marcano et al. 2019). Most of these methods desensitize or encrypt data into ciphertext. Differential privacy (DP), originally developed as a privacy mechanism for data publishing (Dwork 2006; Friedman and Schuster 2010), guarantees that no fragment of data can be traced back to an individual by introducing noise to the query results, thus making it difficult for attackers to perform privacy-violating attacks (Dwork 2008). Currently, DP is emerging as an increasingly significant instrument for defending against privacy attacks in deep learning, and researchers have attempted to regard deep learning as a complicated data querying system to allow DP to prove its effectiveness in this process.

The DP mechanism mitigates threats by introducing a certain degree of random perturbation in the training or prediction phase. The abstract notion states that a DP mechanism should produce similar outputs for adjacent inputs (Dwork et al. 2014). Given two adjacent datasets *D* and *D*′ that differ by only one record, *O* is a possible output. With appropriate parameters and prudently handmade random noise, a randomized algorithm \( {\mathcal{M}} \) outputs similar results for the input datasets *D* and *D*′. As a result, it is difficult to infer from the output whether a particular record is involved in the input dataset. Random noise often proves to be an indispensable mitigation measure for certain vulnerabilities (Li et al. 2021), such as inference attacks (Wu et al. 2019; Chang and Li 2018). For adversarial attacks, regularized noise can help to better formalize defenses (Lecuyer et al. 2019), thus providing meaningful guarantees of producing correct model predictions for malicious inputs. DP not only formalizes the amount of noise needed for a given target utility but also acts as a general mechanism for implementing it.

In addition to privacy guarantees, the significance of DP in deep learning is extended to topics such as overfitting, fairness and robustness. These extensions encompass both positive and negative effects. Concerning overfitting, DP has been demonstrated to considerably enhance the generalization performance of deep neural networks, thus contributing to mitigating the overfitting effect (Dwork et al. 2015; Yeom et al. 2018). Regarding fairness, it has been suggested that DP encourages unfairness in neural networks (Bagdasaryan et al. 2019); however, this issue can be mitigated with proper technical interventions (Farrand et al. 2020; Tran et al. 2021b). In terms of robustness, DP provides a scheme for neural networks to defend against adversarial attacks, regardless of the specific implementation of the attack (Phan et al. 2019; Xu et al. 2020), and the DP mechanism is more robust to poisoning attacks than other approaches (Du et al. 2019). In summary, the effect of DP in deep learning exceeds its original assumptions, and scholars should carefully consider these impacts in their work. This survey provides a comprehensive review of the existing research and makes recommendations for future work in this field.

### 3.3 Important properties

The important properties of DP include three aspects: sequential composition, parallel composition, and post-processing immunity. These properties can be effectively used to analyze the privacy guarantees of complex DP mechanisms.

**Sequential composition property** (McSherry and Talwar 2007). This property indicates that if a series of algorithms each offers a specific level of DP on their own, executing these algorithms in succession will uphold the overall privacy assurance.

Formally, given dataset *D*, when employing algorithms \( {\mathcal {M}}(D) = \left( {\mathcal {M}}_1(D), {\mathcal {M}}_2(D), \ldots , {\mathcal {M}}_N(D) \right) \) that adhere to DP mechanisms, each with its respective privacy budget \( \{\epsilon _1, \epsilon _2, \ldots , \epsilon _N\} \), the output sequence \( \{O_1, O_2, \ldots , O_N\} \) of \( {\mathcal {M}}(D) \) ensures DP with a cumulative privacy guarantee of \( \sum _{n=1}^{N} \epsilon _n \).

**Parallel composition property** (Dwork et al. 2006; McSherry 2009). This property indicates that applying several non-communicating DP mechanisms concurrently on separate datasets will offer privacy guarantees that are at least as robust as those obtained by using a single mechanism on the merged dataset.

Formally, given a dataset *D*, when utilizing random algorithms \( {\mathcal {M}}(D) = \left( {\mathcal {M}}_1(D_1), {\mathcal {M}}_2(D_2), \ldots , {\mathcal {M}}_N(D_N) \right) \), which apply DP mechanisms to introduce noise into the non-overlapping subsets \( \{D_1, D_2, \ldots , D_N\} \) of the dataset *D*, each associated with privacy budgets \( \{\epsilon _1, \epsilon _2, \ldots , \epsilon _N\} \), the resulting output sequence \( \{O_1, O_2, \ldots , O_N\} \) from the algorithms \( {\mathcal {M}}(D) \) will satisfy \( \max _{1 \le n \le N} \epsilon _n \)-DP.

**Post-processing immunity** (Balle and Wang 2018). This property emphasizes that if a mechanism produces results that satisfy DP, any subsequent calculations or operations based on that output will not affect the level of privacy protection, and the privacy of the output will remain unchanged.

Formally, when a stochastic algorithm \( {\mathcal {M}}: {\mathcal {D}} \rightarrow {\mathcal {R}} \) upholds \( \epsilon \)-DP, and given \( {\mathcal {F}}: {\mathcal {R}} \rightarrow {\mathcal {R}}' \) as an arbitrary deterministic function, the composite function \( {\mathcal {F}}\circ {\mathcal {M}}: {\mathcal {D}}\rightarrow {\mathcal {R}}' \) maintains \( \epsilon \)-DP guarantees.

## 4 Deep learning privacy risks

Privacy attacks against deep learning can be roughly divided into two types. The first type of attack targets training data, such as model inversion attacks, which aim to obtain information about training data. Another kind of attack is an attack against a model, such as a model stealing attack, where the attacker tries to restore the parameters of a well-trained model to achieve the same or similar functions.

### 4.1 Attacks against training data

**Membership inference attacks** (Shokri et al. 2017). Membership inference attacks aim to ascertain if a specific data record is included in the training dataset of a model. Membership inference attacks can be set as black-box attacks (Shokri et al. 2017) or white-box attacks (Yeom et al. 2018). In Shokri et al. (2017), a membership inference attack was conducted in a black-box setting, where the attacker could query the target model but lacked knowledge of any other details about the model, such as its parameters or network architecture. Fig. 2 demonstrates an iconic membership inference model in a black-box setting. Generally, such attacks involve the utilization of shadow models in black-box scenarios, through which the attacker can obtain a decision boundary similar to that of the target model and later acquire a malicious model that can infer the affiliations of specific records in the training dataset. Note that membership inference attacks can be performed without using shadow models but are often less accurate than those using shadow models.

In recent years, membership inference attacks have continued to evolve, intensifying the threat to deep learning models (Hu et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2022, 2024b). For example, Liu et al. (2022) proposed a membership inference attack based on loss trajectories. To conduct the attack in a typical black-box setting, this method utilizes knowledge distillation and represents membership information through the losses evaluated across a series of intermediate models (i.e., the distillation loss trajectories) at different distillation epochs, along with the loss of the specified target model. Bai et al. (2024) investigated the threat of membership inference attacks to federated learning. To defend against member inference attacks, some defense mechanisms have been developed (Hu et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2024a).

**Model inversion attacks** (Fredrikson et al. 2014). Model inversion attacks, also known as attribute inference attacks (Mehnaz et al. 2022), are attacks that analyze the correlations between known information and data to infer sensitive attributes or features of individuals from the target training model. Attackers leverage these correlations to deduce sensitive attributes by maximizing the posterior probability estimate with the purpose of reconstructing the input data. For example, Fredrikson et al. (Fredrikson et al. 2015) utilized the confidence scores offered by a facial recognition system API to construct an attack model. Given only API access to the facial recognition system, a user’s name could be input to obtain their identifiable image.

Recently, there has been a growing number of works dedicated to model inversion attacks (Nguyen et al. 2023; Han et al. 2023; Nguyen et al. 2024), indicating the evolution of threat techniques. In response, corresponding model defense mechanisms have also been proposed one after another (Gong et al. 2023).

**Gradient leakage attacks** Gradient leakage attacks allow attackers to try to infer some details of the training data by observing the gradient information of the associated model, thereby violating data privacy and confidentiality (Wei and Liu 2021). Generally, gradient leakage attacks are carried out in distributed training scenarios, such as federated learning (Zhu et al. 2019; Wei et al. 2020), but these attacks are high-risk threats to both the centralized cloud and edge clients (Geiping et al. 2020).

### 4.2 Attacks against models

**Model extraction attacks** (Tramèr et al. 2016). Model extraction attacks, also called model stealing attacks, aim to steal the predictive pattern of the target model by duplicating a substitute model. The attacker uses specifically crafted samples to iteratively query the target model and extracts the prediction pattern of the target model to the maximum extent possible through the predictions returned by the target model. Then, the adversary trains the substitute model with the carefully crafted samples and the coupled predictions obtained by the target model. A schematic diagram of the model extraction attack process is shown in Fig. 3. Model extraction attacks threaten the intellectual property of model owners and destroy the confidentiality of their models (Yan et al. 2022b; Pal et al. 2019).

In recent studies, Wu et al. (2022) introduced a model extraction attack aimed at graph neural networks. This approach begins by generating queries that resemble legitimate nodes within the target graph. It then uses the responses to these queries, along with available structural information, to reconstruct the model. Miura et al. (2024) introduced a data-free model extraction attack aimed at explainable AI, which offers gradient-based explanations for inference outcomes. To defend against model extraction attacks, literature (Jiang et al. 2023) present constructive defense strategies.

## 5 DP in deep learning

In light of the many privacy threats faced by contemporary deep learning methods, researchers have made great efforts to protect deep learning models, among which DP mechanisms are widely favored by scholars. In this section, we comprehensively review the progress achieved by DP in the field of deep learning in recent years.

### 5.1 DP types: adaptive and non-adaptive

Since Abadi et al. proposed DP-SGD (Abadi et al. 2016), the DP mechanism has been rapidly and widely applied in the field of deep learning. DP-SGD has become the gold standard in this area and is extensively used in both academia and industry due to its integration into major deep learning platforms such as PyTorch and TensorFlow. DP-SGD preserves the private information of training data by injecting the same level of Gaussian noise into the gradients. The key to this method lies in limiting sensitivity through a fixed gradient clipping threshold and ensuring overall noise levels with a fixed privacy budget. However, studies (Gong et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2023c) have shown that although DP-SGD theoretically provides strong DP guarantees, it severely compromises the utility-privacy trade-off of the model.

Therefore, impairment of the utility of the model is a significant limitation of DP in deep learning. By introducing noise to ensure privacy protection, DP inevitably distorts the underlying data and its representations. This distortion can occur at various stages, whether it involves the input data, the gradients during the training process, or the final output predictions, leading to decreased accuracy and performance (Pan et al. 2024). Furthermore, the trade-off between privacy and utility becomes a critical concern, as excessively strong privacy guarantees may render the model less useful for practical applications, ultimately limiting its effectiveness in real-world tasks. Thus, finding a balanced approach that maintains adequate utility while ensuring robust privacy remains a ongoing challenge in implementing DP in deep learning contexts.

To address this challenge, recent efforts have focused on developing adaptive DP mechanisms (Gong et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2023c; Bu et al. 2024; Xia et al. 2023). The existing adaptive DP mechanisms can be roughly divided into two categories, including adaptive gradient clipping (Bu et al. 2024; Xia et al. 2023) and adaptive privacy budget allocation (Gong et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2023c).

For adaptive gradient clipping, the choice of clipping threshold is critical for achieving high accuracy under DP. However, adjusting the clipping threshold incurs additional training costs in the search for an optimal value and may result in a suboptimal threshold. Yang et al. (2022) and Bu et al. (2024) simultaneously proposed a normalization gradient clipping method that couples the learning rate with the threshold parameter, eliminating the need for separate adjustment of the threshold parameter. Nonetheless, this approach relies on a monotonic weight function, which exerts excessive weights on small gradient samples and introduces additional bias into the updates. Xia et al. (2023) improved upon (Bu et al. 2024; Yang et al. 2022) by introducing a differential private per-sample adaptive clipping algorithm based on a non-monotonic adaptive weight function. This algorithm not only ensures privacy but also avoids the typical hyperparameter tuning process associated with constant clipping, significantly reducing the bias between the updates and the true batch average gradients. Compared to DP-SGD, these efforts enhance the usability of the model while maintaining the same privacy budget.

In terms of privacy budget allocation, complex neural network models can be viewed as comprising different sub-models, to which different privacy budgets are assigned. The overall privacy of the model is analyzed using the composition properties of DP. For instance, Gong et al. (2020) proposed a general deep learning framework with DP based on correlation analysis, perturbing the gradient according to the associations between the neurons in different layers and the model outcome. During backpropagation, the gradients of neurons that are less correlated with the model output add more noise, and vice versa. Chen et al. (2023) effectively alleviated the gradient distortion problem by designing a grouped gradient clipping method. They integrated gradient perturbation based on layerwise relevance propagation (Bach et al. 2015) into the algorithm, adaptively injecting noise according to the correlation between each feature and the model output. This approach achieved adaptive privacy budget allocation, resulting in improved model utility.

In summary, adaptive gradient clipping achieves adaptive sensitivity, while adaptive privacy budget allocation facilitates dynamic privacy budget distribution during the training process. Both methods adjust the parameters that govern the scale of noise in the training framework, enhancing the trade-off between model utility and privacy.

It is important to emphasize that although efforts in adaptive DP mechanisms have mitigated the damage that DP does to model utility, the detrimental impact on utility still persists, especially when assigning a smaller privacy parameter. It can be seen that as the privacy budget increases, the accuracy increases, but the privacy protection strength decreases. This is because a larger \( (\epsilon ) \) allows less noise to be injected, reducing the interference with the gradient update. In practical applications, the choice of privacy budget is often determined by multi-dimensional factors. For example, in highly sensitive scenarios such as medical data, a low \( \epsilon \) value should be adopted. Even if there is a loss in accuracy, privacy protection should be given priority. In addition, the choice of factor budget should also take into account legal privacy requirements. For instance, the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) have put forward legal requirements for personal privacy. However, adaptive privacy budget allocation methods (Gong et al. 2020; Chen et al. 2023) significantly increase training overhead compared to DP-SGD, as they involve computing the contributions of neurons to the output during the training process.

### 5.2 Locations of DP noise

The data processing strategy in a deep learning model is divided into three stages, namely, an input layer, hidden layers and an output layer. Adding DP noise in these three stages to perturb the training process is already a common design scheme, as shown in Fig. 4. Table 4 provides a systematic summary of key locations for differential privacy noise injection in deep learning architectures.

**In the input layer**, DP noise is primarily injected into the training data (Heikkilä et al. 2017). Adding noise directly to the training data is the most direct and effective way to protect them because the noise can mask or interfere with sensitive attributes in the data, thereby resisting attacks against the data, such as attribute inference attacks. However, introducing noise to the training data can have a substantial impact on the usefulness of the trained model. The learning process heavily relies on the attribute values that are present in the training dataset. Therefore, training a model using a dataset whose attribute values have been extensively altered is equivalent to utilizing the “wrong” dataset.

One mitigation technique is to inject adaptive noise into the input data during training. Phan et al. (2017) added DP noise to the input layer of their network by using layerwise relevance propagation (Bach et al. 2015), which was employed to learn the associations between the input data (features) and the model output, on a neural network. Noise is adaptively added according to the saliency of the features, resulting in a privacy budget that is completely independent of the number of training epochs, allowing upper privacy bounds to be efficiently designed and controlled in advance.

**In the hidden layers**, the training data are protected by introducing noise to the gradients during backpropagation. This straightforward approach applies DP to deep learning models and represents one of the earliest endeavors to incorporate privacy safeguards. The primary objective is to prevent adversaries from inferring precise personal information about the training data from the model’s outputs. After Abadi et al. (2016) proposed DP-SGD, follow-up studies proposed a series of improvement schemes to balance the utility of a model and its privacy guarantees. For example, Gong et al. (2020) and Zhang et al. (2023c) proposed adaptive schemes for reducing unnecessary privacy budgets during training. Papernot et al. (2021) suggested that using tempered sigmoid activation functions can improve the model performance of the DP-SGD algorithm without designing a new DP scheme.

**In the output layer**, noise can be directly added to the output or loss function, or the DP mechanism can be implemented by perturbing labels. Papernot et al. (2018) proposed private aggregation of teacher ensembles (PATE), which served as an effective demonstration of DP output perturbation techniques. PATE uses multiple teacher networks, differential privacy aggregation, and public student networks to cut off the direct connections between private datasets and student networks. The students only learn the aggregated output of each teacher’s perturbation, not the sensitive data. The teacher networks are trained on privately labeled datasets, achieving improved accuracy and consistency. The unlabeled public data are predicted by the teachers, sent to a differentiated private aggregator and added with DP noise. The output of the aggregator is combined with public data to form a new training set for student network training.

For the perturbation loss function, Phan et al. (2016) introduced a DP autoencoder that perturbs the cross-entropy error function of the data reconstruction layer and softmax layer. They utilized Taylor expansions to approximate polynomial representations of the cross-entropy error function and subsequently incorporated noise into these polynomials. As a result, their approach achieves \( \epsilon \)-DP during the training phase.

In terms of perturbing labels, label DP is an excellent scheme for defending against adversaries inferring data privacy from label information (Malek Esmaeili et al. 2021; Ghazi et al. 2021). For example, Ghazi et al. (2021) introduced label DP to the field of deep learning for the first time and proposed a multilevel privacy protection method that protects sensitive information in a model by applying label DP technology at different deep learning model levels.

### 5.3 DP in neural network models

Since the development of deep learning, many widely used neural network models have been formed. These neural networks are very effective in many machine learning tasks. For example, convolutional neural networks have made outstanding contributions in the field of computer vision. Recurrent neural networks are very effective for temporal classification/forecasting tasks. Here, we review the recent applications of DP to common neural network models (Table 5).

**DP in Graph Neural Networks (GNNs)** Sajadmanesh and Gatica-Perez (2021) proposed a privacy-preserving GNN method to achieve local privacy protection by integrating noise into node embeddings. A local DP mechanism can effectively protect the privacy of nodes while maintaining high-quality embedded representations. Furthermore, to find a balance between preserving privacy and maintaining prediction accuracy, by minimizing the tradeoff between the privacy leakage risk and the task loss of the prediction model, the method can maintain high prediction performance while preserving privacy. Tran et al. (2022) proposed a heterogeneous randomized response mechanism for DP in GANs to protect node features and edges from privacy inference attacks. By introducing different levels of randomness to node characteristics and adjacencies, the risk of information leakage is effectively reduced, and the protection ability of DP is improved. Sajadmanesh et al. (2023) proposed a differentially private GNN that incorporates aggregated perturbations. This approach involves adding random noise to a GNN’s aggregation function, resulting in blurred feature differences between neighboring vertices. By leveraging multihop neighborhood aggregation, the proposed GNN ensures both edge-level and node-level DP. Importantly, this privacy protection is maintained during both the training and inference process without requiring additional resources beyond the allocated training privacy budget.

**DP in Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs)** Tian et al. (2022) proposed a DP Bi-LSTM model, which uses a key generator to encrypt user data and protect user privacy. Then, a noise mechanism was introduced to blur the data and increase the privacy protection effect. To further improve the accuracy and robustness of the model, model fusion technology was introduced to effectively combine multiple prediction models. Chen et al. (2020a) proposed an RNN model based on a DP mechanism for dynamic trajectory privacy protection in networks. This method minimizes the risk of privacy disclosure by incorporating the concept of DP into the trajectory data processing strategy. Noise addition technology is adopted to confuse the original data by introducing noise to the trajectory data to realize the protection of individual privacy. Then, using the characteristics of RNNs, this method can model and predict the dynamic trajectory data. Feng et al. (2023) proposed a differentially private tensor-based RNN aimed at addressing privacy breach risks in sequence data analysis. Specifically, a tensor-based perturbed temporal backpropagation algorithm was developed to avoid disclosing private information when training tensor-based RNN models within a DP framework.

**DP in Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)** Bu et al. (2022) proposed an efficient and scalable implementation of gradient clipping for convolutional layers, which significantly simplified training privacy in terms of temporal and spatial complexity without compromising accuracy. This method aims to address the privacy leakage issue when training large CNNs and is able to efficiently handle large-scale datasets. Li et al. (2020) proposed an algorithm for DP subspace approximation with an adjusted bias to protect the training data in an FF-CNN. By integrating differentially private noise into CNNs and properly controlling and tuning it, this research enabled the utilize network to produce interpretable results while preserving the privacy of the original data.

### 5.4 DP in deep learning frameworks

In this article, we refer to an architecture where specific neural network models can be deployed as a deep learning *framework*. For example, in generative adversarial networks (GANs), carefully selected neural network models such as CNN or GNN models are used in the generators and discriminators according to the given task requirements. Therefore, the GAN is called a GAN *framework* (Table 6).

**DP in Autoencoders (AEs)** Li et al. (2023) introduced an approach referred to as a differentially private deep graph autoencoder, which tackles the challenge of anonymous graph publishing while ensuring privacy and usability. This approach transforms the problem into a privacy preservation task for generative models and leverages a multitask learning technique to optimize models with respect to both privacy and usability objectives. Weggenmann et al. (2022) introduced a method called DP-VAE for the anonymization of human-readable text data in online reviews. DP-VAE uses variational autoencoders to learn the representations of comment data. By introducing a DP mechanism during the training process, DP-VAE can ensure that the real identities or sensitive information of individual users cannot be inferred even during the learning process. This enables the DP-VAE approach to preserve user privacy while still maintaining the readability and quality of user-generated text. Fang et al. (2022) proposed a DP recommendation system based on variational autoencoders. By incorporating a DP mechanism into the training processes of variational autoencoders, the resulting model cannot accurately restore the original data when processing user data. Therefore, the user’s privacy is protected. In addition, to balance the accuracy of recommendation and the need for privacy protection, reconstruction loss and regularization terms were also introduced in the training process.

**DP in Meta-Learning (ML)** ML aims to equip machine learning algorithms with the ability to learn how to learn, focusing on the design of general-purpose learning algorithms that can adaptively learn different tasks. In ML, the employed model must acquire generalization capabilities by learning a series of related tasks, which may lead to privacy risks due to the possible privacy correlations between tasks.

Zhou and Bassily (2022) proposed a task-level DP meta-learning method that uses DP technology to protect the privacy of task data and performs noise injection during the meta-learning process to ensure DP. Specifically, the method encodes the given task data by introducing a differentially private encoder and learns them in the embedding space. Then, a specific DP algorithm is used to inject noise into the encoded task data to protect the privacy of the data. Finally, by meta-learning tasks in the embedding space, the model is able to generalize better to new tasks. Li et al. (2019) proposed a differentially private meta-learning approach to address the privacy issue of protecting sensitive data in personalized learning tasks. Specifically, shared knowledge is extracted by learning multiple tasks, and transfer learning is performed in new tasks. To protect the privacy of the individual data involved in the training process, the authors introduced a DP mechanism. By using a privacy-preserving gradient update rule, the method can provide strong privacy protection during the learning process, reducing the risk of privacy leakage while maintaining data utility.

**DP in Federated Learning (FL)** FL is a distributed machine learning paradigm that allows participants to train their local models on local data, thereby creating contributed models. Although the elimination of data sharing largely mitigates the risk of privacy breaches, sharing model parameters and gradients also presents privacy risks (Gu et al. 2023). To defend against attacks, researchers have made numerous efforts in the field of FL using DP tools (El Ouadrhiri and Abdelhadi 2022).

Sun and Lyu (2021) proposed a federated model distillation method based on noiseless DP, which applies a noise-free DP mechanism to protect the privacy of the model and realizes knowledge distillation for multiple local models through FL. In addition, they proposed an accurate privacy analysis method for evaluating the effect of privacy protection. Maddock et al. (2022) improved the enhanced decision tree and proposed a new federated enhanced decision tree model by introducing DP noise and model aggregation technology to federated learning. DP technology was applied to the federated enhanced decision trees. By improving the enhanced decision tree model, introducing DP noise and applying federated learning technology, we can protect data privacy and achieve good classification and regression performance.

**DP in Reinforcement Learning (RL)** RL is the learning of behavior through trial-and-error interaction with a dynamic environment, and it has become one of the most critical subfields of machine learning. RL currently has a wide range of applications. For example, in the field of medical recommendation, patients’ diagnosis and treatment data, which contain much private information, are often used. Privacy is leaked through methods such as inverse reinforcement learning. Therefore, it is very important to study protection mechanisms for RL.

Han et al. (2021) proposed a method that combines DP and generalized linear reinforcement learning, incorporating the concept of local DP into generalized linear reinforcement learning to protect the privacy of personal data. By introducing a DP mechanism to the reinforcement learning algorithm, this method can effectively limit the risk of leaking individual data. Garcelon et al. (2021) proposed a method based on local DP to protect personal data privacy in the regret minimization problem of reinforcement learning. By introducing a DP mechanism, the data are randomized during the learning and exploration process, thereby minimizing the risk of personal data leakage. Wang and Hegde (2019) proposed a method for integrating functional noise into Q-learning to achieve privacy protection. Specifically, a functional noise model based on the gradient direction is first introduced to add noise in the continuous state space to confuse sensitive individual information. Then, by adding noise to the update rules of Q-learning, DP technology is used to control the amplitude of the noise to protect data privacy.

**DP in Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)** GANs work by learning a generative model from the data distribution of the given samples, which is used to estimate the underlying distribution of the dataset. However, when GANs are applied to private or sensitive data, their centralized distribution may leak some key information. Therefore, new technological advancements are needed to mitigate information leakage under GANs, and DP is a good optional technology.

Xu et al. (2019) used DP technology to mitigate the risk of information leakage in GANs and protect sensitive data by incorporating noise into the calculation process. Specifically, they introduced a DP mechanism during the generation and training phases of GANs to ensure the privacy and security of the generated samples. Yang etal. (2020) proposed a PPGAN framework, which modifies the training process of the generator and discriminator so that the generated images do not directly reveal the private information of the original data. Specifically, the proposed method introduces DP random noise in the generator and discriminator and employs a special training method to ensure that the generated images are consistent across different noise instances. This consistency can protect individual privacy and enhance the generalization ability of the generative network.

## 6 Examples of attacks against DP defenses

Although DP mechanisms have been widely used in deep learning and claim to provide privacy protection for deep learning, not all literature on the intersection of DP and deep learning has conducted experiments for resisting attacks. The authors provided rigorous mathematical proofs for the proposed methods to demonstrate that their mechanisms satisfy the DP definition, but conducting rigorous attack-resistant experiments can more directly show the defense effects to readers. Therefore, in this section, we review the DP schemes that successfully resist attacks against neural networks. Table 7 shows several representative examples of DP defense against attacks. It can be seen that with the reduction of privacy budget, the risk of privacy leakage has decreased.

### 6.1 DP against membership inference attacks

As described in Sect. 4.1, the purpose of membership inference attack is to distinguish whether the given data are a component of the training set. In other words, the output of an membership inference attack is one of two categories: member or nonmember. Therefore, membership inference attacks can be seen as binary classification tasks. In this regard, if the accuracy of the attack model is approximately 50%, then the target model is successful in resisting the membership inference attack because this accuracy is close to the probability of random guessing. According to DP’s definition, models with DP inherently restrict the likelihood of successful membership inference attacks that rely solely on the model itself. A large number of studies have shown that DP is effective at defending against membership inference attacks (Leino and Fredrikson 2020; Li and Zhang 2021; Shejwalkar and Houmansadr 2021; Jayaraman and Evans 2019; Jia et al. 2019; Truex et al. 2019).

Chen et al. (2020b) examined the susceptibility of machine learning models to membership inference attacks in the context of genomic data and assessed the efficacy of employing DP as a protective strategy. Their findings indicated that DP helps prevent overfitting and, when combined with model sparsity, can substantially reduce the risk of membership inference attacks. Rahman et al. (2018) demonstrated that deep learning models with DP could fulfill their commitment to safeguarding privacy from powerful adversaries, albeit at the cost of reduced model utility. Conversely, when these models provided acceptable levels of utility, they displayed a moderate susceptibility to membership inference attacks. Yang et al. (2023a) developed a generative framework with DP aimed at mitigating membership inference attacks. They leveraged the information extraction and data generation capabilities of the variational autoencoder to produce synthetic data that satisfies the requirements of differential privacy. Unlike traditional approaches that involve adding noise to the model outputs or altering the training process of the target model, their method focuses on directly processing the original data. Lowy et al. (2024) introduced a privacy concept called practical membership privacy (PMP) to bridge the gap between theoretical and empirical findings in DP. The authors analyzed how PMP can reflect a practical attacker?s uncertainty about private data compared to traditional DP, which focuses on worst-case scenarios. They quantitatively examined two key DP mechanisms: the exponential mechanism and the Gaussian mechanism. The findings reveal that larger privacy parameters often lead to significantly smaller PMP parameters, ensuring robust protection against practical membership inference attacks.

Pan et al. (2021) proposed the FL-PATE method, which incorporated the concept of knowledge transfer into a differentially private federated learning framework. In the federated learning setting, a teacher model is trained, and its knowledge is transferred to a publicly accessible student model for prediction through the aggregation of teacher models on a public dataset. Experiments were conducted on the CIFAR100 dataset, and the ML-leak attack (Salem et al. 2019) method was used for membership inference attack testing. The results showed that the attack accuracy was 50.3%, which is close to that of random guessing. However, the implementation of the FL-PATE method requires a combination of voting aggregation and privacy mechanisms, which increases the complexity of the algorithm and computational costs. Especially on large-scale and complex datasets, this may lead to higher computational and communication overheads, and it limits the scalability of the method. Ye et al. (2022) proposed a DP method with an exponential mechanism to perturb the confidence scores of a model to resist inference attacks. They used two membership inference attack methods for resistance testing, namely, ML-leak (Salem et al. 2019) and NSH attacks (Nasr et al. 2018). When setting a smaller privacy budget, these two attacks result in an attack accuracy of around 50% for the attack model on multiple image datasets. This shows that the developed method is successful for defending against membership inference attacks. More importantly, the authors claimed that the suggested method does not harm the performance of the model, which is an advantage over other DP mechanisms.

### 6.2 DP against model inversion attacks

As mentioned earlier, model inversion attacks, also known as attribute inference attacks, aim to reconstruct the input data of a model. The objective evaluation metric for this attack method is the inversion error, which is typically calculated using the mean squared error (Ye et al. 2022), the structural similarity index measure (Wang et al. 2004) and so on. In addition, visualizing the attack effect is a direct evaluation method. Existing studies have shown that the DP mechanism is a promising solution for defending against model inversion attacks (Ye et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2020b).

As mentioned in Sect. 6.1, Ye et al. (2022) proposed an exponential DP mechanism that perturbs the confidence vector. In their experiments, it was found that apart from resisting membership inference attacks, it was also very effective in terms of countering model inversion attacks. The authors used inversion error as a metric for evaluation, which is measured by calculating the mean squared error between the original input data and the reconstructed data.

Zhang et al. (2020b) proposed a class-level and subclass-level DP method to defend against model inversion attacks. Class-level DP defines neighboring datasets as two datasets that differ in a single class, ensuring that the generated models are undifferentiable even when substituting all the records within that particular class. Subclass-level DP is a concept that establishes neighboring databases by focusing on a specific subclass, which refers to a predefined subset of records within a larger class. This approach is applicable in various real-world scenarios where distinct subclasses exist within a broader category. By maintaining the output model’s indistinguishability across all subclasses, subclass-level DP can be seen as a less stringent form of class-level DP. They used a CNN architecture on two datasets, MNIST and Faces94, to conduct experiments in which they defended against model inference attacks.

Other studies indicate that DP has a restricted capacity to effectively safeguard against model inversion attacks, as it mainly prioritizes privacy guarantees through noise injection and data perturbation (Zhang et al. 2020a; Wang et al. 2021). While it has benefits in safeguarding individual data points, it does not specifically target the protection of attribute privacy. Zhang et al. (2020a) proposed a model inversion attack method based on generative models, using generative adversarial networks to learn distribution priors and employing them to guide the inversion process. They demonstrated that private model (trained using DP-SGD) is nearly ineffective in defending against their attacks. They argue that by restricting the learning of specific training instances, DP may inadvertently promote the acquisition of generic class features, which could promote the execution of model inversion attacks.

Ye et al. (2022) applied the DP exponential mechanism to the output confidence of the model, effectively defending against model inversion attacks. Zhang et al. (2020b) proposed class-level and subclass-level DP (which can be seen as an improvement over DP-SGD), and these also effectively defended against model inversion attacks. However, Zhang et al. (2020a) demonstrated that DP-SGD fails to protect against model inversion attacks. Therefore, the effectiveness of DP in preventing model inversion attacks may be influenced by various factors, including the specific configuration parameters of the DP mechanism itself, the architectural choices and settings of the target deep learning model, and the selection of the attack model.

### 6.3 DP against model extraction attacks

Through model extraction attacks, an adversary can copy the structure and functionality of the target model to establish a similar model. This may lead to intellectual property infringement and unfair competition, causing damage to the interests of model developers. The use of the DP mechanism as a defense strategy has become an effective means for the learning community to defend against model extraction attacks.

Yan et al. (2022a) proposed a monitoring-based DP mechanism to combat query flooding model extraction attacks. They first designed a model extraction attack called query flooding parameter duplication (QPD). Second, they developed a monitoring-based DP scheme to defend against these attacks. Specifically, the authors introduced a real-time model extraction state evaluation scheme called *Monitor*, which assessed the model’s state by introducing monitoring points to the system, enabling the real-time monitoring and evaluation of the query count and query results during the querying process. Then, they designed an adaptive guidance method for DP budget allocation. Finally, all the defense strategies based on a Markov decision process could dynamically adjust the amount of noise added to the model response based on the results output by Monitor to defend against QPD attacks. The authors demonstrated the defense effectiveness of this approach in the literature (Yan et al. 2022a), producing excellent results with contrasting methods.

Zheng et al. (2022) proposed a boundary DP layer (BDPL) for defending against model extraction attacks in machine learning services by obfuscating the predicted responses with DP noise. The BDPL provides a parameterized method that obfuscates responses for queries lying within predefined boundary-sensitive areas. To further protect the decision boundary, the authors used an adaptive perturbation method to perturb the samples differently according to their densities near the decision boundary. ### 6.4 DP against gradient leakage attacks

Gradient leakage attacks are common security threats in federated learning (Yang et al. 2023b). The attacker uses the gradient information transmitted during the update process of the target federated learning model to infer the characteristics and possible labels of the training data. By collecting gradients or model parameters from different parties, an attacker can reconstruct training data or infer sensitive information about each party. Recently, DP has become an effective mechanism for defending against gradient leakage attacks (Gong et al. 2023).

Wei and Liu (2021) proposed a DP method that combines adaptive DP parameters to dynamically control the noise variance throughout the entire training process. To achieve this goal, three adaptive DP parameter optimization schemes were introduced, including dynamic gradient clipping, dynamic sensitivity calculation, and dynamic noise scale adjustment. Through the implementation of these optimization strategies, the early stages of the training process witnessed the injection of increased noise, which was subsequently diminished as the training procedure advanced. This adaptive approach for providing DP in deep learning, as opposed to employing fixed parameter strategies necessitating a constant noise variance, guarantees that the noise injection strategy remains in sync with the gradient updates during the training process. Experimental results demonstrated that this adaptive approach ensures high resilience against gradient leakage attacks while maintaining competitive accuracy and providing better DP guarantees.

Wei et al. (2023) proposed an elastically enhanced federated learning approach with DP, called \( Fed-\alpha CDP \), which extended the traditional per-client method through per-example gradient perturbation and adaptive parameter optimization. Specifically, this study first injected DP noise into each sample gradient during the client’s local training process to protect it under DP. Second, authors reconsidered the use of fixed clipping boundaries to solve the constant Gaussian noise variance problem during multiround training and gradually reduced the noise variance by using the maximum \( l_2 \) value of the gradient to define the sensitivity of the differentially private federated learning algorithm. Finally, a dynamic attenuation noise scale was introduced to align the noise variance with the gradient update trend. The author validated the effectiveness of the proposed method in resisting gradient leakage attacks on datasets such as MNIST and FLW.

## 7 DP in deep learning: more than privacy

The original purpose of designing DP was to protect data privacy. However, when DP is applied to deep learning, it has special impacts on artificial neural networks. These impacts can be both positive, such as mitigating overfitting, and negative, such as exacerbating unfairness.

### 7.1 Fairness

In deep learning, *fairness* refers to the fair and unbiased treatment of different groups of individuals or samples by an algorithm or model. However, achieving fairness in deep learning is a complex and challenging problem because deep learning models are often trained using large amounts of data, and potential biases or inequalities may exist in these data. Interestingly, studies have found that incorporating DP noise into a deep learning model can exacerbate its unfairness (Bagdasaryan et al. 2019; Fioretto et al. 2022; Ganev et al. 2022).

Bagdasaryan et al. (2019) observed the impact of privately training deep learning models with DP-SGD on accuracy parity. They observed that the accuracy achieved for minority groups was disproportionately affected by the private training process, and adding DP noise made the subgroups with poorer performance more susceptible to the adverse effects of DP noise. These findings were validated in several visual and natural language processing tasks in centralized and federated settings. The authors attributed this to the DP-SGD method’s gradient clipping technique, which selectively impacts subgroups with larger gradients during training. This particular approach tends to disproportionately affect under-represented subgroups, as they are more likely to produce larger gradients. Subsequently, Farrand et al. (2020) investigated the effect of data imbalance on the utility and fairness of DP, providing additional perspectives regarding the effects of DP on fairness under slightly imbalanced data conditions and low privacy guarantees.

Although the DP mechanism has negative impacts on fairness in deep learning, recent studies have shown that these negative effects can be mitigated through appropriate interventions. Mangold et al. (2023) demonstrated that the impact of DP on the fairness in classification for strongly convex problems is bounded, indicating that as the number of samples increases, the fairness level of private models approaches that of their non-private counterparts. Tran et al. (2021b) developed a model that protects private information while allowing it to learn nondiscriminatory predictive factors. This method utilizes the concepts of DP and Lagrangian duality to design a neural network that ensures the privacy of sensitive attributes while adhering to fairness constraints. Ding et al. (2020) allotted different parameters to standard attributes and private attributes and designed a calibrated differentially private mechanism to achieve the dual objectives of fair learning and sensitive attribute protection.

Furthermore, several studies have demonstrated that fairness and privacy can be mutually exclusive in certain contexts, meaning there are data distributions in which ensuring one aspect hinders the fulfillment of the other (Chang and Shokri 2021; Sanyal etal. 2022). Chang and Shokri (2021) suggest that enforcing fairness may lead to more privacy breaches for non privileged groups. Their experimental findings indicate that fairness-aware machine learning algorithms experience lower levels of privacy protection. Sanyal etal. (2022) demonstrated that when dealing with data characterized by a long-tailed structure, it is infeasible to create accurate learning algorithms that are both privacy-preserving and yield improved accuracy for minority subpopulations.

### 7.2 Overfitting

Overfitting is an important problem that has been widely studied in deep learning. Overfitting occurs when a well-trained model achieves high accuracy on the training dataset but yields worse accuracy on unseen test data, which results in a large generalization error. Unexpectedly, researchers have found that adding carefully created noise during neural network training can alleviate overfitting, with DP noise being a suitable option (Dwork et al. 2015).

Yeom et al. (2018) conducted a systematic analysis of the relationship between privacy risk sand overfitting, revealing that overfitting increases the risks of model inference and attribute inference attacks. Introducing DP during training serves as a unified solution for mitigating overfitting and protecting privacy. Wu et al. (2019) improved DP-SGD for pathological image classification tasks. By adopting an enhanced DP-SGD algorithm during the training process of the utilized CNN, this method can prevent overfitting and enhance the robustness of the model, thereby improving the accuracy and generalizability of pathological image classification. Lin et al. (2022) introduced a privacy-preserving decentralized network graph learning framework based on GANs, which can tune the noise injected in a decentralized graph to alleviate the overfitting issues encountered when learning noisy graphs. Lu et al. (2022a) investigated the generalization performance under convexified loss functions and theoretically demonstrated using the On-Average-Replace-One Stability Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David (2014) method that DP models exhibit greater stability and are less prone to overfitting compared to non-private models.

### 7.3 Robustness

Robustness refers to a model’s resistance to small perturbations or variations in the input data. In the presence of noise, interference, adversarial samples, etc., a robust deep learning model can maintain good performance and accuracy. As DP originally aimed to conceal variances in neighboring datasets, its implementation can effectively bolster network robustness.

Researchers have found that DP is robust to adversarial attacks. Lecuyer et al. (2019) proposed a certified defense method called PixelDP, which is based on a new connection between robustness against adversarial examples and DP. They first observed the connection between DP and robustness for adversarial examples in machine learning. They utilized this connection to develop PixelDP, a method that can be scaled to large-scale networks and datasets and can be widely adapted to any deep neural network architecture. This method helps improve the robustness of models. Phan et al. (2019) proposed a DP protection mechanism, namely, a heterogeneous Gaussian mechanism, for achieving DP in deep learning. This mechanism utilizes multiple different Gaussian noise injection strategies. Through a theoretical analysis, they demonstrated that using the heterogeneous Gaussian mechanism for DP protection not only provides excellent privacy protection but also maintains high model accuracy and robustness. Phan et al. (2020) proposed a scalable DP algorithm for protecting deep learning, which introduces the concept of authenticated robustness to enhance the robustness of the employed model while preserving data privacy.

## 8 Future research directions

### 8.1 Optimizing the existing methods

Numerous strategies have been proposed for the intersection of DP and deep learning, such as DP-SGD (Abadi et al. 2016). Despite its inherent limitations, DP-SGD has gained recognition as an outstanding work within the learning community. Future research can focus on improving existing frameworks to achieve higher model accuracy under the same level of noise. For instance, Papernot et al. (2021) suggested enhancing utility by selectively designing activation functions specifically for privacy-preserving training processes. In addition, as an emerging research trend, one can consider the generation of DP noise as an optimization problem for mitigating the adverse impact of DP noise on model accuracy (Nasr et al. 2021). Furthermore, combining other privacy protection methods, such as homomorphic encryption and secure multiparty computation, with existing DP-based defenses can lead to the creation of more robust private models.

### 8.2 Exploring new DP solutions

Most existing research has focused on adding noise to gradients to protect privacy. However, DP mechanisms with fixed noise inject the same level of noise into the gradients (parameters) to protect private content, resulting in proportional noise accumulation during the training process. This may affect the tradeoff between the usefulness of the employed model and the level of privacy protection it offers. Therefore, one possible direction is to introduce variants of DP, such as adaptive DP mechanisms (Gong et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2023c). The main idea is to dynamically adjust the amount of noise added during the training process, which reduces the cumulative effect of noise compared to that of fixed noise-level DP mechanisms. For instance, Zhang et al. (2023c) suggested an adaptive DP method based on entropy theory, where the information gain of neurons (abstract features) is evaluated during the training process to decide the level of noise to be injected. Although this adaptive DP mechanism has yielded promising results, the disadvantage is that it requires additional computational overhead.

Although an adaptive DP mechanism enhances the utility of the applied model, it is not necessarily harmless to the model. Therefore, a promising research direction is to protect private information while preserving the utility of the employed model. Ye et al. (2022) provided a good example in which they proposed using a DP index mechanism to perturb the output confidence score vector of a model, preserving the utility of the model. However, this solution may not be deployable in distributed training scenarios, as privacy leakage can occur through gradients and parameters during the collaborative training process.

Another perspective is that in supervised learning, the given dataset is divided into (*data*, *label*) , where the labels are more important. Therefore, the privacy of the labels is protected, leading to the concept of label-DP (Ghazi et al. 2021; Esfandiari et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2025). Label-DP focuses on protecting the sensitive content embedded in the labels of training data. Its goal is to prevent adversaries from inferring individual labels or extracting sensitive patterns from labeled datasets. Traditional DP methods mainly focus on protecting the privacy of individual data points or features, while label DP specifically addresses the privacy concerns related to labels. Notably, label-DP introduces additional challenges and tradeoffs. Incorporating noise or perturbations into the labels may affect the accuracy and practicality of the trained model. The reason is that label noise destroys the correctness of the supervision signal, and the strong fitting ability of deep neural networks causes them to over-learn noise patterns, especially under non-IID data distribution (such as each client only holds a single class label), the noise accumulation effect is more significant.

In short, safeguarding privacy and enhancing the utility of models have been widely discussed topics. Therefore, it is important to encourage the development of new DP mechanisms to balance privacy guarantees and model utility.

### 8.3 Combining DP with other privacy protection technologies

Homomorphic encryption and secure multi-party computation are well-established cryptographic techniques that have found extensive applications in privacy protection within deep learning (Al-Janabi et al. 2023; Xie et al. 2024; Ren et al. 2024). Each privacy protection strategy comes with its own set of advantages and disadvantages.

Homomorphic encryption allows computations to be performed directly on ciphertext, maintaining data confidentiality throughout the computation process, and enabling accurate decryption of results. In contrast, secure multi-party computation facilitates collaborative computations among multiple parties without revealing their private inputs, thereby effectively safeguarding data privacy. However, both techniques have notable drawbacks. The primary disadvantage of homomorphic encryption is that operations on ciphertext are generally much slower than those executed on plaintext data, particularly when employing fully homomorphic encryption. Furthermore, the costs associated with transmitting and storing ciphertext can be significant. On the other hand, secure multi-party computation typically requires multiple rounds of communication between participants, leading to substantial communication overhead, especially as the number of participants increases.

In contrast, DP often incurs lower computational costs and achieves higher data processing efficiency, but it does so at the expense of the utility. DP protection strategies are suited to various scenarios, and combining the advantages of multiple strategies holds promise for developing more satisfactory privacy protection methods (Li et al. 2022a; Sébert et al. 2023; Ogilvie 2024; Mugunthan et al. 2019; Byrd and Polychroniadou 2020).

For example, Sébert et al. (2023) proposed a approach to enhancing training data privacy in federated Learning by combining DP and homomorphic encryption. The authors introduced a stochastic quantization operator that integrates DP guarantees with quantized and bounded noise resulting from homomorphic encryption. The method addresses different confidentiality threats posed by clients and the aggregation server, aiming for a more realistic deployment of these privacy techniques. Byrd and Polychroniadou (2020) presented a privacy-preserving federated learning protocol that combines DP and secure multi-party computation to enable collaborative learning among financial institutions without compromising sensitive client data. The proposed method introduces noise to the model parameters to protect client information while minimizing accuracy loss. By also incorporating secure multi-party computation, the system further enhances data security, making it challenging for adversaries to recover private data from the exchanged parameters.

In addition, anonymization is another well-known practical solution to address privacy issues. In the field of deep learning, anonymization is usually applied in two scenarios (Olatunji et al. 2024; Montenegro and Cardoso 2024; Nankya et al. 2024). Data preprocessing stage: Anonymize the training data, such as removing direct identifiers and generalizing sensitive attributes to prevent samples from being traced back to specific individuals. Model output stage: Anonymize the prediction results, such as blurring user identifiers to prevent inferring user privacy through output. For example, Ratra et al. (2022) proposed an enhanced anonymization framework aimed at protecting patient data privacy, leveraging K-anonymity alongside DP mechanisms to provide robust privacy guarantees for the dataset.

These efforts illustrate that the integration of DP with secure multi-party computation, homomorphic encryption and anonymization can significantly enhance privacy protection techniques for deep learning models. By leveraging the strengths of these three methodologies, researchers can formulate effective strategies to safeguard sensitive data while allowing for valuable insights through machine learning. Overall, the fusion of DP, secure multi-party computation, homomorphic encryption and anonymization opens up new avenues for developing innovative and effective privacy-preserving solutions in the realm of deep learning.

### 8.4 Challenges

#### 8.4.1 The trade-off between privacy and utility

The application of differential privacy in deep learning models has both advantages and disadvantages. On one hand, introducing DP noise during training data processing or model training can protect data privacy. On the other hand, noise introduction impairs model performance. Specifically, higher privacy protection requirements typically demand a smaller privacy budget, leading to more noise injection, which in turn more severely degrades model performance. Conversely, a larger privacy budget can achieve desired model utility, but it is difficult to provide effective privacy protection. Therefore, applying differential privacy to deep learning involves a trade-off between model utility and privacy protection. Facing the rich private information in training data and the diverse scenarios in deep learning, establishing appropriate privacy budget allocation rules for different scenarios and balancing privacy with utility are urgent tasks. It is noteworthy that personalized privacy measurement and on-demand privacy protection are effective approaches to alleviate this issue (Gong et al. 2020; Chen et al. 2023; Pan et al. 2024). Based on adaptive privacy budget allocation or dynamic privacy need awareness, we can maximize data and model utility while reducing the accumulation of privacy loss.

#### 8.4.2 DP in large models

Large-scale foundation models (such as GPT-4, PaLM-2) with hundreds of billions of parameters and training data scales exceeding one trillion tokens pose special challenges to DP mechanisms due to their characteristics like dynamic sparse activation and long-sequence modeling:

-
1.
Exponential consumption of privacy budget: Massive iterations (over 100,000 rounds) in the training phase lead to the rapid exhaustion of the sequential composition budget in traditional DP.

-
2.
High-dimensional gradients and noise sensitivity: The multi-head attention gradients in Transformer architectures exhibit high correlation. Injecting noise causes attention weight bias, degrading text coherence in long-sequence generation tasks.

-
3.
Special contradiction in label privacy: Foundation models often adopt self-supervised + supervised hybrid training. Label privacy protection (e.g., for medical and financial data labels) requires balancing the integrity of self-supervised signals and the accuracy of supervised signals simultaneously.


#### 8.4.3 DP in complex data

-
1.
Time series data: timing dependency and dynamic distribution challenges

-
Destruction of temporal correlation: Independent noise injection of DP-SGD will destroy the temporal dependency of time series (such as trend and periodicity). For example, in stock price prediction, adding noise leads to a decrease in autocorrelation coefficient.

-
Dynamic distribution drift: The fixed noise parameters of traditional DP-SGD cannot adapt to the time-varying characteristics of data distribution. When the distribution changes suddenly, it may lead to waste of privacy budget or a sudden increase in leakage risk.


-
-
2.
Graph data: structural dependence and non-uniform distribution challenges

-
Graph structure information loss: Gradient clipping in DP-SGD destroys the neighborhood aggregation relations of graph neural networks (GNNs).

-
Degree distribution imbalance issue: In graphs with power-law distributions, the gradient sensitivity of high-degree nodes (such as social media influencers) is several times higher than that of low-degree nodes. Uniform noise scaling increases the privacy leakage risk for high-degree nodes.


-
-
3.
Multimodal data: Heterogeneous features and cross-modal dependency challenges

-
Inter-modal sensitivity discrepancy: A uniform privacy budget may lead to insufficient privacy protection for one modality while overprotecting others. For example, the sensitivity mismatch between high-dimensional image pixel features and low-dimensional text word vectors under a uniform privacy budget can result in inadequate image privacy protection and excessive text protection.

-
Cross-modal correlation disruption: In multimodal data analysis, DP noise amplifies the bias in cross-modal attention weights.


-

Thus, to implement DP in deep learning models with complex training datasets, adapting the standard differential privacy framework to accommodate complex data structures and refining existing perturbation techniques alongside privacy measurement methodologies represent worthwhile research directions.

## 9 Conclusion

Recently, with the speedy evolution of deep learning technology and the expansion of its application fields, the privacy risks faced by deep learning have attracted people’s attention. In this paper, we comprehensively survey the applications of DP techniques for protecting deep learning model, with the result that DP has now been deployed in various fields related to deep learning. As a threat mitigation strategy, DP quantifies the amount of required noise and affects model aspects such as robustness and overfitting in deep learning, which require further attention. Furthermore, we review DP mechanisms for defending against attacks and encourage researchers to demonstrate the effectiveness of their proposed privacy schemes in terms of countering threats by presenting the associated attack effects in the literature, providing readers with an intuitive understanding of the efficacy of the proposed methods. Finally, we suggest some possible future research directions regarding the use of DP in deep learning.
