Emily Myers in Bricks & Bucks

Condos will need to hold deeper reserves and allow more scrutiny to stay mortgage-eligible under new Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac guidelines — though an easing on insurance requirements offers some relief. The updated rules require condo boards to increase capital reserves from a minimum of 10% to a minimum of 15% of their building’s annual budget. “So on top of inflationary costs you are now going to have, as of January 2027, an additional 5% to be added to operating costs,” says Orest Tomaselli, president and CEO of reserve study preparation firm Strategic Inspections.

Lender requirements for buildings have increased substantially since the tragic collapse of the Surfside condo in Florida, which heightened concerns about aging infrastructure and deferred maintenance. “Is it well intentioned? Absolutely,” says David Fitzhenry, partner at Moritt Hock & Hamroff. “But for boards, it’s going to be another sticker shock.” Without holding the 15% minimum in capital reserves, boards will increasingly find loans aren’t approved for their building.

Another change to the lending guidelines is a rollback of the limited review process for purchases. This is effective August 3, 2026 and ends the practice of carrying out a more simplified underwriting process for sales in established low-risk condos. It’s estimated at least 40% of loans in the U.S. take advantage of the limited review process. “Many condominium communities skirted through without reserving at all for future repairs,” Tomaselli says. Now, lenders will rely more heavily on the full review process, involving more scrutiny and more paperwork. “For properties that had not been compliant with the rules on reserves or critical repairs or special assessments there is nowhere to hide,” Tomaselli says.

A condo can still submit a reserve study completed within the past 36 months as an alternative to holding larger capital reserves. Reserve studies cost between $7,500 and $9,500 and evaluate a building’s systems, from the boiler to the roof, with an estimate of future repair costs. For buildings without deferred maintenance, a reserve study may allow the board to hold less than 15% of the budget for a period of time. “Eventually a building will run out of that reserve study benefit,” Fitzhenry says. A study can also have the opposite effect. “A reserve study could require a contribution far higher than 15% — it really is a community by community determination,” Tomaselli says.

Although the rules apply to Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac loans, there are impacts for jumbo and commercial loans. If a building is flagged as ineligible for government-backed financing, Tomaselli says other lenders often follow suit. “That designation is an almost automatic reason not to lend in any capacity in the community,” he says. As a result, the impact of the changes will be felt equally from affordable units to luxury condos.

There’s one further change that addresses the increasingly tough insurance market and offers boards some relief. Buildings still need solid insurance, but roofs no longer need to be insured at full replacement cost — coverage that was expensive and often difficult to get. Now the roof can be insured for what it is worth today instead of the brand new replacement value. “The relief from the insurance guidelines is spectacular,” Tomaselli says. For buildings denied residential mortgage financing because insurance policies could not be aligned with the previous guidelines, access to government-backed mortgages will likely be available.
