# Defensibility in the Application Layer of Generative Artificial Intelligence

## Learnings on generative AI

As generative artificial intelligence continues to revolutionize industries and reshape businesses, it’s essential to understand the fundamental differences between AI companies and regular software businesses. In this second post of my series on generative AI, I’ll dive into the differences between AI companies in the application layer and regular software businesses and how AI companies might still be able to create defensible competitive moats.

## Differences between AI companies and regular software businesses

When trying to understand how “AI” companies can become defensible, it makes sense first to uncover the fundamental economic and operational differences between AI companies and regular software companies.

**Lower gross margins**due to heavy cloud infrastructure usage and ongoing human support. Developing and deploying AI models entail significant costs, including the high expenses associated with training and retraining due to evolving data patterns. With its complex mathematical computations, model inference presents a computational challenge distinct from traditional software. Moreover, the unique demands of rich media data, such as images, audio, or video, pose additional resource-intensive and intricate processing requirements, making the development and operation of AI applications multifaceted.**Scaling challenges**due to the thorny problem of edge cases. The lack of user intuition and the assumption of human-like capabilities in AI products often result in numerous edge cases, with a significant portion of intended functionality residing in the long tail of user intent. In addition, the wide range of possible inputs and diverse customer deployments generate unique data, requiring ongoing efforts to handle data diversity and user behavior in AI product development.**Weaker defensive moats**due to the commoditization of AI models and challenges with data network effects. More on this below.

## Factors of Differentiation

Differentiation seems to be more challenging to achieve for AI companies than for regular software businesses. Andreessen Horowitz argue that there are no clear systematic moats in generative AI:


As a first-order approximation, applications lack strong product differentiation because they use similar models; models face unclear long-term differentiation because they are trained on similar datasets with similar architectures; cloud providers lack deep technical differentiation because they run the same GPUs; and even the hardware companies manufacture their chips at the same fabs.

Nonetheless, there are some common factors of defensibility, which seem hard to retain in the long term:

**Scale moats**(“I have or can raise more money than you!”)**Supply-chain moats**(“I have the GPUs, you don’t!”)**Ecosystem moats**(“Everyone uses my software already!”)**Algorithmic moats**(“We’re more clever than you!”)**Distribution moats**(“I already have a sales team and more customers than you!”) and data pipeline moats (“I’ve crawled more of the internet than you!”)

While there might not be any evident and long-term sustainable factors of defensibility, Base10 identify several promising signals of moats and defensibility:

Firstly, exceeding customer expectations through unparalleled levels of **customization**, creating an exceptional experience tailored to each individual’s unique preferences.

Secondly, taking ownership of a targeted **use case from end-to-end**, and integrating proprietary vertical data at various layers can unlock substantial value. Companies finding ways to meet critical vertical pain points compellingly and nailing the workflows that keep customers in their tool the entire day will undoubtedly have a competitive advantage.

Thirdly, leveraging extensive data sets of **proprietary Data**, including increased user base and feedback, to fine-tune models can yield profound downstream effects, optimizing performance and driving superior outcomes.

Additionally, cultivating a robust **brand identity** that resonates with discerning consumers and prosumers, forging solid associations, and establishing a durable competitive advantage.

Lastly, prioritizing comprehensive **user education** and delivering an unparalleled, cutting-edge **UI/UXI design**, fostering customer loyalty through a distinctive and seamless user experience.

## Foundational Models vs. Fine-tuned Models

The defensibility of an AI company hinges significantly on the choice of model employed. Leveraging open-source models, such as those available through Huggingface, and fine-tuning them can yield a competitive moat unless proprietary models like GPT-4 rapidly outperform the fine-tuned model, leapfrogging its performance in short order. Evan Armstrong created an interesting visualization, arguing that foundational models improve leap-wise, whereas fine-tuned models gradually improve over time.

Remarkable parallels can be drawn between contemporary generative AI companies and the traditional software companies that navigated the cloud transition. By circumventing the need for substantial upfront financial investment, both types of companies can create value more efficiently. Just as software companies reduced costs by embracing cloud providers rather than costly on-premise solutions, AI companies can mitigate upfront expenses through access to private APIs and open-source models, effectively eliminating financial barriers to entry. Consequently, this expedites development speed, akin to the expeditious deployment facilitated by industry leaders such as OpenAI. Yet, there is a significant difference between cloud and generative AI businesses:


Unlike cloud, most generative AI businesses are building back-ends that can plug in and out of various models — so the high switching costs that drove value accrual in cloud aren’t as pronounced in generative AI. Platform players will not hold the leverage over customers that they do for cloud, which means stickiness and defensibility is still a question.-Base 10 VC

## AI is not the product

One important thing to remember in the context of defensibility in the application layer is that artificial intelligence is not the product itself. “It is an enabling technology that allows new products to be built,” as the AI Grant program, funded by Nat Friedman (ex-CEO of Github) and Daniel Gross (who formerly ran ML projects at Apple, started YC’s AI program) state. Alexander Kremer, Partner at Picus Capital, adds, “AI-enabled products may initially attract users out of curiosity, [but] they will only continue to use them if they provide a seamless integration of the technology alongside other features.” In a B2C and B2B environment, users will not decide for or against a product based on the type of technology working in the background.


The question of which “AI” startup wins their endpoint is more a matter of sales and marketing and vibe than model performance. For those startups that are competing on the basis of SaaS, the competitive advantage goes toward companies that already have inherent distribution or product capabilities. —Evan Armstrong

In this case, larger companies possess a distinct advantage in integrating AI into their established product offerings compared to startups attempting to build competitive full-suite products from the ground up. Industry giants like Microsoft, Canva, and Notion have already demonstrated the ability to replicate key capabilities of these startups within their existing product suites, showcasing their superior resources and capabilities in leveraging AI technology.

In conclusion, while AI companies face distinct challenges compared to traditional software businesses, they can still achieve defensibility through various strategies. AI companies can establish competitive advantages and create long-term value by exceeding customer expectations, owning end-to-end use cases, leveraging proprietary data, building a strong brand identity, and delivering superior user experience. As the field of AI continues to evolve, understanding these differences and factors of differentiation can help AI companies navigate the landscape and build successful businesses in this rapidly changing industry.
