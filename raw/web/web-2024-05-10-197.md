# GenAI When to Build vs Buy

With the rise of #GenAI there has been a proliferation of offerings on the market. One of the questions data practitioners have been faced with is when to build your own solution vs when to buy one embedded in a tool. To be clear when I say build, I do not mean to actually build your own LLM, which would be significant time and millions of dollars in capital to train. The notion really boils down to, can the vendor/solution serve the need and use case, is it secure and does it have the context window (data) to be effective.

First and foremost is, do you have the talent to build out GenAI solutions in house? Many organizations or data teams may think the answer to that question is no. It may seem daunting to get started without previous experience. There are solutions out there to lower overhead and make the coding of LLMs into pipelines and applications much easier such as #AWS #Bedrock and #Snowflake #Cortex and may be more accessible than you think. Bedrock can give a uniform interface to interact with many LLMs using the same API call, extending the reach. Snowflake cortex can interact with LLMs through built in functions via SQL. LLM development can be simple to get going and provide a lot of complexity and depth as you dig further into prompt engineering, RAG and fine-tuning methodologies.

Let’s assume you have the ability to develop in house and are then contemplating the question to build vs buy, what sort of things should you consider. One of the most important is data security and privacy. You should be aware of how the solution consumes your data and what happens to it. Does the GenAI tool keep it private and separated, is it used to further train the model? This can lead to concerns about data privacy and sensitivity as it could potentially be exposed or leaked. You should always ask vendors the scope and use of supplied data when considering purchasing a tool.

Another consideration is the context the LLM has access to, or what data it consumes. This is important as the ability to perform specific use cases based on knowledge of your business is limited to what it knows. For example, in a case of figuring out customer sentiment you may need to consume many different unstructured data sources from your business applications or data warehouse. If the LLM is part of an embedded tool, does it have access to those sources to paint the full picture or will it only be a partial perspective? It can get much worse if you have multiple solutions all trying to answer the same question with different context windows as they may come up with different answers and create confusion or promote answer shopping. In this case, building a centralized solution with access to all of the data can provide a uniform and fully realized context window. This can then be fed back to the different endpoints or embedded in tools for access.

You should also consider the feature direction for future functionality. Does it fit with your roadmap and where you want to drive outcomes towards? If you are trying to cut down on time spent on meeting preparation to increase the number of engagements by your sales team, does the solution have generating follow up emails as an existing feature or upcoming on the roadmap? With vendor solutions you are locked into their vision and roadmap where internal development can provide the flexibility to prioritize and pivot to where you think the greatest impact lies. Vendors may also be using a single multi-purpose LLM when other LLMs may be more suited to specific tasks and give better performance as LLMs further specialize at specific functions.

There is also the factor of cost. It follows the classic patterns in any software development scenario. On the vendor side how is the GenAI capability priced, is it included in the existing price of the product, is it an add on? If it is an add on how is it priced, per user, consumption based? Ask the vendor how you can estimate costs if it follows a consumption model in order to properly budget for the spend. In the build space the costs can be broken down into two categories at a high level, build and maintenance costs on one side and compute/consumption resources on the other. Build and maintenance can be broken down into the time and effort involved to code the solutions and maintain them. On the compute side many solutions offer pricing calculators that can help you estimate what those costs are as you scale up over time and offer predictability as you budget for ongoing spend. As with any project I recommend that the costs are viewed through the lens as a full business case in tandem with the outcomes and are part of an ROI calculation. For example if you spend X amount on dollars of build or buy what do you expect the Y dollar return to be on increased ARR, or cost savings. This prevents cost from becoming a theoretical exercise and a blocker to progress.

What are you doing in the LLM space, building, buying? What else did I miss that you consider when debating this question?
