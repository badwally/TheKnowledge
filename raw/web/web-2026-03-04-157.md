## ABSTRACT

Rail surfaces deteriorate and develop structural defects (e.g. cracks, squats) over time. If left unattended, these defects can result in everything from poor ride to a safety risk. Thus, rail operators devote a significant portion of their maintenance budget to inspecting, grinding, and replacing rail to manage their surface conditions. Meanwhile, the development of tools to assist in predicting future costs and optimizing maintenance at the network level is urgently needed. However, for many operators, inspection data is sparse relative to the size of the network and techniques for defect prediction must properly describe the uncertainty associated with this limitation. This study aims to develop a model to predict surface defect arrivals over different portions of the network. A Homogeneous Poisson Process (HPP) is identified using a Hierarchical Bayesian Modelling (HBM) approach to permit partial pooling of the unevenly distributed inspection data to improve parameter estimates. The posteriors of the model parameters are estimated using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) techniques and metrics are developed to compare the posteriors from different areas of the network. In order to provide inspection decision support in the absence of cost data, the HPP models are subsequently used to examine the trade-off between the oldest unobserved defect (a proxy for risk) and the inspection frequency. The proposed framework provides new insight into inspection planning for rail asset management and decision-making.

## 1. Introduction

A developed railway transportation system is a significant tool for promoting economic and social sustainability. In addition, railways are playing a vital role in transiting people in different countries, which resulted in becoming the primary transportation mode [Citation1]. Railway infrastructure should be monitored regularly to keep the asset in the safe operating zone. A considerable annual budget is allocated to railway infrastructure inspection and maintenance due to its high safety risk [Citation2].

The loads applied to the rail profile drive degradation of the rail surface (among other aspects), leading to Rolling Contact Fatigue (RCF), squats, and corrugation [Citation3]. Surface-initiated defects mainly appear due to traffic density and axle load increment while subsurface-initiated defects result from metallurgical faults [Citation4,Citation5]. Unattended defects may result in a rail break and subsequent profound safety and operational consequences. Rail operators therefore spend a significant portion of their maintenance and inspection effort on the rail surface to prevent such a scenario. Thus, regular inspections are typically conducted to identify rail surface defects. Upon their identification, two main maintenance options are available: surface grinding [Citation6] and replacement [Citation7]. While grinding may be conducted to mitigate some types of defects (e.g. corrugation) [Citation3,Citation8], it is ineffective in mitigating others (e.g. severe squats) and replacement of the affected rail must be conducted [Citation9].

Predicting the appearance and evolution of surface defects would be highly beneficial to maintenance and inspection planning. To this end, several studies have been performed to establish physics-based (mechanistic) models to investigate the crack propagation on rail surfaces that led to rail breaks. The mechanistic models take the fundamental properties of rail into account. El-Sayed et al. [Citation10] developed a Finite Element Method (FEM) to study the initiation of fatigue cracks in railheads using details of the contact geometry between wheels and rails, nonlinear behaviour of materials, and dynamic responses. They concluded that the crack orientations and the locations observed in the field are in good agreement with the model’s results. Masoudi Nejad [Citation11] constructed a 3D nonlinear model to evaluate the stress field on the rail surface regarding periodic overloads. He found a considerable effect of periodic overload on the fatigue life of the rails. Mai et al. [Citation12] developed a 3D numerical simulation to investigate the growth of fatigue cracks in rails resulting from multiaxial fatigue stresses. They analysed the effect of the friction coefficient between the crack faces, the position of the crack along the sleepers, and the stiffness of the track on crack growth. They found that if the friction coefficient between crack faces decreases, the cracks propagate faster.

In contrast to the mechanistic approaches, some studies employed statistical methods to alleviate the need for detailed mechanical modelling, while additional data is required compared to the mechanistic method. To this end, Jamshidi et al. [Citation13] developed a probabilistic method to evaluate the risk of squat growth. Ghofrani et al. [Citation14] employed a Bayesian framework to investigate the impact of different factors on predicting the risk of rail defect recurrence using a proportional hazard model. They found that the recurrence of the defects is correlated to the type of rail defect, the location of the defect, the train speed limit, and the number of defects. Jamshidi et al. [Citation15] analysed the rail failure risk caused by the squat as the main surface defect. The squats that appeared on the rail surface were detected automatically among the significant number of records from video cameras through an image processing approach. In addition, three different scenarios were constructed to evaluate the impact of rail traffic loads on the crack growth. The results show the efficiency and practicality of the developed method on the Dutch railway. Cronin et al. [Citation16] employed a 2-parameter Weibull distribution to evaluate the rail fatigue defect rate and consequently predict the fatigue life of the railroad. To overcome the traditional drawbacks associated with the Weibull distribution, the Bootstrapping approach has been employed. The approach led to obtaining more accurate results in terms of determining the probability of developing future rail defects.

Inspections are an important tool in mitigating the impacts of rail surface defects, and they are essential data in developing the above statistical approaches. A number of methods have been proposed for carrying out rail surface inspections, including visual inspection, ultrasonic inspection [Citation17], and Eddy current inspection [Citation18]. Ultrasonic inspection and the Eddy current inspection are categorized as Non-Destructive Testing (NDT) methods. These tests use high-frequency sound waves to investigate the internal condition of the rail track. As a result, any internal irregularity within the rail track is detected prior to the defect arrival on the rail surface. Also, through employing NDT methods the length of the inspections is extended significantly compared to the visual inspections [Citation19]. A NDT method alleviates the subjectivity in detecting a rail defect. This will improve the quality of the developed model and the prediction of the rail performance [Citation20]. However, these techniques are relatively new (compared to the life of the asset) and come with considerable costs. Thus, visual inspections – despite their subjectivity – have been and remain important in the maintenance of rail surfaces and provide an opportunity to exploit historical data. This study therefore focuses on the utilization of visual inspection data and quantifying their uncertainty with appropriate tools.

Regarding visual inspections, different regular visual inspection intervals, such as twice a year [Citation18] and daily inspections [Citation21] have been suggested in the literature. It should be noted that the network track length, the number of stations, and the population that uses the railway system play a vital role in planning the visual inspections. The main challenge of scheduling regular visual inspections is the considerable human resources and the budget that should be allocated; however, it is the principal rail surface inspecting technique. To overcome this issue, some studies employed automatic rail surface defect detection. Molodova et al. [Citation22] proposed an automatic approach for detecting squats establishing axle box acceleration measurement on trains. The results demonstrated the accuracy and applicability of the proposed method over the Groningen-Assen track in the Netherlands. In another study, Bai et al. [Citation23] developed a machine vision-based method to detect surface defects automatically. The results show a significant decrease in the amount of the parameter learning that results in faster surface defects detection in comparison with other methods. However, most of the machine vision-based approaches that are mainly constructed according to the deep learning concept require large databases to support the approach which in practice is challenging to provide.

Despite all efforts in establishing a model for predicting surface defect occurrence, there is still a need to develop a model to cope with the uncertainty associated with available datasets when long-term predictions are desired. Mechanistic models do not address uncertainty and instead presume that the complex mechanical and chemical processes (e.g. the wheel–rail interaction) are known. On the other hand, statistical models require significant data and may be quite sparse relative to the size of the rail network and the diversity of the operating conditions (speeds, climate, etc.). To overcome this challenge, this study aims to develop a new approach to predict the probability distribution of defect arrivals across the network by exploiting Hierarchical Bayesian techniques to balance model location specificity with parameter estimation variance. The main utility of the model is in informing inspection and maintenance planning – e.g. setting inspection/grinding intervals according to the arrival process appropriately for different tracks. Therefore, an inspection decision support model is developed based on the fitted models to assess the probability distribution of the oldest defect, which is considered to be a proxy for defect severity since a detailed propagation model is not available. To demonstrate the application of the developed methodology, the visual inspection data for surface defects regarding a combination of suburban and regional tracks in Australia with different traffic patterns and track length have been utilized. The outcome of the study will be beneficial for railway inspection and maintenance teams and specifically for the Australian railway system in terms of providing a long-term prediction for the surface defect occurrence and an optimal visual inspection and maintenance time interval.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: section 2 provides a description on the available data sources. section 3 presents the methodology and tools to cover the research aims. The obtained results and a discussion of the applicability of the developed methodology are presented in section 4 while the conclusion is drawn in section 5.

The obtained master dataset contains the information relevant to the defect type, location, and inspection date. ### 3.2. Parameter fitting via hierarchical Bayesian approach

Data is generally collected through processes where uncertainties arise from imprecision in measurement, censoring, and interpretation errors [Citation27,Citation28]. There are two types of uncertainty associated with the acquired raw data, including aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty [Citation29]. While the aleatoric uncertainty represents the randomness in a natural or artificial process, the epistemic uncertainty concerns the state of knowledge [Citation30]. In rail surface defect context and with respect to the current dataset, the subjectivity in identifying an irregularity as a surface defect through the visual inspection process and the recorded MGT are the uncertainty sources. Once the structure of the model is set (i.e. priors and likelihood), one can make use of well-established MCMC sampling algorithms, including Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) [Citation39], Metropolis-Hastings [Citation40], and Gibbs sampling [Citation41], many of which have software implementations (STAN [Citation42], PyMC [Citation43], WinBUGS [Citation44]). In this study, the posterior samples were drawn using No U-Turn Sampler (NUTS) which is an HMC algorithm [Citation45].

The Half-Normal distribution is characterized by one parameter (known as the scale parameter), which makes it possible to set the corresponding values by exploiting expert knowledge. As recommended in the literature and in order to make sure that the hyperparameters are weakly informative, a Uniform distribution with a wide range is adopted [Citation46]. These considerations in adopting the prior and hyperprior distributions lead to parameter convergence at an acceptable speed rate. ### 4.5. Annual inspection frequency determination

The current analysis, based on evaluating the oldest defect age, provides a novel perspective for inspection decision support, while the time evolution of defect severity data is not available. Incorporating some assumptions and expert knowledge on the oldest defect age that has been estimated previously results in obtaining an improved number of annual inspections. In addition to the oldest defect age, the posterior distribution of the defect arrival for each track is decisive in determining the most appropriate inspection frequency. When the number of inspections increases, the age of the oldest defect decreases considerably. However, frequent inspections require more human resources and a considerable budget.

For instance, assume that the maximum age of the oldest surface defect on every track should not exceed 5 weeks to ensure the reliability of tracks which leads to a smooth ride and guaranteeing passengers’ safety. As can be seen from , track 5 should be inspected every 6 months, while Track 13 needs to be inspected every 2 months to ensure rail safety. Furthermore, Tracks 1, 11, and 16 are expected to be inspected seasonally; however, the distribution of the expected ages is quite different among the aforementioned tracks. Traditional inspection planning in the rail industry shows a fixed inspection plan for all tracks; the current analysis shows that inspecting tracks based on the defect arrivals can yield significantly different inspection intervals (and possible savings in inspection effort).

## 5. Conclusion

The current study presents a methodology to predict a long-term evolution of defect arrivals on rail surfaces and a novel method for inspection planning decision support. Due to the nature of the data and regarding some limitations arising from an industrial operation, an HPP was utilized to predict the defect occurrence rate on tracks. Through the constructed model, the impact of MGT and track length on the defect arrival rate was investigated. The first five tracks were modelled employing the Bayes paradigm. At the same time, the HBM was established to deal with the issue of data scarcity at a higher level of parameter estimation.

Although the developed model was examined through a combination of suburban and regional tracks in Australia with different traffic patterns, the approach is sufficiently general to be applied to other rail networks where defect appearance times are available. The results show reasonable posterior predictive for the tracks with sufficient data, while the uncertainty associated with defect occurrence rates was quantified for all tracks through HBM. In addition, a remarkable feature of the current methodology is providing an initial prediction for a newly installed track, which is without any recorded data. Further inspection and maintenance actions for the recently installed track will be similar to the one with an approximately similar defect arrival rate.

A novel extension to the proposed methodology was obtaining the full distribution of the age of the oldest defect formed between two consecutive visual inspections. The more frequent inspections, the less defect age is expected, while more resources should be devoted. Hence, the inspections were planned by making a trade-off between the oldest defect age and the number of inspections in 1 year.

Future studies will be devoted to, firstly, investigating the impact of covariates, including different climatic conditions and traffic densities on the model’s prediction performance. Secondly, once data regarding the time evolution of defect severity is available (e.g. via new non-destructive testing techniques), defect severity models can be developed based on a synthesis of statistical and physical models to replace the defect age proxy used in this study.
