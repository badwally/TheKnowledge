# What is Full Funding and Baseline Funding?

Yes and no. It’s all a matter of margin. Remember that the timing and cost of all Reserve projects are estimates. This means that budgeting down to the penny may not be prudent. It may be wise to have some additional margin for expenses that occur earlier, or larger than planned. So we describe the different amount of “margin” built into various Reserve Funding Plans by expressing them as having different Funding Goals.

A conservative or aggressive Funding Goal measurably affects the association’s special assessment risk exposure

That brings us to the Funding Goal called “Full Funding”. Full Funding is the goal to maintain the Reserve Fund equal to the amount of common area deterioration. This is much like an accounting balance - as the common areas deteriorate, the Reserve Fund increases in direct proportion.

The most common Reserve Funding Goals are Baseline Funding and Full Funding, because they are easily calculated and not arbitrary. Associations pursuing a Baseline Funding Goal commonly experience cash flow problems that cause project delays, compromises, or special assessments. Associations pursuing a Full Funding Goal commonly sail smoothly through their Reserve projects, even when (especially when!) they don’t occur according to plan.

While any Funding Goal is typically selected or recommended by your Reserve Study professional, Board members should understand the implications of this choice

Associations pursuing a Full Funding Goal average Reserve transfers 10-15% higher than if they were pursuing a Baseline Funding Goal. With Full Funding, more money “sits” in the Reserve Fund providing the homeowners some “margin”, and it comes at the cost of higher ongoing Reserve transfers. But it is not a net higher cost to homeowners. This is because homeowners in many “Baseline Funding” associations end up paying the same higher amount through periodic special assessments. As we often say, the roof doesn’t care.

One caveat - ongoing Full Funding transfers calculated using the Component (straight line) method are commonly two or three times higher than either Baseline or Full Funding transfers calculated using the Cash Flow (pooled) method. Mixing funding methodologies skews any direct comparison between the cost to homeowners of Baseline and Full Funding, allowing some to claim that “Full Funding” is prohibitively expensive. That is not true. Component method funding requirements are higher because it handles funds inefficiently in separate “accounts”, and pursues Full Funding at a very accelerated pace. So if there is ever a huge difference in the “cost” to pursue a more conservative Funding Goal, that can be blamed on an inefficient Funding methodology, not the Funding Goal itself.

Boards have the responsibility to provide sufficient cash for upcoming Reserve projects. Because uncertainties are part of life, Funding Goals, such as Baseline Funding and Full Funding, allow you to articulate how much margin you’ll have to handle those uncertainties in your Reserve Funding Plan.
