# Ontology-Constrained Neural Reasoning in Enterprise Agentic Systems:

A Neurosymbolic Architecture for Domain-Grounded AI Agents

###### Abstract

Enterprise adoption of Large Language Models (LLMs) is constrained by
hallucination, domain drift, and the inability to enforce regulatory
compliance at the reasoning level. We present a neurosymbolic
architecture implemented within the Foundation AgenticOS (FAOS)
platform that addresses these limitations through *ontology-constrained
neural reasoning*. Our approach introduces a three-layer ontological
framework—Role, Domain, and Interaction ontologies—that provides
formal semantic grounding for LLM-based enterprise agents. We formalize
the concept of *asymmetric neurosymbolic coupling* (where ontologies
constrain inputs but not outputs)—the observation
that current enterprise systems constrain agent inputs but not
outputs—wherein symbolic ontological knowledge constrains agent inputs
(context assembly, tool discovery, governance thresholds) while proposing
mechanisms for extending this coupling to constrain agent outputs
(response validation, reasoning verification, compliance checking). We evaluate the architecture through
a controlled experiment (1,800 runs across five industries and three
LLM architectures: Claude Sonnet 4, Qwen 2.5 72B, and Gemma 4 26B),
finding that ontology-coupled agents significantly outperform ungrounded
agents on Metric Accuracy () and Role Consistency ()
across all three models, with large effect sizes (–).
Improvements are greatest where LLM parametric knowledge is weakest—particularly
in Vietnam-localized domains, where the ontology lift is that
of English domains. This effect replicates across all three model
architectures, establishing model-independence. Our contributions include:
(1) a formal three-layer enterprise ontology model, (2) a taxonomy
of neurosymbolic coupling patterns, (3) ontology-constrained tool
discovery via SQL-pushdown scoring, (4) a proposed framework for
output-side ontological validation, (5) empirical evidence for the
*inverse parametric knowledge* effect—that ontological grounding
value is inversely proportional to LLM training data coverage of the
domain, (6) cross-model replication establishing model-independence of
the grounding effects, and (7) a production system serving 22 industry
verticals with 650+ agents.

Keywords: Neurosymbolic AI, Enterprise Ontology, Large Language Models, Agentic AI, Domain-Driven Design, Knowledge-Grounded Reasoning, Multi-Agent Systems

## 1 Introduction

The rapid proliferation of Large Language Models (LLMs) in enterprise settings has exposed a fundamental tension: LLMs excel at natural language understanding and generation but lack the formal semantic grounding necessary for reliable operation in regulated industries [Ji et al., 2023, Huang et al., 2023]. An insurance underwriting agent that hallucinates policy terms, a financial advisor that confuses Basel III capital ratios, or a healthcare agent that misapplies HIPAA guidelines poses not merely a quality problem but a compliance and liability risk.

This tension is not new in artificial intelligence. The “neuro-symbolic
debate” has persisted for decades, with neural approaches offering
flexibility and generalization while symbolic approaches provide
verifiability and formal guarantees [Garcez and Lamb, 2023, Hitzler and Sarker, 2022]. What is new is the *enterprise context*: modern
AI agents must operate within complex organizational structures, adhere
to industry-specific regulations, and produce outputs that can withstand
audit scrutiny.

We contend that the resolution lies not in choosing between neural and
symbolic paradigms but in their *principled integration*—a
neurosymbolic architecture where formal ontologies constrain, guide, and
verify neural reasoning. This paper presents such an architecture,
implemented within the Foundation AgenticOS (FAOS) platform, and
proposes extensions that would advance the state of neurosymbolic
enterprise AI.

### 1.1 The Enterprise Grounding Problem

Enterprise AI agents face a unique variant of the grounding problem [Harnad, 1990]. Unlike general-purpose assistants, enterprise agents must:

-
(i)
Speak the domain language: Use industry-specific terminology correctly (e.g., “combined ratio” in insurance, “net revenue retention” in SaaS, “average length of stay” in healthcare).

-
(ii)
Reason within regulatory bounds: Ensure outputs comply with applicable regulations (SOX, Basel III/IV, HIPAA, GDPR, EU AI Act [European Parliament and Council, 2024]).

-
(iii)
Follow organizational workflows: Respect handoff protocols, approval chains, and escalation paths defined by the organization.

-
(iv)
Adapt to role-specific perspectives: A CFO and a product manager examining the same data should receive analyses framed through their respective decision-making lenses.


Current approaches address these requirements through prompt engineering
[Wei et al., 2022, Wang et al., 2023], retrieval-augmented
generation (RAG) [Lewis et al., 2020, Gao et al., 2024], and
fine-tuning [Hu et al., 2022]. While effective for individual
dimensions, none provides a *unified formal framework* for
enterprise grounding. Prompt engineering is brittle and unverifiable;
RAG retrieves but does not reason; fine-tuning is expensive and
domain-locked.

## 2 Background and Related Work

### 2.1 Neurosymbolic AI

The integration of neural and symbolic AI has been a persistent research theme since the connectionist-symbolic debates of the 1980s [Fodor and Pylyshyn, 1988]. Modern neurosymbolic AI has been catalyzed by three developments: the success of deep learning, the maturation of knowledge representation standards (OWL, RDF, SKOS), and the emergence of LLMs as general-purpose reasoning engines.

Garcez and Lamb [2023] propose a taxonomy of neurosymbolic
integration patterns, distinguishing between *symbolic[neural]*
(symbolic systems enhanced by neural components), *neural[symbolic]*
(neural systems enhanced by symbolic knowledge), and *neural:symbolic*
(tight bidirectional integration). Our work falls primarily in the
*neural[symbolic]* category, with proposals toward
*neural:symbolic* integration. A recent systematic review of 167
NeSyAI papers [Bianchi and Palmonari, 2025] confirms the accelerating
convergence of these paradigms.

Hitzler and Sarker [2022] argue that ontologies provide a natural formalism for neurosymbolic integration, offering type hierarchies, property constraints, and inference rules that can bound neural computation. We extend this argument to the enterprise domain, where ontologies must capture not only domain concepts but organizational roles and interaction protocols.

### 2.2 Knowledge-Grounded Language Models

Knowledge grounding for LLMs has been pursued through several mechanisms:

- •
- •
-
•
Ontology-Enhanced Prompting: Using ontological definitions to structure LLM prompts [Babaei Giglou et al., 2023]. Li et al. [2025] extend this with OntoTune (WWW 2025), an ontology-driven self-training method that aligns LLMs through in-context learning on SNOMED CT, achieving state-of-the-art performance on hypernym discovery and medical QA without large-scale domain corpora. This is closest to our current implementation but neither approach has been formalized for multi-layer enterprise ontologies.

-
•
Constrained Decoding: Restricting LLM output tokens to conform to grammatical or structural constraints [Willard and Louf, 2023]. This provides output-side control but at the syntactic rather than semantic level.


Recent work on augmented language models [Mialon et al., 2023] surveys the broader landscape of tool-augmented LLMs, while Kambhampati et al. [2024] demonstrate that LLMs struggle with planning tasks without external symbolic scaffolding—a finding that supports our argument for ontological grounding. Marcus [2020] argues broadly for hybrid architectures combining neural and symbolic components.

Recent empirical work has begun to validate these approaches quantitatively. Agrawal et al. [2024] survey knowledge graph augmentation techniques for hallucination reduction, finding promising but inconsistent results across domains. Giordano et al. [2025] demonstrate that ontology-grounded LLMs exhibit reduced digression in industrial conversational settings. Asprino and Presutti [2025] compare ontology-guided knowledge graph construction against vector retrieval baselines for RAG, finding that structured approaches outperform flat retrieval when domain knowledge is specialized. Sansone et al. [2024] propose a KG-based evaluation framework for measuring hallucination at the triple level, complementing our metric-based approach.

Our work synthesizes these approaches within a unified ontological framework, extending beyond document retrieval to encompass role-aware context injection, domain-hierarchical tool discovery, and interaction-protocol enforcement. Unlike prior empirical work that evaluates general-purpose QA, our evaluation targets enterprise-specific tasks requiring domain metrics, regulatory compliance, and role-aware reasoning—capabilities that test the boundaries of LLM parametric knowledge.

### 2.3 Ontology-Grounded RAG and Hallucination Mitigation

Recent work has demonstrated dramatic improvements from ontological grounding over baseline RAG. Sharma et al. [2025] propose OG-RAG (EMNLP 2025), which constructs hypergraph representations anchored in domain ontologies, achieving +55% fact recall and +40% response correctness across four LLMs compared to standard RAG. In the clinical domain, Ali et al. [2026] report 98% accuracy (vs. 37% for ChatGPT-4) on clinical question answering, reducing hallucination from 63% to 1.7% through an RDF/OWL ontology-grounded knowledge graph. Amayuelas et al. [2025] ground each LLM reasoning step in knowledge graph data, achieving +26.5% over chain-of-thought baselines.

These results independently validate our central claim that ontological
structure outperforms unstructured retrieval. However, all three systems
use *single-layer* domain ontologies. Our three-layer framework
(, , ) extends this by encoding not just
*what* the agent should know (domain concepts), but *how* it
should reason (role layer) and *when* to delegate (interaction
layer)—layers absent from prior ontology-grounded systems.

### 2.4 Neurosymbolic Integration Taxonomies

Yang et al. [2025b] present the first comprehensive IJCAI survey of
neurosymbolic approaches for LLM reasoning, proposing a three-way
taxonomy: SymbolicLLM (symbolic systems feeding into language models),
LLMSymbolic (language models generating symbolic representations),
and LLM+Symbolic (hybrid integration). More recently,
Acharya et al. [2026] provide a PRISMA-based survey of 178
neurosymbolic agentic AI papers, covering integration patterns,
meta-cognition, and safety verification—confirming that neurosymbolic
architectures for agentic systems are a rapidly maturing field. Our work
falls primarily in the SymbolicLLM category at coupling levels
L1–L3, with proposals toward LLM+Symbolic at L4–L5. Critically, neither
survey taxonomy distinguishes *input-side* from *output-side*
coupling—a distinction central to our asymmetric coupling analysis
(Section˜4).

### 2.5 Parametric Knowledge and Contextual Interference

A growing literature documents the complex interaction between LLM
parametric knowledge and injected context. Lin et al. [2026]
demonstrate that LLMs can become “overly reliant on external information,
suppressing their internal parametric knowledge”—what they term
*contextual interference*. Their Knowledgeable-R1 achieves +22.89%
improvement in counterfactual scenarios by explicitly resisting this
displacement, validating the phenomenon underlying our Inverse Parametric
Knowledge Effect (Section˜9.3).

Du et al. [2025] provide the most striking evidence: even with
*perfect retrieval*, LLM performance degrades 13.9–85% as context
length increases, demonstrating that context volume itself imposes a cost
independent of content quality. Tang et al. [2025a] show that
parametric representations operate at deeper feed-forward layers than
token-level context, suggesting that the displacement we observe occurs
at a computational level distinct from surface processing. A mechanistic
explanation is provided by Tang et al. [2025b] (NeurIPS 2025
spotlight), who discover that parametric and contextual knowledge route
through *distinct attention heads*, with aligned knowledge
accumulating across layers—providing the architectural basis for why
context injection can displace rather than supplement parametric knowledge.

These findings collectively ground our Inverse PKE in emerging
theoretical understanding: ontological grounding helps most where
parametric knowledge is weakest (low-resource domains like Vietnamese
regulatory content), and can actively *harm* performance where
parametric knowledge is strong (well-known English terminology)—a
non-obvious prediction that our 5-industry experiment confirms.

### 2.6 Enterprise Ontologies

Enterprise ontology engineering has a rich history, from the Toronto Virtual Enterprise (TOVE) [Fox et al., 1996] to the Financial Industry Business Ontology (FIBO) maintained by the EDM Council [Bennett, 2013]. Upper ontologies such as the Basic Formal Ontology (BFO) [Arp et al., 2015] and DOLCE [Borgo et al., 2022] provide foundational categories for domain ontology alignment.

However, existing enterprise ontologies were designed for data
integration and semantic interoperability, not for constraining AI
agent behavior. A recent industry effort, the Agentic Ontology of Work
(AOW) [Skan AI, 2026], defines eight entity types (Agents, Skills,
Intents, Contexts, Policies, Memory, Confidence, Outcomes) for
enterprise agent orchestration—the closest industry parallel to our
framework, though AOW focuses on workflow automation rather than
neurosymbolic grounding of LLM reasoning. We extend the enterprise
ontology tradition with three additional layers: *role ontologies*
(how domain actors reason), *interaction ontologies* (how actors
coordinate), and *governance constraints* (what regulatory bounds
apply).

### 2.7 Multi-Agent Systems and Coordination

The multi-agent systems (MAS) literature provides formal frameworks for agent coordination [Guo et al., 2024]. However, classical MAS assumes agents with well-defined utility functions and communication protocols. LLM-based agents introduce stochastic behavior that challenges these assumptions. Concurrent work addresses this gap from different angles: Wang et al. [2025] propose Metagent-P, a neuro-symbolic planning agent that uses symbolic verification to ensure reasoning correctness before execution, while Peer et al. [2025] decouple agent architecture into offline symbolic knowledge base ingestion and online deterministic execution (ATA). Our work addresses the gap by using ontological constraints to bound the stochastic behavior of LLM agents within formally defined operational envelopes, with a focus on enterprise multi-industry deployment rather than open-world planning.

### 4.3 Output-Side Coupling (Proposed)

Output-side coupling would constrain LLM outputs against ontological
definitions *after* generation. This is the primary research
frontier and is not yet implemented in FAOS. We formalize the
requirements in Section˜7.

### 4.4 Coupling Maturity Model

We propose a six-level maturity model for neurosymbolic coupling in enterprise AI systems:

| Level | Name | Description |
|---|---|---|
| L0 | Ungrounded | LLM operates without ontological context |
| L1 | Context-Injected | Ontology provides prompt context (input-side) |
| L2 | Discovery-Constrained | Ontology filters available tools and skills |
| L3 | Process-Gated | Ontology enforces approval gates and escalation during execution |
| L4 | Output-Validated | Ontology validates and constrains LLM outputs post-generation |
| L5 | Closed-Loop | Full bidirectional coupling: ontology constrains inputs, guides reasoning, validates outputs, and evolves from agent experience |

The FAOS platform currently operates at L2–L3, with mechanisms for L1 (context injection), L2 (tool discovery filtering), and partial L3 (autonomy gates, quality judge). This paper proposes the architecture for L4–L5.

For industries with formal regulatory requirements, we propose integrating lightweight OWL reasoning to verify output compliance:

| (15) |

That is, an output is valid if and only if the ontology
does not entail its negation. This requires translating relevant
portions of the Domain Ontology into OWL axioms and running a
description logic reasoner (e.g., HermiT, ELK) on the output. A

our proposed validator operates at the semantic level—the two
approaches are complementary and could be combined in future work.
A related approach by Deng et al. [2025] achieves 99.5% schema
accuracy through model-agnostic post-processing (SLOT), providing
syntactic output structuring that could serve as a preprocessing
stage for our semantic validation.

### 7.3 Feedback Loop: Ontology Evolution from Agent Experience

The most ambitious extension is *ontology evolution*: using agent
experience to refine the ontology itself. We propose a supervised
evolution mechanism:

-
1.
Discovery: Agents encounter domain concepts not in the current ontology

-
2.
Proposal: The system proposes ontology extensions based on recurring unrecognized concepts

-
3.
Validation: Domain experts review and approve proposed extensions

-
4.
Integration: Approved extensions are merged into the ontology with provenance tracking


This creates a *learning ontology* that improves with use, closing
the loop between neural experience and symbolic knowledge.

Recent work on semantic grounding as a hallucination mitigation layer
(ICLR 2026 Workshop on Reliable Autonomy) argues that “hallucinations
arise at perception, not generation”—the problem is how agents
interpret structured data, not how they generate text. This supports
our architectural choice: L1–L3 coupling constrains the agent’s
*perception* (input context) through ontological grounding,
while the proposed L4–L5 extensions would additionally constrain
*generation* (output validation). The industry convergence is
notable: Microsoft’s Fabric IQ (announced November 2025) independently
adopted an ontology-based “IQ layer” for agentic AI, providing
commercial validation of the ontology-first approach that our
architecture formalizes academically.

##### Execution.

Each task was presented under all four conditions with three repetitions ( runs per model). The primary agent LLM was Claude Sonnet 4 [Anthropic, 2025] (temperature 0.3). To establish model-independence, the full experiment was replicated on two additional open-source models: Qwen 2.5 72B [Yang et al., 2025a] via OpenRouter and Gemma 4 26B (MoE, 4B active) via Google AI Studio, for a total of 1,800 runs. An independent judge LLM (Claude Sonnet 4, temperature 0.0) scored all responses across all three replications against ground truth using metric-specific rubrics, following the LLM-as-judge paradigm [Zheng et al., 2024]. Cross-cutting tasks were scored on all four metrics simultaneously. Three design choices mitigate known reliability concerns with LLM-as-judge evaluation [Schroeder and Wood-Doughty, 2025]: (1) temperature 0.0 for deterministic judging, (2) three repetitions per condition providing multiple samples per data point, and (3) structured JSON output schemas that constrain judge scoring to defined rubric dimensions. The same judge model evaluates all conditions, eliminating inter-judge variance as a confound.

#### 8.3.4 RAG vs. Ontology

A notable finding is that C2 (RAG) is competitive with—and on TF, superior to—C3 (Ontology). On the primary model, C2 achieves TF vs. C3 TF , meaning unstructured prose chunks outperform structured ontological injection for pure terminology recall. We identify two contributing mechanisms:

First, this is partially an artifact of our experimental design: the RAG chunks were curated from the same ontology blueprints that define the structured injection, making them unusually well-organized compared to real-world document retrieval.

Second, and more fundamentally, *structured ontological format may
be less token-efficient for terminology recall than unstructured prose*.
The C3 injection organizes content into formal sections (Role definitions
with decision patterns, Domain concepts with metric ranges, Interaction
handoffs with SLA constraints), consuming tokens on structural overhead
(section headers, property-value pairs, typed relationships) that
unstructured C2 prose can devote entirely to weaving domain terms into
natural explanatory context. This structural overhead hypothesis
predicts that C3’s advantage should emerge on tasks requiring
*relational reasoning* (handoff chains, metric range assessment)
rather than *term recall*—a prediction consistent with our
observed pattern where C3 excels on MA and RS (relational) but not TF
(lexical). We note that the token count differential itself constitutes
a confound: C3 injects – tokens versus C2’s
tokens, a 40–60% overhead. A controlled experiment
equalizing token budgets between C2 and C3 would isolate the structural
format effect from the volume effect; this remains future work.

In production deployments where RAG chunks are drawn from heterogeneous, noisy document collections, we expect a larger ontology advantage. The C2–C3 post-hoc comparison was not significant for any metric (), supporting this interpretation.

## 9 Discussion

### 9.1 Why Ontologies, Not Just RAG?

A natural question is whether formal ontologies add value beyond well-organized RAG. Our empirical results provide a nuanced answer. On the significant metrics (MA, RC, RS), structured ontology injection (C3) and RAG (C2) achieved comparable scores. However, we argue ontologies remain essential for three reasons beyond what our pilot study measured:

-
1.
Structural constraints: Ontologies define relationships (hierarchies, handoff patterns, approval chains) that flat documents cannot express. The RS metric captures only a subset of this structural value; full governance enforcement requires machine-readable ontological definitions.

-
2.
Composability: The three-layer framework allows independent evolution of roles, domains, and interactions. Adding a new role does not require rewriting domain documentation.

-
3.
Verifiability: Ontological definitions are machine- readable and can be checked programmatically. RAG documents are opaque to formal verification. This distinction becomes critical at coupling levels L4–L5, where output-side validation requires structured definitions.


We note explicitly that on the primary model’s grand means, C2 (curated RAG)
scores at or above C3 (Ontology) on TF, MA, and RC; only RS shows a consistent
ontology advantage. The three architectural arguments above (structural
constraints, composability, verifiability) therefore remain the primary
justification for ontologies in production settings, where RAG corpora
are noisier and more heterogeneous than our carefully curated C2 baseline.
Our claim is not that ontologies uniformly outperform RAG at the lexical
level, but that they provide *categorically different* capabilities
(relational reasoning, machine-checkable constraints, output-side
validation) that a document-chunk retrieval pipeline cannot.

Beyond these architectural arguments, the cross-model replication provides direct empirical evidence: on Gemma 4, the C2C3 pairwise comparison for Role Consistency was significant (), indicating that structured role ontology injection provides significantly better role consistency than unstructured RAG—the only metricmodel combination where ontology achieved pairwise significance over RAG. This suggests that the structural value of ontologies manifests most clearly on metrics that require relational reasoning (role decision patterns, metric ranges) rather than lexical recall (terminology).

Our pilot study also revealed that the RAG advantage diminishes when chunks are drawn from curated sources. In production settings with noisy, heterogeneous document corpora, ontological structure is expected to provide clearer differentiation.

### 9.2 The Asymmetric Coupling Gap

The most significant finding from our architectural analysis is the
*asymmetric coupling gap*: the current system rigorously constrains
LLM inputs through ontological context but does not validate LLM outputs
against the same ontological definitions. This asymmetry means that an
agent can receive perfect domain context and still generate outputs
that violate domain constraints—the ontology guides but does not
*enforce*.

Closing this gap (moving from L2–L3 to L4–L5) is the primary research and engineering challenge. The difficulty is computational: running OWL reasoners on every agent output introduces latency that may be unacceptable in interactive settings. We believe lightweight constraint checkers (domain-term lookup, metric range validation, handoff pattern matching) can capture the most critical validation requirements without full ontological reasoning.

### 9.3 The Inverse Parametric Knowledge Effect

Our pilot study reveals a phenomenon we term the *inverse parametric
knowledge effect*: the value of ontological grounding is inversely
proportional to the LLM’s pre-existing parametric knowledge of the
domain concept. Three observations support this:

-
1.
Metric Accuracy benefits most (, ). KPI benchmarks (healthy ranges, world-class targets, SLA thresholds) are enterprise-specific and rarely appear in public training corpora. Ontological injection directly supplies this missing knowledge. Symmetrically, Regulatory Compliance shows the

*smallest*lift among the significant metrics (Claude vs. MA ) because regulatory frameworks (Basel III, HIPAA, PCI-DSS, SBV circulars) are public documents widely represented in pretraining corpora. This asymmetry—KPI benchmarks private, regulations public—is itself a prediction of the Inverse PKE rather than an anomaly. -
2.
TF regression on well-known concepts. For insurance terms like “combined ratio” and “persistency rate”—concepts extensively covered in textbooks and public documents—ontology injection

*reduced*scores (IN-T1: ; IN-T2: ). We hypothesize a*context displacement*mechanism: injected ontological context occupies system prompt capacity that would otherwise be available for the LLM’s natural parametric recall. -
3.
Vietnamese industries show the largest . The 5-industry expansion provides the strongest evidence yet: banking_vn () and insurance_vn () show 2 the improvement of English-language industries (avg ). Vietnamese regulatory concepts—SBV Circular 11/2021 NPL thresholds, MoF solvency margin requirements per TT 132/2023, bancassurance cooling-off periods—are severely underrepresented in English-centric pretraining corpora, making ontological grounding essential rather than merely beneficial. This finding is independently supported by three convergent results: Xu et al. [2024] taxonomize knowledge conflicts in LLMs, formalizing context-memory conflicts as cases where injected context contradicts parametric knowledge—precisely the dynamic we observe in well-known TF tasks; Lin et al. [2026] demonstrate that LLMs suppress parametric knowledge when context is injected (“contextual interference”, +22.89% from resisting this); and Du et al. [2025] show that context length alone degrades performance 13.9–85%. Additionally, Longpre et al. [2025] demonstrate that fact dynamicity predicts when LLMs ignore injected context in favor of parametric knowledge, providing a principled mechanism for why well-known terminology resists ontological grounding. Together, these suggest that our Inverse PKE reflects a fundamental property of neural-symbolic interaction, not a Claude-specific artifact.

-
4.
RS improvements are language-invariant. Role Consistency improved by +.27 (C1C3) across all five industries regardless of language, with C3 scores in a narrow range (.913–.936 for Claude, .899–.968 for Gemma). This uniformity is consistent with role-specific decision patterns being uniformly underrepresented in parametric knowledge, though an alternative explanation is judge calibration ceiling effects—the LLM judge may score role consistency in a narrow band regardless of actual variation. Human expert validation would disambiguate these interpretations.


This effect has practical implications for system design: a production
ontology injection pipeline should estimate the LLM’s parametric
confidence per concept and suppress injection for well-known domains,
reserving context budget for enterprise-specific knowledge where the
marginal value is highest. The Vietnamese results suggest that ontological
grounding becomes *mandatory*—not optional—for non-English
enterprise domains where LLM parametric coverage is structurally insufficient.

Toward a formal model. The interaction between parametric and
injected knowledge bears a structural resemblance to wave interference:
*constructive* in low-coverage domains (Vietnamese, where injection
supplies missing knowledge and the two signals reinforce) and
*destructive* in high-coverage domains (English insurance
terminology, where injection displaces existing parametric knowledge).
This interaction is further supported by the knowledge conflict taxonomy
of Xu et al. [2024], who formalize context-memory conflicts as
cases where injected context contradicts parametric knowledge—precisely
the dynamic we observe in well-known TF tasks. Our companion work on
context optimization [Luong Tuan, 2026b] develops a formal
information-theoretic model that predicts the optimal
injection volume as a function of parametric knowledge density, providing
testable predictions for adaptive injection strategies. A planned
standalone study [Luong Tuan, 2026a] will formalize the interference
analogy through a quantum-inspired mathematical framework with testable
predictions derived from the RA-3 and RA-4 empirical datasets.

Entropy signature of Inverse PKE. A retroactive entropy analysis
of the experimental data provides quantitative evidence for the interference
interpretation. Following Farquhar et al. [2024], who introduced
semantic entropy as a measure of meaning-level uncertainty in LLM outputs,

Wei et al. [2025] independently demonstrate that structural entropy

semantic entropy from single generations [Kossen et al., 2025] suggest that
entropy-based Inverse PKE detection could operate at inference time, enabling
adaptive injection pipelines that suppress context for high-confidence domains.

### 9.4 Why C3 and C4 Are Indistinguishable

A notable non-result across all three models is the absence of significant C3C4 improvement on any metric ( in all cases). This means our experiment found no evidence that process-side coupling (L3: quality judge + escalation) adds measurable value beyond input-side coupling (L2: structured ontology injection).

We attribute this to experimental design rather than architectural
inefficacy. The quality judge in C4 scored output quality and flagged
sub-threshold responses for escalation, but *did not alter the
response itself*—by design, to ensure fair within-subject comparison
of the grounding conditions. In production deployment, the escalation
mechanism interrupts low-quality interactions entirely, routing to a
supervisory agent or human operator, producing a qualitatively different
user experience not captured by our within-subject scoring. Future
evaluation should measure C4’s value through user-facing metrics
(e.g., escalation rate, downstream task success) rather than
single-turn response quality.

### 9.5 Multi-Tenancy as Neurosymbolic Challenge

The multi-tenant nature of FAOS introduces a unique neurosymbolic
challenge: the *same* ontological framework must support
*different* domain instantiations per tenant. Tenant A (a bank)
and Tenant B (a hospital) share the three-layer schema but populate it
with entirely different domain concepts. This requires ontological
*polymorphism*—the ability to reason generically over the
ontology structure while respecting domain-specific content.

### 9.6 Convergence with Independent Systems

Our findings participate in a broader convergence: multiple independent systems have validated that ontological structure dramatically improves LLM grounding. OG-RAG [Sharma et al., 2025] achieves +55% fact recall through ontology-anchored hypergraph retrieval (EMNLP 2025). Ali et al. [2026] report 98% accuracy (vs. 37% baseline) in clinical QA through RDF/OWL knowledge graphs. Amayuelas et al. [2025] improve reasoning by +26.5% through step-level KG grounding. Microsoft’s Fabric IQ (November 2025) commercially adopted an ontology-based intelligence layer for enterprise agentic AI. In the governance domain, Pandey [2025] propose a Governance-as-a-Service framework with Trust Factor scoring for multi-agent compliance enforcement—complementary to our ontology-driven approach, which provides the semantic foundation that such governance frameworks can operate upon.

The key distinction of our work within this convergence is
*multi-layer semantic typing*: while OG-RAG, Clinical GraphRAG, and
KG-grounded reasoning use single-layer domain ontologies, our three-layer
framework (, , ) encodes orthogonal
information classes—*how* to reason, *what* to know, and
*when* to delegate. This decomposition enables the priority-based
token optimization (Section˜4) that production
systems require under strict context budgets.

### 9.9 Threats to Validity

-
1.
Ontology completeness: If a domain concept or regulation is missing from the ontology, the grounding is incomplete. Ontology maintenance cost scales with regulatory change velocity.

-
2.
OWL reasoning practicality: The proposed output-side validation via description logic reasoners (Section 7.2) requires translating free-text LLM outputs into OWL-compatible representations—itself an error-prone process that may introduce new failure modes.

-
3.
Single-system analysis: Our findings are based on one platform (FAOS); generalization to other enterprise agentic systems (CrewAI, AutoGen, LangGraph standalone) requires further study. However, the cross-model replication across three distinct LLM architectures (Section˜9.7) demonstrates that the grounding effects are not model-specific.

-
4.
LLM-as-judge reliability: Our evaluation relies on an LLM judge (Claude Sonnet 4) rather than human expert scoring. Schroeder and Wood-Doughty [2025] demonstrate that single-shot LLM judgments can be misleading, and Gu et al. [2025] provide a comprehensive survey of LLM-as-judge failure modes including position bias, length bias, and transitivity failures. More critically, Gatto et al. [2025] show that SME–LLM judge agreement reaches only 64–68% in expert knowledge tasks—directly relevant given our domain-specific enterprise evaluation. We mitigate these concerns with three repetitions per condition, temperature 0.0 (deterministic scoring), and structured JSON output schemas. However, our study has not yet validated inter-rater reliability () against domain experts—a priority for the full evaluation. The TF regression observed on well-known concepts may partially reflect judge sensitivity to structured versus unstructured input format rather than genuine performance degradation—a distinction resolvable only through human validation. As a sensitivity envelope: under a pessimistic judge–human disagreement rate (consistent with Gatto et al. [2025]’s SME–LLM agreement band), assuming disagreements are evenly distributed across conditions, our observed MA grand-mean effect (; C1 , C3 ) would compress to approximately after distributional correction—still a large effect by conventional thresholds and larger than the minimum detectable difference for our design. The entropy signature in §9.3 provides a second line of evidence independent of judge calibration.

-
5.
Curated RAG baseline: Our C2 (RAG) condition used chunks extracted from the same ontology blueprints that define C3, making C2 unusually well-structured. Production RAG deployments with heterogeneous source documents would likely show a larger ontology advantage, consistent with Sharma et al. [2025]’s finding that ontological structuring yields +55% fact recall over baseline RAG.

-
6.
Context interference: The TF regression on well-known concepts suggests that ontology injection can displace useful parametric knowledge—a phenomenon independently validated by Lin et al. [2026] (+22.89% from resisting contextual interference) and Du et al. [2025] (13.9–85% degradation from context length alone). This interaction warrants further investigation with adaptive injection strategies.

-
7.
Ecological validity: The ontology content used in the experiment is the

*same content*deployed to production agents in the FAOS platform across 22 industry verticals. This production grounding provides ecological validity beyond laboratory conditions, though generalization to other enterprise agentic systems requires replication.

## 10 Conclusion and Future Work

We have presented a neurosymbolic architecture for enterprise AI agents
that uses a three-layer ontological framework (Role, Domain, Interaction)
to ground LLM reasoning in formal domain knowledge. Our primary
contribution is the formalization of *asymmetric neurosymbolic
coupling*—the observation that current enterprise AI systems constrain
agent inputs but not outputs—and the proposal of a *closed-loop
neurosymbolic framework* that would extend ontological constraints to
output validation and ontology evolution.

Our empirical evaluation across 1,800 experimental runs (600 per model) spanning five regulated industries—including two Vietnamese-language domains—and three LLM architectures (Claude Sonnet 4, Qwen 2.5 72B, Gemma 4 26B) provides strong, model-independent evidence for the value of ontological grounding. On the primary model, ontology-coupled agents significantly outperformed ungrounded agents on Metric Accuracy (, ) and Role Consistency (, ), with large effect sizes. Regulatory Compliance reached significance on the primary model (, ) but was model-sensitive: significant on Claude and Qwen () but not Gemma (), suggesting that regulatory reasoning benefits from ontological grounding but the magnitude depends on the model’s pre-existing regulatory knowledge. Cross-model replication confirmed MA and RS universally: both achieved on all three models. Crucially, the improvement is greatest where LLM parametric knowledge is weakest: Vietnamese industries showed 2 the improvement of English-language domains ( vs. ), and this amplification replicated across all three models, confirming that ontological grounding becomes mandatory—not merely beneficial—for non-English enterprise domains where LLM parametric coverage is structurally insufficient.

The architecture is implemented in a production system serving 22 industry verticals with 650+ specialized agents, demonstrating that the three-layer ontological framework is both industry-invariant (in structure) and industry-specific (in content). Our proposed six-level maturity model provides a roadmap for the field, from ungrounded LLM deployment (L0) to full closed-loop neurosymbolic integration (L5).

Our findings participate in a broader convergence: OG-RAG
[Sharma et al., 2025] validates ontological structuring for retrieval
(+55% fact recall), Ali et al. [2026] demonstrate 98% accuracy
through ontology-grounded clinical KGs, and Lin et al. [2026]
independently validate the parametric displacement phenomenon underlying
our Inverse PKE. Within this landscape, our unique contribution is the
*multi-layer enterprise ontology* with controlled cross-industry
empirical validation—the first study to demonstrate that ontological
grounding value varies systematically with LLM parametric coverage of
the target domain.

Future work will focus on four areas:

-
1.
Layer ablation: Isolating the contribution of each ontology layer (, , ) through a factorial design that selectively injects one or two layers, determining which layer drives which metric improvement.

2.

Prior work on adaptive retrieval—Self-RAG [Asai et al., 2024] uses reflection tokens to decide

We believe neurosymbolic architectures are not merely beneficial but
*necessary* for enterprise AI agents operating in regulated
industries. The question is not whether to integrate symbolic and
neural reasoning, but how to do so with minimal latency, maximal
coverage, and provable guarantees.
