## Introduction and Motivation
The human brain exhibits a striking spatial organization in which neurons with similar functional responses are often located in close proximity. This spatio-functional organization has been extensively documented in sensory systems, particularly in the visual cortex, where neurons cluster based on orientation preferences or object categories such as faces and scenes. However, the mechanisms underlying similar functional clustering in the language system—such as regions selective for verbs, nouns, or syntactic structures—remain poorly understood. While modern artificial neural networks, especially transformer-based language models, have demonstrated remarkable success in predicting neural responses to linguistic stimuli, they lack an explicit spatial structure that mirrors the topographic organization observed in the human cortex. This gap limits their ability to serve as comprehensive models of brain function. To address this, the authors introduce TopoLM, a novel topographic language model that integrates spatial constraints into the transformer architecture, enabling the emergence of brain-like functional clusters. The model is designed to simultaneously perform next-token prediction and optimize a spatial smoothness loss, encouraging nearby units to develop correlated activation patterns. This dual objective mimics the biological principle of minimizing wiring cost, which is hypothesized to drive the development of topography in the brain. By training solely on naturalistic text without fitting to neural data, TopoLM demonstrates that brain-like spatial organization can emerge from a unified learning objective. The model successfully replicates key empirical findings, including the presence of language-selective networks and fine-grained semantic clusters for verbs and nouns, particularly for concrete words. These results suggest that the functional architecture of the human language system may arise from general principles of spatial efficiency and response smoothness, rather than being hardwired or learned through domain-specific mechanisms. The work extends the topographic deep artificial neural network (TDANN) framework from vision to language, offering a new avenue for understanding cortical organization across modalities.

## Model Architecture and Training Procedure
TopoLM is built upon the standard transformer architecture but introduces a critical innovation: an explicit two-dimensional spatial layout for the model’s internal units. Each attention and MLP layer is mapped bijectively onto a 28×28 grid, resulting in 784 units per layer, which allows for a structured spatial representation. To prevent the model from exploiting feed-forward propagation to trivially minimize spatial loss, the spatial positions are randomly permuted across layers, ensuring that each layer develops its own unique topographic map. During training, the model is optimized using a combined loss function consisting of the standard cross-entropy loss for next-token prediction and an additional spatial correlation loss (SL). The spatial loss is computed by measuring the Pearson correlation between unit activation patterns and the inverse of their spatial distances. Specifically, for each layer, pairwise correlations of unit activations are calculated across a batch of inputs, and these are compared to a vector of inverse distances based on the units’ grid positions. The loss is defined as half of one minus this correlation, ensuring it ranges between 0 and 1. To improve computational efficiency, the spatial loss is approximated using small local neighborhoods rather than the full layer, focusing optimization on local smoothness rather than global structure. The total loss is then a weighted sum of the task loss and the spatial losses across all layers, with a fixed weight αₖ = 2.5. The model is trained on a 10-billion-token subset of the FineWeb-Edu dataset using autoregressive language modeling. A non-topographic baseline model is also trained with αₖ = 0 to isolate the effects of the spatial constraint. Both models use identical hyperparameters, including batch size, learning rate, and optimizer settings (AdamW with warmup and cosine decay). After training, the topographic model achieves a slightly higher validation task loss (3.075 vs. 2.966), indicating a minor trade-off in language modeling performance for the sake of spatial organization. However, this cost is offset by the emergence of structured functional maps. To simulate fMRI measurements, a Gaussian smoothing kernel with full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 2.0 mm is applied to model activations, mimicking the spatial blurring inherent in neuroimaging data. This readout sampling is applied before computing selectivity or clustering metrics, ensuring that comparisons with brain data are methodologically consistent.

## Spatio-Functional Organization of the Core Language System
The core language system in the human brain consists of left-lateralized frontal and temporal regions that respond selectively to linguistic input. This system is functionally organized such that different subregions exhibit consistent response profiles to various types of linguistic stimuli, including sentences, scrambled words, and nonwords. To investigate whether TopoLM replicates this organization, the authors apply a functional localizer based on Fedorenko et al. (2010), contrasting responses to syntactically valid sentences versus nonword sequences. Units that show significant language selectivity (p < 0.05, FDR-corrected) are identified as part of the model’s core language system. An evolutionary clustering algorithm is then used to detect spatially contiguous clusters of language-selective units. The results reveal that TopoLM develops multiple such clusters across its layers, demonstrating a clear spatial organization. Moreover, the response profiles of these clusters are highly consistent with one another, mirroring the uniformity observed across human cortical language areas. When tested on a battery of linguistic stimuli—sentences, unconnected words, Jabberwocky sentences (syntactically correct but lexically nonsensical), and nonwords—TopoLM shows a response hierarchy similar to that seen in the brain: sentences elicit the strongest activation, followed by Jabberwocky and unconnected words, with nonwords producing the weakest response. While the model does not perfectly replicate the human data (e.g., unconnected words produce higher activation than in brain recordings), its overall pattern is significantly more brain-like than that of the non-topographic baseline, which fails to capture the neural response profile. This suggests that the spatial smoothness constraint is crucial for developing a functionally coherent language network. The findings are further supported by supplementary analyses showing robust clustering across layers and under simulated fMRI conditions.

## Emergence of Semantic Clusters for Verbs and Nouns
Beyond general language selectivity, the human cortex exhibits fine-grained functional specialization, including distinct clusters for verbs and nouns. Neuroimaging studies, such as Hauptman et al. (2024), have shown strong spatial clustering of verb- and noun-selective regions in the left hemisphere, with a high degree of spatial autocorrelation (Moran’s I = 0.96). To test whether TopoLM reproduces this organization, the authors compute contrast maps between verb and noun stimuli and quantify clustering using Moran’s I. The results show that TopoLM develops clear verb- and noun-selective clusters, with a Moran’s I of 0.48 at the unit level, increasing to 0.81 under simulated fMRI conditions. In contrast, the non-topographic baseline shows minimal clustering (I = 0.11). This indicates that the spatial smoothness loss is essential for generating brain-like semantic organization. The model also replicates findings from Moseley & Pulvermüller (2014), who reported that verb-noun selectivity is stronger for concrete words than for abstract ones. TopoLM shows significantly higher clustering for concrete verb-noun contrasts (I = 0.80) compared to abstract ones (I = 0.23), while the baseline model shows no such difference. This specificity suggests that the model captures not only categorical distinctions but also semantic nuances. The authors further validate these results using Topoformer-BERT, a previously proposed topographic model with local connectivity. While Topoformer-BERT shows some clustering before significance thresholding, it fails to produce meaningful brain-like clusters after correction, indicating that local connectivity alone is insufficient for functional alignment. These findings collectively demonstrate that TopoLM’s spatial smoothness objective leads to the emergence of semantically interpretable, brain-like functional clusters.
