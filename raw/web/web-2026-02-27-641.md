## Picture this: You’ve found the perfect strata unit in Coquitlam – ocean views, gym access, the works. But as closing day nears, a surprise $3,000 special levy hits, tied to a leaky condo repair you never saw coming. Sound familiar?

In British Columbia’s bustling strata market, these curveballs are all too common, unless you arm yourself with a Form B Information Certificate.

Under Section 59 of the Strata Property Act, Form B is more than a form; it’s a transparent snapshot of your potential home’s financial health, legal standing, and hidden gems (or pitfalls). Whether you’re buying, selling, or refinancing, this guide demystifies Form B step by step. We’ll cover what it reveals, why it’s non-negotiable, and pro tips to review it like a strata veteran. By the end, you’ll close deals with confidence, and maybe even save thousands.

Ready to unlock the strata secrets? Let’s start with the basics.

### Step 1: Quick Primer

What It Is: Form B is an official “Information Certificate” under Section 59 of the Strata Property Act. It’s a standardized disclosure document issued by a strata corporation (or its manager) for a specific strata lot. It certifies key details as of the issuance date, helping buyers, sellers, lenders, or owners assess risks and obligations.

Why It Matters: It reveals financials (e.g., fees, levies), legal issues (e.g., lawsuits), and amenities (e.g., parking). Skipping it can lead to surprises like unpaid debts or bylaw violations, critical for due diligence in BC’s hot real estate market.

Key Contents: The form covers 16 main items (a-p), plus mandatory attachments like the strata’s rules, budget, and Depreciation Report. Highlights include monthly fees, outstanding debts, reserve funds, unresolved disputes, parking/storage allocations, and insurance summaries.

Cost & Timeline: Typically $35, with a 5 business day turnaround. Requests go through the strata council or property manager or through a platform like StrataPress.

Common Pitfalls: Outdated info (it’s only current to the certification date), incomplete attachments, or ignoring “yes” flags on legal/judgment items.

### What Is a Form B Certificate?

In British Columbia’s strata world, the Form B Information Certificate (often just called “Form B”) is the go-to disclosure document for anyone involved in buying, selling, refinancing, or even just understanding a strata lot. Officially named under Section 59 of the Strata Property Act, it’s a standardized, mandatory certificate that the strata corporation must provide upon request.

Think of Form B as a certified snapshot of the strata lot and the corporation as of the exact date it’s issued. The strata council (or authorized manager) certifies the accuracy of the information, making it a reliable tool for due diligence. It’s not optional in most transactions, lenders often require it, and savvy buyers treat it as non-negotiable.

### Who can request it?

Any owner of the strata lot, a prospective purchaser, or someone authorized by either (like a real estate lawyer, notary, or agent).

### What does it cover?

Form B discloses key financial, legal, and operational details specific to the lot and the broader strata corporation. This includes monthly fees, outstanding debts, special levies, contingency reserve fund status, litigation, parking/storage allocations, insurance summaries, and more, plus mandatory attachments like the strata’s rules, current budget, and (if available) the latest depreciation report.

The form itself is prescribed in the Strata Property Regulations and must use the most current version (regularly updated; always check the latest on BC Laws or gov.bc.ca).

As of April 2023, it includes a required summary of the strata corporation’s insurance coverage, a critical addition for assessing risk in today’s market. In short: Form B isn’t just paperwork, it’s your first line of defense against hidden surprises in a strata purchase or sale.

### Why Form B Is Essential for Strata Transactions

In British Columbia’s competitive strata market, where condos, townhomes, and duplexes make up a huge portion of urban ownership, skipping or rushing through a Form B Information Certificate can turn a dream purchase into a financial nightmare.

This isn’t hyperbole: Form B is often called the most critical document in a strata deal because it delivers certified, binding transparency on the lot and corporation that verbal assurances or glossy listings simply can’t match.

Under the Strata Property Act, the information in Form B is legally binding on the strata corporation, giving buyers, sellers, lenders, and even current owners a reliable foundation for decisions. Here’s why it’s non-negotiable in most transactions:

- Financial Clarity and Cost Protection:

Form B reveals exact monthly strata fees, any arrears or special levies (outstanding or approved), and the health of the contingency reserve fund (CRF). Low reserves or pending levies can signal big future bills – think $20,000+ per owner for repairs like balconies, elevators, or leaky envelopes. Without this snapshot, buyers risk inheriting massive unexpected costs post-closing. - Legal and Risk Mitigation:

It flags ongoing litigation, court orders, Civil Resolution Tribunal disputes, or municipal work orders. A “yes” on these can mean liens, legal fees, or forced repairs – issues that could derail financing or resale value. Lenders almost always require a current Form B to assess mortgage risk; no Form B often means no loan approval. - Amenity and Ownership Confirmation:

Details on parking stalls, storage lockers (whether assigned as limited common property or subject to change), and rental restrictions help buyers confirm what they’re actually getting. In dense BC markets like Vancouver or Coquitlam, parking allocation alone can add or subtract thousands in perceived value. - Insurance and Long-Term Health Insights:

Since the 2023 updates, Form B includes a mandatory summary of the strata’s insurance coverage (deductibles, perils covered). This helps spot gaps that could leave owners exposed in disasters which is vital in an era of rising claims and premiums. - Due Diligence and Transaction Smoothing:

Paired with Form A (bylaws/rules) and the depreciation report, Form B completes the “strata health check.” It empowers buyers to negotiate repairs, adjust offers, or walk away with eyes wide open—often preventing post-purchase regrets or disputes.

In short, Form B isn’t optional paperwork, it’s your shield against hidden liabilities in a shared-ownership world. Many experts call it the single most important review before removing subjects or closing. Lenders demand it, smart buyers insist on it, and savvy sellers provide it upfront to build trust and speed up deals.

To make this crystal clear at a glance, Form B delivers:

- Financial Transparency – Uncovers fees, levies, reserve status—avoids surprise special assessments that can reach tens of thousands.
- Legal Safeguards – Highlights lawsuits, orders, or disputes that could lien the property or trigger costs
- Amenity Verification – Confirms parking/storage rights which is critical in high-demand urban areas.
- Insurance Overview – Shows coverage details to assess personal risk exposure.
- Lender Requirement – Banks/mortgage providers typically won’t fund without a current Form B.
- Overall Risk Reduction – Enables informed decisions, negotiations, or walking away – it protects investment and peace of mind.

In BC strata transactions, Form B turns guesswork into facts. Ignore it, and you’re gambling with one of the biggest purchases of your life. Review it thoroughly, and you’ll close confidently.

### Breaking Down the Form: What Every Section Reveals

This is where the rubber meets the road.

The Form B Information Certificate is structured around a series of specific items (labeled a through p in the prescribed form under the Strata Property Act and Regulations), each disclosing critical details about the strata lot and corporation.

The information must be current as of the certificate’s date, and the strata certifies its accuracy, making any misstatements potentially actionable. We’ll break it down group by group for easier reading, with plain-English explanations, what to watch for, and practical tips. (Note: Always use the most current prescribed Form B version from BC Laws or gov.bc.ca, as minor updates occur periodically; the structure below reflects the version effective as of recent amendments, including the 2023 insurance summary requirement.)

Financial Details (Items a–f): The Money Picture

These sections reveal the strata lot’s day-to-day and potential big-ticket costs, often the biggest red flags for buyers.

- (a) Monthly strata fees payable

The regular monthly contribution for this specific lot.

Tip: Compare to similar units; sudden increases can signal upcoming budget pressures. - (b) Any amount owing (e.g., arrears, unpaid special levies)

What the current owner owes the strata (excluding trust-held amounts).

Red flag: Any positive dollar amount – buyers may negotiate seller payment or face collection post-closing. - (c) Agreements for owner responsibility on alterations

Yes/No on indemnity agreements where the owner covers future repair costs for changes to the lot, common property, or assets (attach copies if yes).

Tip: Common in older buildings with owner-installed windows or balconies which could mean ongoing liability. - (d) Future payments for approved special levies

Amounts and due dates for already-approved special assessments.

Watch: Even if not yet due, these are binding – factor this into your offer. - (e) Budget shortfall projection

Expected excess expenses over the current year’s budget.

Tip: A large shortfall often leads to fee hikes or levies soon. - (f) Contingency reserve fund (CRF) balance

Funds available minus approved (but unspent) expenditures.

Pro insight: Healthy CRFs are typically 10–25%+ of annual operating costs; low balances signal potential future special levies for repairs.

Governance & Bylaw Status (Items g–i): Upcoming Changes

These flag shifts in rules or structure that could affect use or value.

- (g) Unfiled bylaw amendments

Yes/No + attach copies. - (h) Unfiled 3/4 or unanimous resolutions

Including (h.1) winding-up resolutions. - (i) Notice of pending 3/4, 80%, or unanimous votes (e.g., bylaw changes).

Tip: Pending votes on rental restrictions, pet bylaws, or major repairs can dramatically change living conditions. Review attachments closely.

Legal & Compliance Issues (Items j–k, p): Risk Alerts

- (j) Court, arbitration, CRT proceedings, judgments/orders

Yes/No + details/attachments.

Red flag: Litigation (especially leaky condo or defect claims) can mean liens or big payouts. - (k) Outstanding notices or work orders

From municipality or others for the lot, common property, or assets.

Tip: These often require costly fixes. Get copies and consult a lawyer. - (p) Electrical planning reports (under s. 94)

Confirmation if obtained (attach if yes).

Note: Required for buildings with certain electrical risks; absence might indicate no major concerns.

Amenities: Parking & Storage (Items m–n)

Critical in BC’s urban markets. These details confirm what you’re actually buying.

- (m) Parking stall(s) allocated

Detailed breakdown: part of strata lot, separate lot, limited common property (LCP), or common property; allocation method (council approval, rental, developer assignment); note on potential change (common property allocations can be revoked).

Tip: “Common property” stalls aren’t guaranteed long-term. - (n) Storage locker(s) allocated

Similar detailed options as parking.

Pro hack: Verify numbers match what the seller showed; discrepancies kill deals.

Insurance Summary (Item o)

- Summary of strata corporation’s insurance coverage

Provided on separate sheet(s) – includes perils covered, deductibles, etc.

Why it matters: Since April 2023, mandatory. High deductibles (e.g., $50k+ for water damage) mean owners may need personal top-up coverage, check against your condo policy.

Mandatory Attachments

The Form B isn’t complete without these (per section. 59(4)):

- Strata corporation’s rules (if any)
- Current operating budget
- Most recent Depreciation Report (if any)
- Summary of insurance coverage
- Electrical planning reports (if any)
- Plus any copies required from “yes” answers (agreements, resolutions, work orders, etc.)

Quick-Scan Checklist: 7 Must-Check Items in Your Form B

- Any amounts owing or special levies? → Negotiate seller payoff.
- CRF balance healthy? → Low = future costs likely.
- “Yes” on litigation/work orders? → Lawyer review ASAP.
- Parking/storage as expected? → Confirm allocation type.
- Insurance deductibles reasonable? → Budget for potential gaps.
- Pending bylaw/vote notices? → Could change lifestyle/rules.
- All required attachments included? → Missing ones delay or derail.

This breakdown turns the Form B from a dense document into actionable intel. Spot issues early, and you can adjust offers, seek remedies, or walk away protected. In the next section, we’ll cover how to obtain and review one effectively.

### How to Obtain and Review a Form B

Obtaining a Form B Information Certificate is straightforward under British Columbia law, but timing, preparation, and thorough review are key to making it work for you. Section 59 of the Strata Property Act mandates that the strata corporation provide it promptly and accurately. Here’s the practical step-by-step process, plus pro tips for reviewing it effectively.

Step-by-Step: How to Obtain a Form B

- Determine Who Can Request It

Only an owner of the strata lot, a prospective purchaser, or a person authorized by either (e.g., real estate agent, lawyer, notary, or family member with written permission) can make the request. In practice, buyers often have their realtor or lawyer handle it. - Contact the Right Party

Submit your request to the strata corporation – typically via the property manager (most common) or strata council (for self-managed stratas).

Check your strata’s contact info in the bylaws, recent minutes, or strata portal.

Many managers (including StrataPress) offer online request forms for faster turnaround.

If it’s a sectional strata (e.g., townhome section within a larger condo complex), request from both the main corporation and the section. - Make the Request

Put it in writing (email, letter, or online portal) and include:

Strata plan number and lot number. - Pay the Fee

The maximum fee is set by regulation—currently $35 (subject to change; verify at gov.bc.ca). Some managers charge less. Payment is typically required upfront before the form is issued. - Wait for Delivery

The strata corporation has up to 5 business days to provide the completed Form B after receiving a request and payment. Many managers turn it around faster. If you’re in a tight subject-removal window, request it the moment your offer is accepted—or even before, if the seller can arrange early access - Confirm Completeness

When you receive the Form B, check immediately that all mandatory attachments are included: the strata’s rules, current operating budget, most recent Depreciation Report, insurance summary, and any copies triggered by “yes” answers. An incomplete Form B is not legally sufficient and may need to be reissued – which costs you time.

### How to Review a Form B Like a Pro

Receiving the form is only half the job. Here’s how to extract maximum value from it:

Read it the same day it arrives. Subject-removal windows in BC can be as short as 5–7 business days. Don’t let the Form B sit in your inbox.

Start with the red-flag items. Jump straight to items (b), (d), (f), (j), and (k)—amounts owing, special levies, CRF balance, legal proceedings, and work orders. If any of these have concerning answers, escalate immediately to your agent, lawyer, or notary.

Cross-reference the attachments. Don’t just skim the form itself—the attachments often contain the most important details. Read the budget carefully for signs of underfunding. Scan the Depreciation Report for upcoming major repairs and recommended levy amounts. Review the insurance summary for high deductibles.

Compare fees to market norms. Monthly strata fees that are unusually low can be a red flag—they may indicate an underfunded CRF or deferred maintenance. Unusually high fees warrant explanation too.

Verify parking and storage in person. Confirm that the stall and locker numbers on the Form B match what was shown during your property viewing. Mismatches have derailed closings.

Consult a strata lawyer if anything is unclear. For a few hundred dollars, a BC strata lawyer can review the Form B and flag issues you might miss. This is especially worthwhile for older buildings, complex stratas, or any form with “yes” answers on legal items.

### Common Mistakes to Avoid

Even experienced buyers can slip up when dealing with Form B. Watch out for these pitfalls:

Treating it as just a formality. Form B is a legal document with binding implications. The strata corporation is certifying its accuracy, take every item seriously.

Missing the certification date. The form is only valid as of the date it was issued. If it’s more than a few weeks old by the time you’re reviewing it, request a fresh one. Strata finances and legal situations can change quickly.

Ignoring the Depreciation Report. Many buyers focus on the Form B itself but skim the Depreciation Report attachment. This is a mistake. The report forecasts future repair costs over a 30-year horizon and is one of the best indicators of upcoming special levies.

Not requesting it early enough. In competitive markets, buyers sometimes delay the Form B request to save the $35 fee until they’re sure the deal is moving forward. In practice, this can compress your review window dangerously. Order it as soon as possible after your offer is accepted.

Forgetting to request from sectional corporations. In complex strata developments with sections (e.g., a mixed residential/commercial building, or a townhome complex with a separate section corporation), you may need Form B from both the main strata and the section. Missing one can leave you blind to section-specific levies or bylaws.

### Form B and the Bigger Due Diligence Picture

Form B is powerful, but it works best as part of a complete strata health check. Pair it with these other key documents:

**Form A (Schedule of Standard Bylaws and Rules):** Tells you what you can and can’t do in and around your unit—pets, rentals, renovations, noise. Often attached to Form B, but always verify it’s current.

**Strata Meeting Minutes (last 2 years):** Minutes from AGMs and council meetings reveal decisions, disputes, complaints, and upcoming work that may not yet appear on Form B. Request these separately.

**Depreciation Report:** Provides a long-range view of the building’s physical health and projected repair costs. If the strata doesn’t have one (some are exempt), factor that uncertainty into your offer.

**Engineering or Building Envelope Reports:** For older buildings or those with known moisture issues, additional technical reports can reveal problems that no form fully captures.

Together, these documents give you the full picture, not just a snapshot, but a movie of where the strata has been and where it’s headed.

### Final Thoughts: Form B Is Your Best Friend in a Strata Deal

Whether you’re a first-time buyer navigating your first condo purchase or a seasoned investor adding to your portfolio, the Form B Information Certificate is one of the most valuable tools at your disposal in BC’s strata market. It brings transparency to a type of ownership that, by its very nature, involves shared finances, shared decisions, and shared risk.

The $35 and five business days it takes to obtain one are among the best-spent resources in any strata transaction. Reviewed carefully—and paired with the right professional advice—Form B can save you from costly surprises, empower smarter negotiations, and give you genuine confidence as you close.

Don’t just collect it. Read it. Question it. Use it.
