# The Unit Economics of AI Agents: How I Budget LLM Costs in Production (With Real Numbers)

**Most “agentic AI” content skips the one question that decides whether anything ships:**

What does it cost when this is running every day?

Not “what does it cost to demo.” Not “what does it cost to test prompts.” I mean production costs: invoices processed, emails drafted, tickets resolved, workflows executed. This is where most teams get surprised.

I run a 14-agent AI workforce at Kloudedge Apex, and we also build agentic systems for clients. This article is a practical breakdown of how I think about unit economics for LLM-powered workflows. It is not theory. It is the budgeting model you can steal.

The mental model: cost is mostly output, not input

The first pricing detail most teams miss is simple:

**Output tokens are usually 3 to 10 times more expensive than input tokens.**

So if you are running a workflow that generates long explanations, long summaries, long emails, or verbose JSON, you are paying for that verbosity.

This is why “make it helpful and detailed” is not a neutral product choice. It is a cost choice.

For most production systems, you should assume:

- Inputs are relatively cheap
- Outputs dominate the bill
- Your margins live or die on: output length, retries, and how often you call the large model

**The unit you should budget in: cost per job**

Token pricing is useful, but it does not map cleanly to business reality.

Your CFO does not want to hear “we spent 90 million tokens last month.” They want to hear:

- Cost per invoice processed
- Cost per support ticket resolved
- Cost per sales email drafted
- Cost per month-end close checklist

So the unit you budget in is **cost per job.**

A “job” is the full end-to-end workflow outcome the business cares about.

Examples:

- “Extract an invoice, validate it, propose GL coding”
- “Draft a compliant outreach email, personalize it, produce a short follow-up”
- “Summarize a meeting, extract action items, update CRM notes”

When you define your system this way, you can price your product, set usage limits, and measure margins.

**A real anchor: invoice processing costs humans real money**

In accounts payable, the benchmark matters because it tells you what you can charge.

Manual invoice processing is commonly cited in the **$10 to $15 per invoice **range, and many sources put it higher in fully manual environments. Automation products often push this down to low single digits per invoice.

That gap is your product wedge:

- If manual processing is $10 to $15 per invoice
- And you can get your AI workflow to around cents to a few dollars per invoice
- Your gross margin can be excellent even at SMB pricing

That is why AP invoice processing is such a good “agentic” use case: it has a clean unit (invoice), high volume, and painful labor costs.

## **My cost breakdown: what actually drives spend in agentic workflows**

In practice, LLM spend comes from five buckets:

**Context size:**how much you send into the model**Output size**: how much text or JSON the model generates**Number of calls**: how many times you hit an LLM per job**Retries and fallbacks**: how often you re-run because parsing failed or confidence was low- Model tier selection: how often you call the premium model vs the cheap model

If you control those five things, you control your margins.

**The three-tier model strategy (cheap, good, premium)**

A mistake I see: teams pick one frontier model and use it for everything.

That is like paying a senior staff engineer to rename files and format JSON.

For production, you want a three-tier strategy:

**Tier 1: Cheap model for high-volume, low-risk**

**Use this for:**

- Formatting
- Light extraction when OCR is clean
- Summarization with strict length caps
- Classification

Goal: keep your unit costs low for the bulk of traffic.

**Tier 2: Mid-tier model for most real work**

**Use this for:**

- Core extraction
- Reasonable reasoning tasks
- Drafting short customer-facing text

Goal: the “default engine.”

**Tier 3: Premium model for exceptions and hard cases**

**Use this for:**

- Weird invoices
- Edge cases
- High-value customers
- Anything where failure is expensive

Goal: premium model usage should be a small percentage of total calls.

If you do this right, premium quality is available, but it is not burning your margins all day.

**The budgeting worksheet: estimate cost per job**

Here is the simplest version of the math.

You need four inputs:

- Average input tokens per call
- Average output tokens per call
- Number of calls per job
- Pricing per million tokens (input and output)

Then:

- Input cost per call = (input_tokens / 1,000,000) × input_price
- - Output cost per call = (output_tokens / 1,000,000) × output_price
- - Cost per job = (input_cost + output_cost) × calls_per_job

That is the whole model.

Now the part most teams forget: add overhead.

**Add a “waste factor” for retries**

In production, you will have:

- OCR glitches
- Vendor templates that break extraction
- JSON parsing failures
- Low-confidence outputs

If you do not budget for retries, your real spend will surprise you.

A practical approach:

- Early stage: assume **1.2x** waste factor
- After hardening: aim for **1.05x**

So:

**Real cost per job = estimated_cost_per_job × waste_factor**

**Example 1: invoice extraction and coding**

Let’s model a typical invoice job:

- OCR is handled outside the LLM (still a cost, but separate)
- LLM does extraction to structured JSON
- LLM does a coding suggestion step using a short list of candidate accounts

A conservative model:

- 2 LLM calls per invoice
- Each call: 2,000 input tokens, 500 output tokens

Total per invoice:

- Input tokens: 4,000
- Output tokens: 1,000

Now plug in your model pricing.

The point is not the exact rate. The point is what moves the number.

If you double output length, your cost can double.

If you move from mid-tier to premium, your cost can jump an order of magnitude.

**The real lesson**

**If you want predictable margins, the workflow should be designed to:**

- Keep outputs compact
- Use strict JSON schemas
- Avoid long explanations in the core path
- Route premium models only on exceptions

**Example 2: an AI agent that drafts sales emails**

Sales email drafting looks cheap until you scale it.

If your agent:

- Pulls context from CRM
- Summarizes the company
- Generates an email
- Generates two follow-ups

That can easily be 4 to 6 LLM calls.

And the output size is huge if you let it be.

This is where cost discipline matters:

- Cap output length
- - Generate one email, not three variants
- - Use templates and fill variables, do not “freewrite” everything

A good agent system feels personal without being verbose.

**Example 3: running a 14-agent AI workforce for $8/day**

This is our real setup at Kloudedge Apex. 14 agents running on scheduled crons, each handling a specific function: sales research, outreach, content writing, infrastructure monitoring, pipeline management.

The cost math:

- Most agents run 1–3 times per day
- Average session: 30k-180k tokens (input heavy, output lean)
- We use mid-tier models as default, premium only for complex reasoning
- Total daily LLM spend: approximately $8

That is $240/month for a full autonomous workforce. Compare that to one junior SDR at $5,000/month who can only do one of these jobs.

The key cost levers:

- Strict output schemas (agents return structured JSON, not essays)
- Shared state file (agents read each other’s work instead of re-researching)
- Cron-based scheduling (no idle compute, agents only run when needed)
- Tiered model routing (classification tasks get the cheap model, proposal writing gets premium)

**The hidden bill: long context is not free**

Agentic workflows love context.

They want:

- The whole PDF
- The whole email thread
- The whole ticket history
- The whole policy doc
- The whole codebase

That is usually unnecessary.

In production, you win by being ruthless about context:

- Summarize once, reuse summaries
- Retrieve only top-k relevant chunks
- Use embeddings for recall, not huge prompts
- Cache intermediate artifacts

In other words: **You** **do not pay for intelligence, you pay for tokens.**

**Where RAG actually saves money**

People talk about RAG as an accuracy tool.

It is also a cost tool.

If you retrieve the 10 paragraphs you need instead of pasting 40 pages, you are saving tokens and making the model more reliable.

This is why we lean on:

- PostgreSQL + pgvector embeddings
- Short candidate lists for coding suggestions
- Structured outputs instead of open-ended prose

RAG is not just “better answers.” It is “cheaper answers.”

**The reliability rule: deterministic checks are cheaper than retries**

Every retry is cost.

So you want deterministic checks around the model:

- Validate JSON against a schema
- Verify totals (subtotal + tax = total)
- Validate dates
- Validate vendor names against known vendors

When the checks fail, do not blindly retry.

Route it:

- Ask for a missing field
- Escalate to human review
- Or fall back to a stronger model

This saves money and improves UX.

**A practical KPI: LLM cost as a percentage of revenue**

For most SaaS products, compute is not the biggest line item.

The question is: does it stay sane as you grow?

A simple KPI I like:

**LLM Cost / Revenue**

If you are charging per invoice, you can compute this cleanly.

Example logic:

- If you charge $2,000/month
- Customer processes 2,000 invoices/month
- You have $0.15/invoice in AI cost
- That is $300/month in LLM cost

That is 15% of revenue.

Then add OCR, storage, and infrastructure. If your total COGS stays under 30 to 40%, you have a very healthy SaaS.

And if your LLM cost starts creeping up, the levers are clear:

- Reduce outputs
- Reduce calls
- Improve first-pass accuracy
- Push more work to the cheap model

**The takeaway: agentic AI is a margin discipline game**

“Agents” are not magic. They are software systems that call models.

If you want production systems that last, you need two things:

**Engineering discipline**: routing, validation, caching, retrieval**Economic discipline**: cost per job, tiering strategy, waste factor

If you do those two things, you can build systems that are both powerful and profitable.

That is the real unlock.
