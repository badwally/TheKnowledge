Do S&P500 Options Increase Market Volatility?
Evidence from 0DTEs
Greg Adams Chukwuma Dim
The Wharton School George Washington University

Abstract
The recent surge in trading of S&P500 zero-days-to-expiration options (0DTEs) raises con-
cerns that market makers’ hedging activities can spill over from the option market, desta-
bilizing the aggregate stock market. On the contrary, we show that the presence of 0DTEs
dampens market volatility through a shift in market makers’ hedging needs. Importantly,
this shift occurs not due to trading 0DTEs on their expiration day but due to positions
in longer-dated options accumulated earlier and becoming 0DTEs. Intraday evidence also
supports the causal hedging mechanism: intraday variation in market makers’ hedging needs
predicts stronger order-flow reversals, lower momentum returns, and lower volatility.

1. Introduction
In the last five years, the trading volume in SPX options linked to the S&P500 index has
more than doubled, largely due to trading of options with zero-day-to-expiration (0DTEs).1
0DTE prices are far more sensitive than those of longer-dated options to changes in the
S&P500 index. Their trading volume continues to grow and now accounts for more than
half of the total SPX trading volume.2 As a result, investors and regulators have raised
concerns about potential spillovers and feedback e!ects between the S&P500 option and
equity markets. A central question in this debate is whether option market makers generate
large order flows in the S&P500 market to hedge their 0DTE positions and can thus amplify
volatility or even destabilize the index.
We provide compelling evidence that the presence of 0DTEs dampens the S&P500 in-
dex volatility, on average, and that the market makers’ hedging needs underpin this e!ect.
The results highlight the importance of specialized intermediaries in integrating option and
equity markets (Arrow, 1964). It is remarkable that this role can lead to a reduction in
aggregate volatility in today’s S&P500 market despite the potential limits to the capacity of
intermediaries (Grossman and Miller, 1988) or the presence of noise traders (Stein, 1987).
We o!er causal evidence linking 0DTEs to the market makers’ hedging needs and the
underlying market volatility. First, we document that the volatility of S&P500 index re-
turns is lower by 60 annualized basis points (bps) on days when 0DTE trading is present, on
average. We identify this e!ect using exogenous variation in the presence of 0DTEs on Tues-
days and Thursdays before May 2022. The di!erence between the volatility across weekdays
disappears once 0DTE options start trading every business day in 2022.3

2. The Impact of 0DTE trading on the Index Volatility
This section establishes the treatment e!ect on the S&P500 index volatility due to the
presence of 0DTE trading. We discuss the expiration schedule of SPX options, explain the
identification strategy, define intraday realized volatility measures, and then provide the key
empirical tests and robustness checks.
2.1. Introductions of 0DTE
SPX options are plain European contracts that reference the S&P500 index and trade
on the Cboe Exchange. The 0DTEs belong to a large subset of SPX options that are
settled when the market closes (PM settlement). The options in this group are listed using
a weekly, end-of-month, or end-of-quarter maturity schedule. However, all contracts in this
subset naturally turn into 0DTEs on the expiration day. Other SPX options are settled
when the market opens (AM settlement): they cannot become 0DTEs. In the following, we
use the non-0DTE label for all SPX options that are not 0DTEs on a given trading day.
0DTE options became available in 2005 when the Cboe introduced weekly contracts
expiring on Friday with PM settlement. This allowed investors to trade index options during
the expiration day, thus leading to the emergence of 0DTE options trading. Other SPX
options with PM settlement were subsequently introduced in a staggered way over time.
Contracts that expire on the last business day of each quarter (SPX Quarterly) and on
the last business day of each month (SPX end-of-month) were introduced in 2006 and 2014,
respectively. Contracts that expire every week on Mondays and Wednesdays were introduced
in 2016. Lastly, contracts that expire every week at the end of the day on Tuesdays and
Thursdays were introduced in April and May 2022, respectively. The Cboe symbol root
SPXW now represents all the index contracts with PM settlement.

2.2. Identification Strategy
Our identification strategy rests on the fact that, between 2016 and April 2022, 0DTE
options regularly expired on Mondays, Wednesdays, and Fridays, but also sometimes expired
on specific Tuesdays or Thursdays. These latter exceptions occurred when the end-of-month
and end-of-quarter contracts expired on one of these two weekdays, or when a business
holiday occurred on a Monday, Wednesday, or Friday, in which case the Cboe moved the
expiration date to the neighboring business day.6 These calendar-based rules generated
plausibly exogenous variations in the presence of 0DTE trading on Tuesdays and Thursdays.

2.3. Intraday Volatility Measures
We calculate the S&P500 realized volatility for every 10-minute interval between 9:30
am and 4:00 pm. Using 10-minute intervals matches the highest frequency at which we can
compute the intraday market makers’ hedging needs with the data available to us from the
intraday Cboe Open-Close Summary. We check that our results are similar when computing
realized volatility with data from the S&P500 cash index, the S&P500 SPY ETF, and the
S&P500 E-mini futures. For brevity, we rely on E-mini futures data throughout most of the
paper. The SPX contracts settle using the cash index level, but this index is not a traded
security. By contrast, the S&P500 E-mini contract is highly liquid and commonly used for
hedging SPX options.
We sample the 1-minute S&P500 returns using the close prices for the cash index, or
the mid-quote for the SPY ETF and the E-Mini futures from DTN IQFeed. We use the
E-mini future contract with the shortest maturity but switch to the next contract before it
reaches one week to expiration. We construct the Realized Vol measure as the square root
of the sum of squared one-minute log returns in an intraday 10-minute interval. We scale
this 10-minute volatility measure to annualized terms by assuming 252 trading days per year
and 39 intervals per trading day.

3. The Impact of 0DTE Trading on Market Makers’ Hedging
We use the Cboe Open-Close Volume Summary data to identify positions held by market
makers so that we can measure their hedging needs within the day. This section summarizes
the mechanics of delta hedging by option market makers and details how we measure their
hedging needs based on this framework. In doing so, we distinguish and discuss two sources
of market makers’ hedging needs—the net delta and the net gamma.
Empirically, using the exogenous variations in 0DTE trading, as before, we find that the
presence of the 0DTE contracts substantially changes market makers’ hedging needs. The
presence of 0DTE trading causes net gamma hedging needs to increase in the direction that
can explain the lower index volatility on expiration days that we documented in the previous
section.

3.9. Discussion
Summarizing the evidence so far, we document four causal facts due to the presence
of 0DTE trading: (i) the S&P500 intraday realized volatility is lower on days when 0DTE
contracts are present, (ii) market makers have larger net gamma on these expiration days,
(iii) this larger exposure comes from 0DTE net gamma present at the start of the expiration
day and due to pre-existing option positions, and (iv) market makers maintain a positive
portfolio net gamma throughout the 0DTE expiration day.
Overall, the evidence suggests that the treatment e!ect of 0DTE trading on the market
makers’ hedging needs can explain the average net reduction in index volatility that we
document in Table 1 for our sample period. We cannot directly check whether the hedging
needs drive the treatment e!ect, since we cannot observe the transactions of option market
makers on the S&P500 futures exchange. To address this gap, Section 4 provides indirect
evidence linking the intraday market makers’ hedging needs to the intraday index volatility,
order flow reversals, and momentum returns in the E-mini futures market.

4.3. Intraday Hedging and Order Flow in the E-mini Futures
This section leverages data about intraday order flows in the E-mini futures market. The
E-mini contract is extremely liquid and one of the primary instruments that market makers
can use to hedge SPX options. We document that a higher market makers’ net gamma
predicts a stronger order flow reversal e!ect within the day, consistent with the direction of
trading suggested by the analysis of hedging needs. The evidence supports our hypothesis
that the attenuation in index volatility is due to market makers’ hedging activity.
We measure the order flow within each 10-minute interval to match the frequency of the
option open-close volume data. We obtain intraday trades and quotes at the top of the order
book data from the Chicago Mercantile Exchange. Since trades are executed at the bid or
ask price, we can identify each trade as a buy or a sell order. We can then cumulate the buy
orders minus the sell orders within each 10-minute interval, which we use to construct two
measures of order flows.
The first measure Order Flow has in the numerator the number of contracts that traded
following buy orders minus the number that traded following sell orders, multiplied by 50,
thenumberofsharesrepresentedbyonefuturescontract. Thedenominator M isthenumber

Contrary to these concerns, we show that availability of 0DTEs actually reduces the index
volatility. We also document the economic mechanism behind such volatility attenuation:
the presence of 0DTE trading significantly increases the hedging needs of market makers
in the direction that requires market makers to trade against the index price movements,
thereby acting as a liquidity provider and dampening the index volatility on the underlying
index market. The evidence provides a baseline and contributes to the active debate between
investors, regulators, and the Cboe exchange around the potential spillover from the option
market to the S&P500 due to the growth in 0DTE trading.
Our results also raise important questions for future research. We find that the market
makers’ net gamma increases on 0DTE trading days because of transactions in non-0DTE
options in earlier days. This points toward a dynamic management of hedging needs over
multiple days beyond the traditional view of a static or passive inventory management by
intermediaries. We also conclude that the intermediates acting in the option market have
a substantial price impact on the aggregate stock market. While the dampening e!ect on
volatility appears beneficial to investors on average, the variations in hedging needs may
introduce a distinct source of aggregate risk with further asset pricing implications for the
stock and option markets.
