The Dolby Channel
The implementation of Dolby technologies on an increasing array of device platforms offers a
new opportunity to engage creators, build more useful and engaging demos, and experiment
with new applications of Dolby IP. This document offers an overview of the drivers for a new
Dolby Channel – a first-party content service that delivers content created in Dolby formats to
best-in-class applications developed internally and deployed to Dolby-enabled device
platforms. More than a marketing vehicle, this Channel would allow Dolby to accelerate
technical experimentation, develop significant internal application engineering expertise, and
tie our ecosystem tools with consumer-facing content offerings. The result will be a more
efficient and effective approach to accelerating our growth in the eMedia space.
Platform Dependency
Dolby’s rate of innovation in the creation of new technologies and experiences outpaces the
ability to bring these innovations to market. In many cases, this is due to a weighted
dependence on adoption by large scale content providers in Hollywood. These content
providers increasingly position Dolby’s innovations as part of the feature set of content
products that have higher cost to consumers, are made available to content distributors with
higher licensing costs and defined business model constraints, and are subject to geographic
constraints on distribution. When Dolby enters the market with a new technology such as Dolby
Atmos or Dolby Vision or AC4 but the product faces significant limitation on content availability
or service adoption, the company’s ability to fully capture value is, at best, limited. These
platform and content dependencies impart an economic cost on the company.
Ecosystem Tools
Dolby’s Emedia business group and marketing organization also make a considerable
investment in both ecosystem testing tools (e.g. DISC and OnDel) as well as independent
demonstration applications for new technologies or experiences. Tools are used to test
implementation of Dolby technologies according to internal and industry best practices, as well
as provide reference code and documentation to ecosystem partners implementing Dolby
Audio or Vision into their product or service. These tools span the end-to-end ecosystem chain,
from content creation (RMU) and encoding (DMG) to playback (DRP, DISC reference player). For
external marketing, demo apps are developed on a per-platform, per-experience basis and
demonstrate an ongoing source of incremental operating (and at times capital) costs associated
with design, engineering, deployment, maintenance, etc. Today, this includes demos like the
DAA Demo, the Manzana demo, a pending Apple TV demo, the Dolby Audio Experience demo
for the web, proof-of-concept demos like that for personalized audio for the NBA, as well as
other efforts including those focused on the new market for VR using Dolby formats. To support
these and other demos, we often incur significant cost licensing content for public and private
demonstration.

Engaging Creators
In an effort to broaden awareness and increase the utility of Dolby’s audio and video
technologies in the marketplace, the eMedia BG has also prioritized focus on made-for-web and
user-generated content creators and services. This includes: strategic engagements with
YouTube in an effort to improve the value proposition for our offering to mobile device OEMs;
programmatic outreach to applications developers; engagement with so-called premium made-
for-web content creators and “MCNs” like Maker Studios and Fullscreen Media in alignment
with the Dolby Institute; and, early stage experimentation with first-party app creation through
programs like “Narrate” in order to develop new technical expertise and identify new areas for
revenue growth. As part of these efforts, Dolby has also engaged in business development
efforts with video technology partners such as Adobe to ensure that tools like Adobe Premiere
and Adobe Primetime include the ability to capture and publish content in Dolby formats. To
date, there are not yet any content platforms to which creators might publish stories created
using Dolby tools and technologies. In addition, as an ingredient technology provider and not a
content service, Dolby is often at arms length from real-world feedback on the utility of our
efforts in the form of direct user engagement data.
Channel Solution
Platform dependencies, content dependencies, tool development, developer outreach,
technology adoption, demo costs, experiments with new technical avenues: to create and
capture value in the rapidly changing emedia marketplace, there are many dependent and
independent variables in play, often each carrying some level of cost. One response to this
problem set is to assert more control over the use of our technology through development of a
first-party content service. Rather than license content, Dolby would weave together our efforts
at developer outreach, made-for-web creator engagement, and the Dolby Institute to create
and publish a Dolby Channel to device platforms implementing our technology. Creators would
be offered access to resources to support development of new content using best-in-class
implementations of both Dolby and partner technology. In exchange for perpetual
demonstration rights, Dolby would master, publish and distribute this content to Dolby Channel
applications, as well as support marketing and discovery of this content through mechanisms
such as the Dolby Institute.
Platform Development
Such an approach is not without precedent. For example, GoPro has aligned with film schools to
engage a new generation of media creators to use their camera technologies, and Adobe has an
outreach program to foster use of their software tools. However, unlike a basic marketing
outreach program, Dolby’s approach here would differ. In creating a series of applications, we
would develop deep technical expertise in app development and publishing, experience in
developing and managing a content service, and generate data on consumption patterns
associated with Dolby content formats. In addition, we would develop a platform upon which
we could actively prototype new uses for our early stage technology, manage a media cloud

service, and experiment with software-based implementations of our technology without
complicating existing business or licensing arrangements. While there is marketing benefit to
these efforts, the core value is this: by developing deep first-party expertise in engineering,
deploying, and managing new cloud-based applications of our technology we will dramatically
increase the odds that we identify new areas for product development and associated revenue
growth. That we might do so in a way that creates immediate efficiency in marketing and
ecosystem development costs may result in a cost-neutral program that will accelerate
innovation.
