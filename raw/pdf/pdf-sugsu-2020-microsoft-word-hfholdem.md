Hedge Fund Hold ’em*
Yan Lu#, Sandra Mortal^ and Sugata Ray+
Abstract
We find that hedge fund managers who do well in poker tournaments have significantly better
fund performance. This effect is stronger for tournaments with more entrants, larger buy-ins, larger
cash prizes and for managers who placed in higher positions or who win in multiple tournaments,
suggesting poker skills are correlated with fund management skills. After a manager wins a poker
tournament, net flows to the manager’s fund increase significantly. These increases are
concentrated in cases where there is media coverage of the manager’s poker playing, when the
tournament win is bigger, and for more prominent tournaments (e.g. the World Series of Poker).
Along with higher net flows, fund alpha also decreases significantly following the tournament win
compared to a sample of matched peers, especially in cases where net inflows are highest,
suggesting decreasing returns to scale or distractions correlated with higher net inflows erode the
informativeness of the poker win signal. Given this, hedge fund investors would be better off
investing in an otherwise similar manager without poker tournament success.
Keywords: Poker; Hedge funds; Investor flows; Alpha erosion
JEL Classification: G11; G12; G14; G23

1. Introduction
Academic studies have generally cast managers’ personal pursuits as sources of value
destruction. Whether it is distraction arising from executives’ excessive golfing (Biggerstaff,
Cicero, & Puckett, 2017) and fund managers’ romantic relationships (Lu, Ray, & Teo, 2016); or
managers’ hobbies, purchases, and indiscretions correlated with excessive risk taking and fraud
(Brown, Lu, Ray, & Teo, 2018, Cain & McKeon, 2016, Griffin, Kruger, & Maturana, 2019), the
documented evidence suggests personal pursuits are largely value destroying for shareholders and
investors.1 Yet, at odds with this, at least in the investment management context, is proficiency in
one particular personal pursuit: poker playing. Poker skills are widely believed to be correlated
with success in investment and trading.2 David Einhorn, a fund manager, likens poker and
investing, stating both “are games of incomplete information…. You have a certain set of facts
and you are looking for situations where you have an edge…” Another fund manager, Steve
Cohen, attributes poker as “the biggest determinant in [his] learning to take risks.”3 On the other
hand, anecdotally, the distraction and shirking noted in the academic literature regarding personal
pursuits in general has also been associated with card-playing. James Cayne, CEO of Bear Stearns,

Finally, a natural question arising from these results is how do these hedge fund investors
fare after poker tournament cashes? To answer this, we use a matched sample to examine how the
performance of funds is affected by poker tournament cashes. Our results suggest that
performance, measured either as raw returns or seven factor alphas, decreases significantly after
cashes, and such funds underperform their matched peers to the tune of 40-70bp a month. This
decrease in performance, along with the increased net inflows to these funds, suggest that this may
be a function of the decreasing returns to scale highlighted in Berk & Green, 2004. Given the
magnitude of the decline in performance, we also entertain the possibility that distraction may be
driving some of the relatively poor performance after tournament wins. We explore further by
looking at the link between underperformance and net inflows as well as measures of fund activity,
such as active share and index holdings after tournament wins.

2. Literature Review
Our study contributes to several areas of the academic literature. Most directly, our study
adds to the growing literature on using personal characteristics to gain additional information on
manager performance. While this literature is broad, and examines both corporate and fund
managers, our study is most closely linked to studies examining personal pursuits of managers.
For example, Biggerstaff, Cicero, & Puckett, 2017, examine CEO golfing to measure leisure
consumption and find evidence consistent with shirking behavior. To our knowledge, we are the
first to study potential positive spillovers between skills honed through a leisure activity and
professional success. We do note that Agarwal, Qin, Sing, & Zhang, 2018 show positive impact
of golfing on real estate developers bidding on land prices in Singapore, but they attribute these

There is a large literature examining the list of potential alternative signals, including some

innate levels of ambitiousness or competitive drive (Judge & Kammeyer-Mueller, 2012).14
However, to the extent that fund manager IQ, ambition, and competitive drive are not immediately

we believe results documented in this paper are of value to investors. Also related, in the context
of CEO performance, Malmendier & Tate, 2009 show that distractions after CEOs win awards
lead to poorer firm performance. This is similar to our finding of poorer performance after a
personal poker tournament win. However, while the channel of value destruction in Malmendier

documented in Schwarz & Sun, 2018. In our case, hedge fund investors are learning fast in

3. Data and summary statistics
3.1 Sample Construction
We hand collect data on hedge fund managers poker wins from The Hendon Mob website,
the largest live poker database.15 We start with a list of all manager names from the Lipper TASS
database. Our data on poker tournament results is from the Hendon Mob database. The database,
available at https://pokerdb.thehendonmob.com/, describes itself as “the most comprehensive
database of poker players, venues, festivals, events and results.” It allows us to search for hedge
fund managers by name, and returns any tournaments in which the manager won a cash prize,
along with details of the tournament and some additional information on the manager (including
manager’s city and state, and if available, a picture).
Our sample starts in 2001, the first year for which we have data in the Hendon Mob
database, and ends in 2015, the last year for which we have data from Lipper TASS database. From
TASS, we obtain a list of all US-based funds. We exclude funds if main strategy is ‘fund of funds’,
and if the manager name is not available. After these filters, we begin with a sample of 4,748 funds
managed by 3,377 managers. We search for our sample of hedge fund managers from TASS on
Hendon Mob, and verify our matches using several cross-checks. First, if there is a single match
returned, we check the location to see if the match in Hendon Mob is located near the fund address
in TASS. If not, we check to see if there are any references to the fund manager having a residence

3.1.1 A note on the databases used
The two central databases used in our study, TASS and the Hendon Mob have some
potential drawbacks. We detail them below and note some of the steps we take to mitigate any
biases. TASS has some known biases such as backfill bias, survivorship bias and return smoothing
bias. Since well-performing funds have a strong incentive to list and attract capital, backfilled
returns are higher than non-backfilled returns (see Bhardwaj, Gorton, and Rouwenhorst, 2014). To
mitigate this, we remove all returns prior to the fund listing date, which is specified in TASS
database. To mitigate survivorship bias that failed funds are likely to have poorer returns, our
10
Electronic copy available at: https://ssrn.com/abstract=3392130

analyses include both live and dead funds. Another known issue in TASS is funds may smooth
returns, and the resulting serial correlation in fund returns could potentially inflate some of the test
statistics. In unpresented robustness analysis, we repeat out baseline analysis following the
algorithm of Getmansky, Lo and Makarov, 2004, and find our inferences don’t change after
adjusting for serial correlation in fund returns.
The Hendon Mob database contains data on entrants who won cash prizes in tournaments,
as reported by tournament directors and casinos. For data from a tournament to be included, The
Hendon Mob requires that all entrants who cashed, along with position, prize, and tournament
details are reported. Thus, fund managers cannot simply unilaterally report their favorable finishes
to the Hendon Mob. The Hendon Mob, unfortunately, does not have or report data for all entrants,
just the entrants who cashed. Thus, it is possible that managers have entered more tournaments
than we observe (e.g. if they do not cash in them). This could potentially lead to some spurious
inferences. For example, if we find that managers who cash in tournaments do well in their funds,
it may be a result of managers who do well having time and money to enter many tournaments and
winning some by luck. We continue by describing the dataset and will address this concern later
in our paper along with some tests we conduct to mitigate these concerns.
Finally, given that we use name matching (with locational and other cross-checks) to match
TASS managers to poker records in the Hendon Mob, it is possible that the matching procedure
may generate some issues. One of the potential issues with name matching is that unique names
are more likely to be matched than common names. Given unique names are more likely for
managers with non-US backgrounds, and there are studies documenting significantly different
dynamics for such managers (see Kumar, Niessen-Ruenzi, and Spalt, 2015) this may lead to
systematic biases in our final dataset. We explore the implications of this in the Alternative

Explanations section of the paper. Another issue with the matching could be that we miss situations
where managers play under a nickname, or perhaps we generate false matches in cases where we
successfully match a manager based on name and location, but the match is incorrect. These
situations will add noise to the final analysis and will likely reduce the statistical significance of
our results. To the extent our analysis generates significant results, the “true” results are likely to
be stronger than those we document as these sources of noise will have weakened our observed
results.

Table 1 presents summary statistics. Panel A presents data on fund characteristics and
performance sorted by whether or not the hedge fund manager won a poker tournament. We term
funds with managers who have cashed in at least one poker tournament as poker funds and the
remaining as non-poker funds. Non-poker funds include funds whose managers have never played
in a tournament or have played but never cashed (i.e. managers for whom we do not observe any
tournament cashes in the Hendon Mob database). Our dataset contains 3,017 funds whose
managers did not win any poker tournaments and 220 funds whose managers won at least one
poker tournament. Most fund characteristics are identical across poker and non-poker funds,
suggesting that the sample of funds whose managers have won in poker tournaments is
representative of a typical fund. For example, the average management fee for poker funds and
non-poker funds is 1.44% and 1.43% respectively, the difference of 0.01% is statistically
insignificant. The average incentive fee is 14.33% and 14.70% for poker funds and non-poker
funds respectively, and the difference of 0.37% is also statistically insignificant.
There are also some notable differences in fund characteristics across the two groups of
managers. Poker funds have a longer longevity: the average age for poker funds over our sample

4.1 Reverse causation
One possible concern is that the positive relation between poker tournament cashes and
fund performance we document earlier is driven by reverse causation. Unfortunately, since we are
only able to observe events where the poker manager won a tournament, and we do not know how
many times a person entered a tournament, it is possible that cashing in a poker tournament is not
the result of skill at poker. Perhaps managers of well performing funds play more poker
tournaments and win some by luck, as opposed to our interpretation that fund managers who win
poker tournaments perform well. To mitigate this concern, we exploit the fact that among the
tournament cashes we observe, we still have a distribution. On average, tournaments have the top
10% of entrants cashing, and if it were simply the case that managers who did well played more
tournaments, and by luck cashed in some of them, these managers would be equally likely to finish
at any percentile in the top 10%. In other words, a manager who cashed closer to the top should
perform no differently than a manager who barely cashed. With that in mind, we compare the
performance of managers with better than median and worse than median placements. We present
results in the last column of Table 3. We continue to find higher returns, alphas and fund flows for

4.2 Alternative measures of skill
In addition to proxying for manager skill using returns and alphas, for robustness we
estimate several alternative measures of skill. Value added (in million dollars), follows Berk and
Binsbergen, 2015, it is the mean of the product of gross abnormal returns and fund’s assets under
management. We estimate gross abnormal returns monthly with a four-factor model using the
factors identified in Fama and French, 1992, and Carhart, 1997. It measures the value that hedge
funds extract from capital markets each month, and accounts for the well documented inverse
relation between scale and alpha, that is unrelated to investment skill. Return gap, follows
Kacperczyk, Sialm and Zheng, 2008, it is the difference between the reported return of a fund to
their investors and the return of a hypothetical buy and hold portfolio that invests in the previously
disclosed holdings after adjusting for reported expense ratios. It measures the impact of
unobserved actions on fund returns.
Strategy distinctiveness index (SDI), follows Sun, Wang, and Zheng, 2012, it is one minus
the sample correlation of a fund's return with the average return of all funds belonging to the same
style. It measures the fund’s investment strategy uniqueness. This measure is based on the premise
that only unique investment strategies are likely to generate superior performance. Reliance on
public information (RPI), follows Kacperczyk and Seru, 2007, is the R-square of the regression of
percentage changes in its portfolio holdings on changes in analysts’ past recommendations. This
18

measure is inversely related to skill, and captures the degree to which a fund managers rely on
public information. This measure is based on the notion that only private information should
generate superior returns.

Given the magnitude of the underperformance, we also consider whether distraction or
mean reversion may be driving our results. While it is true that managers who win poker
tournaments have done well recently before winning the poker tournament (see Table 2, Columns
3 and 4), our matched sample setting accounts for this by matching on recent performance (or in
the case of propensity score matching, matching based on the determinants from Table 2). Thus,
any mean reversion due to recent good performance should also be observed in our control sample.
However, our results document that performance deterioration for the poker-winning funds is
significantly greater than that for the control sample, making it unlikely that mean reversion is
driving our results.
What about distraction? Is it possible that managers start playing poker and get distracted
from fund duties, leading to the anemic performance post-tournament win? Given the tight link
between underperformance and inflows, it seems unlikely that this underperformance is driven by
overall distraction and shirking. However, it may be possible that networking and marketing efforts

using poker success as a platform may lead to distractions that are correlated with flows and would
similarly lead to poorer performance post-win, where the poor performance goes hand-in-hand
with increased flows. Such a channel would be indistinguishable from and, in our view, is likely
to co-exist with the diseconomies of scale conceptualized in Berk and Green, 2004. Put another
way, in addition to decreased alpha due to a lack of alpha generating ideas when there are
additional flows, the act of garnering the added flows and administering their intake may lead to
distractions for the manager and reduce alpha as well. Abuzov, 2020, documents a related
phenomenon in the venture capital context, where venture capitalists who get distracted by being
involved in raising additional capital for and/or exiting from existing projects make poorer
investments during this period of distraction, which in turn lead to poorer returns down the road.
One of the implications of Berk and Green, 2004, is that added flows would dampen alphas
towards zero, but are not going to lead to negative alphas, as we find in some of our analyses.
While we note the negative alphas are not statistically significant, we also note it is possible that
the distractions discussed above and transaction costs in deploying the additional capital (as
documented in Lou, 2012) may lead to further reduction in alphas and the negative point estimates
we observe.

distractions constrain hedge funds’ ability to place active bets, we might expect hedge funds to
place fewer active bets relative to the size of the fund. Extant research has found that funds with
fewer active bets yield poorer performance in the future (see Cremers & Petajisto, 2009,
Kacperczyk, Sialm, & Zheng, 2005, and Titman & Tiu, 2011). Thus, the reason hedge funds

experience poor performance after poker cashes may be that it is difficult for managers to place

We compute three metrics that measure the extent of non-active bets placed: R-squared is
from a regression of fund excess returns on Fung & Hsieh, 2004 seven factors; 1-Active Share uses
active share as defined in Cremers & Petajisto, 2009 ; S&P 500 Ratio is the ratio of the number of
S&P 500 stocks bought in a quarter to the total number of new positions in the quarter. The latter
two measures are computed using quarterly holdings reported on 13-Fs, which results in a more
limited sample. These measures are estimated for one year before and one year after the poker
tournament win for treatment and control samples. The control sample is obtained by matching
each fund in the treatment group with another fund with the same investment style and the closest
value of the trading metric being considered for the year before poker tournaments.
Table 11 reports results for the full sample (Panel A) and for funds with change in flows
above and below median (Panels B and C respectively). The extent of non-active bets placed
increase after poker cashes. R-squared increases by 0.20 (p<0.01) from 0.30 in the before period,
1-Active Share increases by 0.03 (p<0.01) from 0.43, and S&P 500 Ratio increases by 0.04 (p<0.1)
from 0.27. Our results remain unchanged after accounting for possible trends in trading behavior
using the control sample. Panels B and C show that the change in non-active bets is related to fund
flows. Non-active bets increase the most when fund flows are above median. Our results suggest
that after poker tournament cashes, managers follow less active trading strategies because the
additional fund flows that follow the tournament cashes hamper managers’ ability to implement
their proprietary strategies.21

6. Alternate Explanations
In this section we consider several alternative explanations for our results. There are
several alternative, intuitive, explanations that may be driving some of the links between poker
and hedge fund management. First, we may expect hedge fund managers who play poker to have
a higher tolerance for risk, which in turn leads to better performance due to a positive risk-return
tradeoff. We find no evidence for this as funds managed by poker playing managers do not exhibit
higher return risk. On the other hand, we only observe winning results, so perhaps the riskier poker
playing managers do not cash in tournaments. More generally, perhaps hedge fund managers who
play, and do well in, poker tournaments have some different personal characteristics from other
managers. While we have noted it is possible that some unobservable characteristics such as IQ or
level of ambition may be driving both poker and fund success, we conduct a robustness test where
we include a slate of observable personal characteristics as controls. The personal characteristics
we use include sensation seeking and sensation avoiding flags from car purchase data, used in
Brown et al, 2018; manager age, and home purchase mortgage levels, used in Ling, Lu, Ray, 2020.
Our results (available in the Appendix in Table A1) remain robust after controlling for these
factors, however, we do note our list of controls is not exhaustive, and it is still possible that some
other fund or manager characteristics may be driving our results.22

than the pattern we observe in the panel analysis. Our data suggests that performance actually
decreases after tournament wins, suggesting it is unlikely that such information exchange is driving
our performance differentials.

7. Conclusions
Together, our findings suggest that skilled poker players are, on average, better fund
managers. Additionally, investors seem to be responsive to managers’ poker successes and
increase investment in fund managers who demonstrate poker skill by cashing in a poker
tournament. This may be the result of investors being cognizant of the link between poker skill
and fund performance, or it might be the result of managers using their poker success as a
marketing opportunity by networking at the poker tables, or giving interviews showcasing their
poker skills.
However, the signal coming from the poker tournament cash does not provide any
additional information over and above the past performance of the fund (which tends to be better,
consistent with poker and fund management skills being correlated) as such funds experience a
decline in performance after the poker cash. We interpret the decline in performance, together with
the increase in net flows, and the fact that the decline in performance is concentrated in funds
experiencing the largest net inflows as evidence consistent with decreasing returns to scale. It
appears that investors allocate additional capital into the funds of managers with poker tournament
cashes, which strains the funds’ capacities, reducing alpha. Put another way, in this case, investors
in hedge funds do not know when to hold’em.
