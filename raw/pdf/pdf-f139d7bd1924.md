The Damped Spring Report
“Shifts in growth, inflation, risk premium and positioning all lead to
opportunities in markets”
08/11/2024
Since the FOMC, BOJ Meeting and refunding announcement a classic
deleveraging move in assets prices has occurred. While the move has
been somewhat violent, we consider this to be a just another step along
the way of the process of already leveraged financial markets absorbing
huge and ongoing supply of assets. As the supply continues to weigh on
the private sector an occasional sharp deleveraging will occur. During the
deleveraging episode market pundits scurry to explain the most recent
move and predict how long it will last. The current popular theory is that a
tiny hike by the BOJ has resulted in the deleveraging of the Yen Carry
Trade (YCT). We agree that the YCT unwind is likely happening, however
we do not think it is causal or the sole deleveraging occurring. The camel
that is the global asset markets just temporarily shrugged under the
weight of supply. It’s back will break eventually. The deleveraging has a
long way to go. In this DSR we introduce our deleveraging framework and
review all the major deleveraging’s over the last 40 years to help place the
current circumstance in context. This deleveraging episode has these
features
• Broad but not systemic
• Financial institutions are not contributing to the deleveraging and
while they have ample liquidity to provide new leveraged buyers
financing, balance sheet is tight, and the cost of leverage is high
• The economy is not being impacted by the deleveraging and CB’s
financial stability or the jobs mandates are not threatened

Deleveraging framework
A deleveraging is an inelastic selling of risky and/or long-term assets and a similar
inelastic buying of short-term risk-free assets. Unlevered investors sell assets and
as they must reinvest cash create inelastic demand for short term treasuries.
Levered investors are forced to sell assets to fund margin calls or to stop out risk
positions. Levered unwinds results in demand for short term assets, for collateral
obligations for margin calls, and from leverage providers as loans are paid back.
During such periods the inelastic supply of assets and demand for short term
treasuries meets the other side of such flow which often becomes highly elastic
resulting in large price dislocations. The events of the last few weeks have followed
this path. For investors it is important to frame the likely duration and intensity of
any deleveraging and the potential economic consequences and policymaker
responses if any. Our framework for understanding deleveraging episodes asks
these questions
• Who is deleveraging?
• A single investor
• a cohort of investors
• broad set of investors
• Systemic
• How are leverage providers involved?
• Are they able and willing to provide leverage?
• Are they themselves deleveraging?
• Does the episode threaten the real economy?
• Will growth and inflation be impacted?
• Is Financial Stability risk threatened?
• Will Policymakers react to the de-leveraging?
• Will CB’s cut rates, provide funding programs for leverage providers,
purchase assets?
• Will Fiscal act to offset economic consequences if any?
In this DSR I am dropping the “we” pronoun as I will be relating my first-hand
experience living through notable deleveraging episodes over the last 38 years of
my career. While my personal experience and understandings of what happened
are limited by my perceptions I will also rely on my framework and the
understandings I have gleaned over the decades studying these events and
working with others who have lived through these episodes and/or have studied
them in depth.
Common Market reactions to de-leveraging
The first signs of a de-leveraging are common to all episodes. A sell off in risky
assets and a sharp rally in short term bonds. As mentioned above the flow is the
driver of the price action. But other measures of market stress also move
coincident with broad asset changes. In the current deleveraging:

Equity implied volatility and high yield credit spreads spiked.

What’s next?
As can be seen on all of the above charts the peak market reaction occurred early
in the week and in particular the preopening on Sunday night and Monday Morning
and since then markets have calmed down somewhat. The reaction has been a
classic deleveraging and some deleveragings are short lived and recovery occurs

deleveraging with snapback rallies and occasional sharp declines. We are
not worried about financial institutions health. Based on what has
occurred so far, we do not expect an impact on the real economy, nor do
we expect the central banks to change policy. Lastly, we do not expect any
sort of fiscal action. We liken the current deleveraging to 1987 without the
drama of a crash, but with a more leveraged system and less room to ease
by policymakers.
Past de-leveragings
Overview
I have selected thirteen memorable deleveragings that span my career to examine
through our deleveraging framework. All had violent risky assets selloffs.

Assuming that markets are now in the midst of some sort of deleveraging which is
indicated by market action over the past three weeks it seems clear from our
review that in order to conclude that the deleveraging is over, one has to
believe that the deleveraging is by a few concentrated forced sellers, will
have no impact on the real economy and will be offset by strong central
bank action to address possible contagion from the concentrated
deleveraging if necessary. It is also clear that many deleveragings result in
difficulty for private sector banks and a recession which then is offset by significant
and broad policymaker action. Those episodes are also long lasting, and the
policymaker actions only occur after a significant drawdown in risky assets and a
weak economy. Given the circumstances of today’s deleveraging we consider the
1987 episodes to be most comparable. We certainly aren’t calling for an imminent
crash in the stock market for multiple reasons but mostly due to the safeguards put
in by the Brady Commission in 1988. A more prolonged and perhaps shallower
deleveraging is a real possibility.
In the following section I will describe in greater detail these 13 notable
deleveragings using my framework. I will also give my personal experiences
navigating those deleveragings and some of the important lessons that can only be
learned by experiencing these things firsthand. Each time was different, each
episode can never be fully explained despite meaningful study. Hopefully the
combination of first-hand experience, frameworks built over decades and
perspective will be useful.

1987 Stock Market Crash – Equity Event
I was not sitting in a trading seat when the stock market crashed. I was a
corporate finance analyst at Salomon Brothers and had started just over 15 months
prior to the crash. Nonetheless I have a unique perspective. Soon after the crash
I was assigned to the Presidential Task Force on Market Mechanisms, “The Brady
Commission”. Our job was to determine why the stock market had crashed and
recommend regulatory changes that could improve market function. The story of
that experience is here.
The 1987 Crash started with a peak in the US Equity market on August 25, 1987.
The drawdown from that peak to the low was 33.51% on December 4, 1987. It
took 599 days for the SPX to recover back to its peak.
The sellers during the whole drawdown were quite broad, however during the crash
itself the selling was dominated by two major institutional sellers. Those sellers
used a technique created by Leland, O’Brien and Rubinstein called portfolio
insurance. It is worth remembering that in 1987 index options were only 6 years
old. Because of low liquidity for institutional investors LOR created a hedging
technique that replicated the payoff of a purchased index put for their clients to
execute. That technique is exactly the same as a dealer who has sold a put to a
client would use to dynamically hedge their exposure. All that was necessary was a
liquid hedging market and a moderate realized volatility. Leading up to the peak in
August the stock market was up an astounding 39% for the year. Portfolio
insurance strategies when initiated start with a short futures position. As the
market rallies as it did all year the Portfolio Insurer would cover the short. It’s
likely that this was partly causal for the sizeable rally. As the market began to fall
in late August the PI’s began selling. Through the Friday before the crash the SPX
had fallen 16%. The Portfolio Insurer kept selling and its very clear from their
activities on 10/19 that they caused the extreme move that day. However, the
lead up to Black Monday was much broader and certainly had something to do with
a difficult economic situation and high valuations. The Brady Commission report
goes through all the details in depth. It’s worth a read.
Portfolio insurance is by definition a deleveraging. So, this episode clearly was a
deleveraging. However, the damage was largely contained to equity investors.
Banks were not impacted at all. While Salomon and other’s lost money that day in
equity businesses the massive rally in fixed income offset a lot of pain. Banks were
willing and able to provide credit to the real economy and to financial asset owners.
The Economy hardly missed a beat. GDP growth remained strong
6

Fed Chairman Greenspan notably cut interest rates immediately after the crash
which was extremely reassuring to markets at the time but with perspective the
cuts were not large. Just this week during the heat of the panic Professor Jeremy
Seigel called for an emergency cut of 75bp which would have been as large as the
whole cutting cycle during the crash. Perhaps Chairman Greenspan’s action which
has been famously described as the Fed Put is invoked prematurely given the 4%
Drawdown of the “Crash of August 5, 2024”.
The Deleveraging episode of 1987 was an equity markets event. It was
exacerbated by investors who hedged dynamically. Banks were fine.
Policymakers reacted modestly to an extreme move. Except for its
extreme nature this event is an excellent proxy for any potential
deleveraging today. Today Long only investors lever and delever with futures,
hedge via purchasing puts whose liquidity had massively improved but leaves the
dealers with “Portfolio Insurance” dynamic hedging to do in a downdraft. The big
difference is circuit breakers which were introduced based on recommendations
from the Brady Commission. These circuit breakers aren’t magic, and they won’t
keep markets from finding their equilibrium price, but they have been shown to
slow down markets during times of stress. If a broad deleveraging due to high
valuations and levered positions does unwind without hurting the economy or
necessitating a massive policymaker response a crash is unlikely. We think a
deleveraging of this type will be slower and choppier but eventually a
drawdown of 20% would not surprise us.

1989-1991 S&L Bailout, Drexel & Junk Bond Market – Credit event
By the late 80’s the long and troubled history of the Savings and Loan Industry was
ending with a government funded liquidation which cost over $160BN. At the same
time Drexel Burnham was defending itself from a slew of charges by the DOJ and
SEC regarding insider trading and other illegal activities. Many of Drexel’s clients
were S&L’s. Ultimately the pressure on Drexel caused the firm to declare
bankruptcy in February 1990. These two events resulted in a freezing of the credit
pipes for below investment grade borrowers as a major buyer became a forced
liquidator, and the bank that dominated the liquidity provision and intermediation
of the high yield was abruptly shut down.
Despite this clear contraction in credit which in retrospect presaged the recession in
1990, the late 80’s was a boom period. The economy was hot, and the Fed was
increasing rates. Leveraged Buyouts were happening almost every weekend. The
RJR takeover in particular demonstrated the animal spirits involved as a
competitive bidding process played out in 1989. Stock markets were volatile but
buoyant. On October 13, 1989, the United Airlines LBO by its unions collapsed and
the stock market fell 13% in two days. Nonetheless it quickly recovered only to
sell off again on the days around the Drexel Bankruptcy. Yet again in the face of
this wall of worry the market continued to climb. But finally, the tightening of
conditions impacted not only the markets, but real economic growth fell rapidly,
and a recession dragged down stock prices. The invasion of Kuwait on August 2,
1990, and the spike in oil prices confirmed the slowdown that drove the equity
drawdown.
The drawdown, began on July 16th, lasted three months and resulted in a total
drawdown from peak of 19.02%. The recovery was rapid, and markets returned to
peak 119 days after the low. This particular section of the Deleveraging was brief
but the period that began in early 89 was one of significant volatility throughout
the 2-year period ending when the US famously responded in Iraq.
8

My personal experience during this period was as a convertible bond trader. At the
time leading up to the Drexel Bankruptcy below par convertible bonds (broken
bonds) of below investment grade issuers were for sale, savings and loans were no
longer buyers or had become active sellers, and Drexel who was often the
underwriter was somewhat impaired. Once Drexel went bankrupt Salomon and a
couple other desks were asked by Drexel traders to bid competitively for their
inventory. This was my first experience of a fire sale of assets by a major end
users and liquidity provider.
This deleveraging episode looks nothing like today’s environment. This
episode was a classic credit boom bust cycle with a meaningful impact on
the real economy. The equity drawdown was coincident in timing with the
economic slowdown and was exacerbated by the liquidation of major
credit providers.
1994 Broad deleveraging – Duration led event
1994 was the first duration led deleveraging. In this case despite tame inflation the
Greenspan led Fed decided to begin a hiking cycle to address a labor market that
was perceived as overheating. Starting in February 1994 the Fed hiked Fed Funds
by 300 bp over the next year.
10-year yields rose rapidly which had a major effect on the mortgage
market. Remember the mortgage market had gone through massive change in the
late 80's and early 90's led primarily by Salomon and Goldman Sachs. Mortgages
were sliced up into various parts splitting up interest payments IO's and Principal
payments PO's of active mortgage pools. Increasingly sophisticated complex
formations of mortgage cash flows and derivatives to speculate on and hedge those
flows also exploded in volumes and activity. The rapid move in the 10-year yield
9

caused all mortgages to significantly extend in duration. IO’s became much more
valuable and PO's much less valuable. Many levered investors and dealers,
counting on low interest rates and low interest rate volatility, including particularly
Merrill Lynch, Kidder Peabody and a few large hedge funds got destroyed.
This episode of deleveraging began with mortgages but as mortgage hedge funds
blew up a contagion swept the hedge fund world. Due to concentrated losses end
investors withdrew capital broadly from all hedge funds. Hedge funds had been
around for quite some time but the early 90’s was a period of massive growth
particularly for hedge funds that performed market neutral arbitrage strategies. As
the contagion spread it was clear that many such funds and dealer desks as well
found out that interest rate risk was a much bigger part of their exposure than they
had thought in the past. By the end of 1994 the bankruptcy of Orange County
caused by Robert Citrons speculative exposures on interest rates capped the period
and marked the end of the bond unwind.
Though 1994 was a meaningful deleveraging event it was not a big deal for equity
markets. The surprise hike marked the peak on 2/2/1994 but the drawdown in
equities was mild at 8.4% over 3 months. Given the extended period of time that
the fixed income and relative value arbitrage space was impacted the recovery took
279 days.
At the time Wall Street dealers were actively enhancing returns from market
making with proprietary trading acting like hedge funds. 1994 was my first
drawdown. I was managing the U.S. convertible trading business at Salomon and
our proprietary inventory was marked down as hedge fund clients were forced to
sell to meet redemptions and the market cheapened aggressively.
1994 is not comparable to today’s environment. 1994 was about duration and the
ability for leveraged fixed income investors to manage through a large bond market
sell off. The impact on all leveraged relative value players was significant due to
contagion. The economy and the equity market were largely unaffected. While
market participants look to the hiking cycle and subsequent soft landing as a
potential parallel to today, the events of that tumultuous period in Fixed income
history are not reflected in bond markets today.
1997 Asian Financial Crisis – Minor contagion
The 1997 Asian financial crisis began when the Thai Baht was let float. It is an
interesting case study which you can read about here in which I describe the full
event and my personal experiences in detail. It is a good story with lots of famous
characters. I will limit the focus to the brief and violent contagion event that
impacted global markets and marked the bottom of the both the Asian Crisis and
the deleveraging it drove.
10

The De-pegging of the Thai Baht which ignited the Asian Financial Crisis of 1997
occurred on July 2nd of 1997. US equity markets didn’t notice and preceded to rally
11% while all hell broke loose in Asia.
As the Baht continued to devalue and after an initial and typical surge and
retracement of the local Thai equity market post devaluation, speculative traders
began buying the dip. Thai equities priced in USD appeared to be on sale. One
notable investor ex-Soros veteran and Asia expert Victor Niederhoffer entered into
long swaps on Thai equities in September as a bottom appeared to be developing.
By mid-October the Thai Baht and Thai equities began another leg lower. Margin
calls were sent. An important lesson that is now well understood by most users of
derivatives but was relatively new at the time was the speed at which margin calls
by one counterparty result in other counterparties calling for more margin as well.
LTCM and most recently Bill Hwang at Archegos have experienced this mass margin
call. In the 1997 case the margin calls on Victor Niederhoffer’s OTC Thai Swaps
quickly caused the US futures and options clearing broker REFCO to liquidate
11

Niederhoffer’s short SPX put spreads. The impact was immediate. Does this look
familiar to anyone from last week?
The deleveraging that occurred in October of 1997 resulted in a 10.8% drawdown,
was essentially over in 3 days and markets took a short 39 days to recapture the
prior high.
Every sharp drawdown is scary at the time. Market participants are worried about
further drawdowns but also don’t want to miss a dip like the one in 1997. The 1997
episode was certainly a lesson in contagion, but it was limited as the dip buyers
speculating in Asia currencies and assets simply couldn’t have positioning that
could create broad exposure and systemic contagion. The DM economy was not
impacted by the Asian Financial Crisis and the central banks had absolutely no need
to respond. This is a classic buyable dip deleveraging. The factors creating the last
month’s deleveraging are unlikely to be resolved in three days. We do not consider
the Asian Financial Crisis deleveraging to be a comparable example.
1998 LTCM – Leverage Event
Long Term Capital Management was a 4BN hedge fund founded by a dozen or so
ex colleagues from Salomon Brothers. They took leveraged positions in global
fixed income and equity markets in order to extract arbitrage profits within and
across asset markets. After the 1997 Asian Financial Crisis hedge funds and
proprietary desks at Goldman, and Salomon and others began questioning the
opportunity set, sizes, and risks of their arbitrage books. LTCM had just returned
$2BN to its investors to address the smaller opportunity set. By the 2Q LTCM had
started giving back profits on holdings. Salomon had an arb group at the time run
by my friend and mentor Rob Stavis. The arb group was the crown jewel of
Salomon’s business for a decade. However Sandy Weill had recently bought the
firm and merged with Citibank to form Citigroup and had no interest at all in risk
12

taking on markets. I related my own story about Sandy and risk taking in the 1997
section link above. My team had lost $10MN short vol on the Hang Sang Index.
We were forced to cover by Sandy directly at 100 implied vol. The losses in the arb
group were likely at least 1BN. Sandy closed the business in July. Liquidating the
positions of the Salomon Arb group into a weak market is not give adequate blame
for the events of later in the summer. In addition, the Russian Ruble crisis
occurred in mid-August of 98 and other multi-strat hedge funds were exposed to
both EM risk and had similar trades to LTCM. LTCM is the well-deserved poster
child of this deleveraging, but it was also part of a large, concentrated exposure
that included banks. Where 1997 was a small deleveraging of a handful of players
with modest contagion to tangential markets the, LTCM crisis was far more intense.
Besides the dislocation in arbitrage positions the LTCM episode also resulted in
significant drawdowns in equity markets. From peak in July the SPX fell 19.2%
making a low on October 8 and recovering rapidly just 46 days later to retake the
July high.
The rapid recovery of the markets despite a large deleveraging involving not only
LTCM but other hedge funds and bank prop desks was almost certainly due to
policymaker action. The Fed engineered the bailout of LTCM by organizing
investment of 3.5BN of capital by 14 banks. Then the Fed cut rates by 75bp in less
than 2 months including a highly unusual intermeeting surprise cut late in the
afternoon on October 15th. It’s certainly impossible to determine the counterfactual
of less robust action by the Fed but it is clear to us that the economy didn’t miss a
beat, and the tech bubble inflated further due to the actions taken during this
deleveraging.
My own experiences during the LTCM event were pretty interesting. I was sent up
to LTCM by Sandy to attempt to buy the book of positions with two colleagues and
Sandy’s son. We were rebuffed nicely by my friend Victor Haghani, who now
ironically runs a fantastic company called Elm Wealth which focuses on optimal
beta investing.
But the more interesting experience was during the stressful period of the pre
bailout days. Every silo at Salomon and across most of Wall Street had some
exposure to LTCM. The Equity Derivatives business I was responsible for had 5MN
vega, spread across major global equity indices, of 4-year straddles that we had
13

purchased from LTCM as a hedge to other customer buyers. If LTCM went
bankrupt, we were unsure of whether we had a hedge at all. This forced us to bid
up long term implied volatility in order to replace LTCM’s exposure. This created a
vicious circle as bidding up the position required LTCM to place more collateral with
us. Of course, every exposure they had was also bid up against them as each silo
looked to find a source to replace LTCM risk. It’s a tremendous lesson for risk
takers of any sort and in the GFC was key to survival. Mark to markets determine
maintenance margin. When you are in positions that are levered and crowded there
is no fair value. Otherwise perfectly safe positions can get margin called.
The last personal experience from the LTCM episode was months later when LTCM
finally decided to unwind positions. The bailout was designed not to keep LTCM
afloat in perpetuity but to slow down the liquidation. We were asked to offer the
straddles they had sold on SPX in competition with other dealers. While they had
over 100MN of vega to buy over the next few months the mark to market was so
inflated that the trade happened well below the bid side. From that moment on and
despite their continuous liquidation buying the market never saw that level for
years. We lost the auction to sell the peak vol. Vol crush ensued.
2000-2002 Tech bubble and Credit collapse – Bubble Event
This section will be brief. Clearly including the tech bubble and post bubble credit
collapse is irrelevant to understand a financial market deleveraging cycle. The only
reason we mention this episode is as a potential post financial deleveraging cycle
outcome.
As a reminder this cycle began with a peak in the equity market on 3/24/2000.
The drawdown was 49.2% hitting a low on 10/9/2002 and took 4.64 years to
recover the all-time high.
The important take aways during this episode was the ordering.
The Equity market led falling steadily. When credit contracted equities fell sharply.
14

Credit spreads were volatile but then widened rapidly consistent with credits
convex nature.
Unemployment ticked up from 4 to 4.2 during 2000 and didn’t begin to increase
rapidly until over a year had passed from the equity top
GDP was weakening but didn’t fall into recession until equities had fallen enough to
tighten conditions and credit began contracting.
15

Significant rate cuts took place during the whole drawdown
Today AI is the Internet of the 30 years ago. Artificial Intelligence will almost
certainly make a major impact on the future of the world in a similar way as the
internet did over the past thirty years. Since the peak of the US Equity market the
AI stocks have underperformed the broader market. We have no idea if this is the
cyclical top of the AI stocks, but we doubt it. What matters is if the relatively
stretched values of US equities begin to correct will that lead to higher
unemployment and below trend GDP? This is unknown but private sector credit is
in much better shape to withstand an equity selloff and recession than in 2000.
Comparing 2000-2002 with 2024 may or may not be a good comparison regarding
stock prices based on speculative valuations. But the comparison for signs of a
deleveraging will not tell us much.
Last story as most of you have lived through the rest of the events discussed
below. My personal experience from the Tech bubble era is participating in the
unwind of the Investment Banking, Equity Research, Institutional Equity Trading
business model on Wall Street. The whole business model was disassembled. It
was a pretty bad business model to begin with rife, with conflict of interest and
barely profitable. By the time I left Salomon to start my own hedge fund it was
clear that further head count and pay reduction was inevitable. Today’s business
looks nothing like it did in 2003.
2007-2009 GFC – Bank Event
The GFC is simply irrelevant to current markets. The GFC was at its heart a
deleveraging of the leverage providers of the financial system. The breadth depth
and economic consequences of such a deleveraging was unprecedented.
Today, examining bank health and credit risk in the system it is clear that the GFC
is not a proxy. While a cautionary tale for some future environment when private
sector credit is extended and banks are over-levered, we are confident that any
deleveraging event in the near future will not look like the GFC

2010-2014 European Debt Crisis – Geopolitical Event
Post GFC the next 8 years or so were dominated by the still intractable problem of
a monetary union without a fiscal union. The occasional flair ups that have
occurred have created brief contagions which at best have revealed potential
vulnerabilities for which we can draw lessons. The most notable contagion from
the on and off European debt crisis happened in May of 2010. The flash crash
revealed the ongoing difficulty of strategies that attempt to adjust positioning
dynamically as negative catalysts occur.
On April 22 of 2010 the EU announced the budget deficit for Greece was going to
be worse than expected. Greek debt made new lows. A global equity selloff
commenced. I joined Bridgewater on May 3rd. 2010. Later that week the Flash
Crash occurred which was a portfolio insurance strategy that broke its algo. The
selloff that day quickly reversed as the seller had oversold. But soon after the
market made new lows as a risk off deleveraging continued. The flash crash was a
severe reaction not dissimilar to price action on Sunday August 4- Monday August
5th of 2024.
This cycle began with a peak in the equity market on 4/22/2010. The drawdown
was 12.4% hitting a low on 7/5/2010 and took 122 days to recover the all-time
high.

The drawdown in the summer of 2015 which reverted rapidly but took many

China's economy continues to struggle. Major currency repricings generate

This cycle began with a peak in the equity market on 5/21/2015. The drawdown

2018 Fed Overtightens – Monetary Event
Perhaps comparable to 1994 the 2018 deleveraging was the result of meaningful
central bank tightening
Like 1994 the Fed decided to begin an aggressive tightening cycle in 2018 based on
a hot labor market.
18

The Fed increased Fed Funds by 125 bp in 2018 and began QT
QT Runoff preceded throughout 2018 and into 2019 reducing the balance sheet by
$750BN
Bank Reserves fell even more as the Treasury General Account grew a bit. By early
2019 Bank Reserves were only 7% of GDP. Reserves were tight and short-term
rates had doubled.
By late 2018 Long term bond rates had also risen by 60bp. This was too much
tightening for financial assets. A large deleveraging ensued.
19

The low in equities occurred on Christmas Eve 2018 and soon after Chairman
Powell pivoted. Rate hikes ended but QT was kept on “Autopilot”. As the economy
had not slowed much at all due to the tightening, equities recovered. However, by
the Fall of 2019 QT had to be ended as reserves were scarce and repo rates spiked
well above the Fed Funds target. The Fed responded by offering unlimited usage of
their Standing Repo Facility.
This cycle began with a peak in the equity market on 8/24/2018. The drawdown
was 19.8% hitting a low on 12/24/2018 and took 119 days to recover the all-time
high.
This deleveraging like 1994 was caused by Fed tightening. Unlike 1994 there was
no duration crisis and no forced deleveraging of hedge funds or dealers. Currently
the Fed is priced by the market to be entering a meaningful cutting cycle and has
tapered QT with reserves at over 14% of GDP. Yet the Fed is concerned about
reserves which we don’t understand except for the uneven distribution of reserves
that we have written about in multiple DSR’s. Given ample reserves and a cutting
cycle price in we do not see the 2018 deleveraging as a guide for a deleveraging in
the current environment.
2020 Covid – Exogenous Event
Covid was notable due to the strength of both the fiscal and monetary response. It
is relevant, not due to the drawdown in financial markets which reversed and took
out ATH highs rapidly, but due to the power and consequences of a massive
double-barreled response to a crisis and the economic conditions that would
necessitate such a response.
This cycle began with a peak in the equity market on 2/19/2020. The drawdown
was 33.9% hitting a low on 3/24/2018 and took only 148 days to recover the all-
time high.
The Covid response will simply not occur without another exogenous event or a
deep recession. None of that is relevant to trade markets today but the bazooka
MMT put has a very low strike.
2022 UK LDI Crisis – Duration Led Event
Like the 1994 duration crisis the LDI duration crisis was the result of monetary
tightening. Unlike 1994 the monetary tightening did not result in a massive rise in

2023 SVB Banking Crisis – Duration Led Event
The Fed response to the duration crisis was to massively ease financial conditions
and kick the can down the road for 2 years. Other agencies, assisted by the Fed,
essentially guaranteed that even uninsured depositors would be safe. The
economic consequence of the US policymaker response to the crisis may have been
proper at the time but unlike the BOE response the easing lingers.
The relatively strong response once again resulted in a short-lived deleveraging
and rapid recovery.
Because of a full court press by policymakers this cycle was a deleveraging “for
ants” it began with a peak in the equity market on 2/15/2023. The drawdown was
6.1% hitting a low on 3/13/2023 and took only 31 days to recover the local high.

Primary dealer positioning is elevated

Synthesis
A deleveraging has occurred. No policymaker response is likely and based
on asset prices today no damage to the economy has occurred. Banks are
willing and able to provide credit to the private sector and bank balance
sheets are relatively safe from credit losses
