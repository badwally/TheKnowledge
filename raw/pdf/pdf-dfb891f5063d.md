How to get your inner adult in place.
Week of 11/4/24 Journal Prompts (edited)
Much of childhood trauma recovery work is about navigating an internal battle
between our inner adult and our inner child. Often, it can feel like our inner child
pulls left when we want to take a right.
If you are new to the inner adult and inner child concept, the inner adult can be
seen as our prefrontal cortex, the part of our brain that allows us to think and feel
simultaneously. When our prefrontal cortex is more driving our cognition, we can
function better under stress and, for example, not lose our ability to communicate
when triggered.
We can view the inner child as our limbic system or trauma brain, specifically our
amygdala, which houses our emotional memory system and acts like our body's
smoke alarm.
When our inner adult (prefrontal cortex) wants to take a right and ask our boss for
a raise or our partner for special times, our inner child (amygdala) pulls left and
can sabotage those conversations of the childhood trauma history of getting
screamed at about expressing needs.
We often struggle between how we want to feel, react, be, and respond versus our
survival conditioning from our family system of origin. Survival conditioning
usually runs the show until we can practice and develop a healthy inner adult.
Some examples of this internal battle.
Wanting to choose better partners, but old habits and attachment issues take
•
over.

Wanting to be more present and light-hearted, pessimism or negativity takes
•
over.
Wanting to function better with everything from being organized to not
•
sweating mundane tasks such as laundry or cleaning, but coping or
emotional chaos takes over.
Wanting to set boundaries or have hard conversations, fear or dissociation
•
takes over.
Want to switch jobs or start creative projects, but shame and stuckness take
•
over?
Wanting to communicate well at the moment, but anxiety or either shutting
•
down or over-explaining takes over.
Wanting to navigate conflict better but shutting down or going big takes
•
over.
The wanting in all those examples can be seen as the desire of your inner adult
(you have one) to seek a better life and better emotional reality.
Where things end up can be seen as your inner child taking over or operating your
system from the old childhood place of survival. The inner child is not bad for
doing this. We need to honor them for getting us through life in that family. Now,
they need someone to take over, but that is often terrifying for them.
At the beginning of healing work, the inner adult often struggles with poor
confidence. Of course, we do. We are new at this and are fighting to create a new
way of living from scratch.
If you are unfamiliar with or need help understanding the dynamics between the
inner adult and inner child. Here is a role-playing video I did that covers the
relationship and dynamics. Pay attention to the attitude and approach of the inner
adult in both role plays.
Getting the inner adult more in place is a process. The more you practice and dig
into the concept of the inner adult, the more you gain skills and confidence. You'll
know your inner adult is in place when you can get yourself out of a trigger by
reparenting your inner child with the dialoguing tool in your membership's
Reparenting The Inner Child E-Course.

Here are some activities and journal prompts about getting your inner adult more in
place. Again, the more you do, the more confidence is built, and trust is established
with your inner child.
In these activities, it's crucial to think good enough and not be done from a place of
perfectionism. Our inner child watches us in terms of consistency, and the work is
about pointing out that you are showing up well enough.
Inner Adult Activities
1. Try committing to being mindful of your inner child for thirty days. VIDEO
HERE If you are new to dialoguing and overwhelmed, write to your inner
child that you are thinking of them for 1-2 weeks. This is a one-way
conversation to start.
2. Pick a task you keep putting off and handle it. (Taking out the trash to get to
those boxes in the basement).
3. When upset, visualize holding or hugging your inner child for a few minutes
and sit with the feelings. It's helpful to say, 'Yes, this is upsetting, but you
are safe with me."
4. If you isolate or avoid, commit to showing up in some small way more.
Think small, such as making it a point to talk more in a work meeting or
inviting someone you are comfortable with for coffee.
5. Write out a list of the ways your inner adult is different from your parents.
("I don't use people." "I don't gossip or think everyone else is out to get
me.", "I pay my bills." "I don't make my children feel like burdens."
6. Write out a list of things you do for your recovery. (joined membership, tried
or attended therapy, left that horrible relationship, did the family cut off,
signed up for the class, showed up at events). Both the inner adult and inner
child don't recognize the ways you are already a good parent for yourself.
7. Practice not reacting and getting time to formulate a healthier response when
triggered.
8. Practice accountability. With a friend, partner, or therapist, commit to
emailing them for a month about not doing a behavior. "I'm letting you
know I didn't doom Sroll last night." – "Emailing you to let you know I
dialogued with my inner child." It can be understood that the recipient

doesn't need to respond but can check in with you if three days go by
without notice from you.
9. Write out a list of how you're already different. If you grew in areas, it was
because of work your inner adult did – even if you wouldn't use that idea or
language at the time. "I stopped dating in my mid-thirties for a year to work
on myself." "I went to a program and got my eating under control."
Some common issues with getting our inner adult in place.
We all start in the inner child with random pockets where the inner adult
•
pops us. It's ok to feel like you're all an inner child.
I've never met a client who stayed in the inner adult the minute I suggested
•
it. It's like a flickering light until we can get in on more consistently.
There is usually a trigger around trying and failing. Our inner child can often
•
say, "See, I knew you'd fuck that up or wouldn't do it." This is our inner
child projecting our parents onto the inner adult or a cycle of self-hate. It's
important for the inner adult not to take this in and let the child know they
are trying and it's unfair.
It is possible to get to a place where your inner adult is more in charge and
•
aware of the inner child's tendency to be in survival mode. The more we are
in our inner adult gradually, the more the child can trust and let go of
engaging in survival responses.
Journal Prompts
Journal Prompt #1
What does it look like when my inner adult comes online, even if it's sporadic and
short? Write out specific examples where you've been in your inner adult which
might have surprised you such as setting a boundary with someone while being a
bit dissociated but you were still firm.
Journal Prompt #2

What does your inner child need from your inner adult?
Consistency?
•
Boundaries around coping strategies?
•
Connection?
•
Can you provide more consistency, attention, and compassion in a good enough
way? The process isn’t going to be perfect so you have to allow for mistakes.
Journal Prompt #3
Write about what your inner adult struggles with or is afraid of.
"I'm afraid of failing, not showing up, and being like my parents." (Remember,
you're not like your parents because you are trying.)
"I'm overwhelmed and really don't know what to say or do."
Think of how you might be the different from your family at reparenting a child.
Journal Prompt #4
What is your inner adult's attitude about the task of reparenting?
"I just want someone else to do that. I can't."
"I'm afraid I won't do it perfectly, or my kid won't like me."
Could your inner adult’s attitude be related to childhood such as being parentified
and not wanting to have to be a parent again?
Could your fear come from not wanting to cause more damage like the adults did
in your childhood?
