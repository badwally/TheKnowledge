The Body As A Computer Analogy
Here at Primal Trust™, we approach things differently. We
believe that addressing the mind-body connection through
nervous system regulation and limbic system retraining can
make a world of difference when it comes to reversing chronic
conditions. Rather than believing symptoms are the result of
your diagnoses, we find that many unresolving symptoms
can be the result of a maladaptive stress response wreaking

impaired mind-body communication keeping you from
realizing at a deep level what your body needs to self-heal.
You can think of your body with chronic issues like having a
sluggish computer, clogged up with old files, and running on
an out-of-date operating system. At first, you try to work with
what you’ve got, installing more programs, and downloading
more files, but inevitably picking up more viruses. Until one

pathogens, and inflammation because the brain-body stress
signaling is limiting self-healing).
Nothing is running smoothly and all the computer’s energy is
getting used up just powering on, let alone performing the
tasks you need (i.e. your body’s energy is stuck and not
detoxing, digesting, fighting infections, etc).

The autonomic nervous system (ANS) has two branches: our

balanced state. Heart rate and breathing slow down, and the
body returns to calm. When things get too stressful for too

parasympathetic nervous system activates in an extreme way

A Basic Overview of the Science
Behind the Protection Response
Naviaux explains that when a threat (like a pathogen or toxin
exposure, physical injury, or trauma) is detected, mitochondria
change cellular metabolism to protect the cell from further
injury. Mitochondria decrease oxygen consumption and stiffen
their membrane to limit the spread of pathogens, and they
release antiviral/antibacterial chemicals and ATP from the cell.

organs, this extracellular ATP (eATP) acts as a warning
messenger to other cells, rapidly spreading the ‘danger
response’ throughout the body. A host of defense-related
processes occur as a result, affecting metabolism,
inflammation (increased release of cytokines and
chemokines), immunity, microbiome, brain function, sleep
patterns, and behavior. These CDR processes are
fundamental to survival and are all part of a normal
response to a perceived threat at the cellular level. They are
universal, meaning that the same CDR is activated by many
different types of threats (stress, toxins, pathogens, etc).
Normally, once the danger has been eliminated or perceived
as gone, a choreographed sequence of anti-inflammatory and
regenerative processes in the body is activated to reverse the
CDR and heal (i.e. you have innate self-healing mechanisms
when you come back into safety!). In some cases, however, the
cells become stuck in this protective ‘danger phase’ and
the body cannot complete the healing process; this is what

Autonomic nervous system
The autonomic nervous system (ANS) is a division of the peripheral
nervous system that controls involuntary bodily functions. It
regulates processes such as heart rate, digestion, respiratory rate,
pupillary response, and sexual arousal. The ANS has two main
branches: the sympathetic nervous system, which activates the
"fight or flight" response, and the parasympathetic nervous system,
which promotes the "rest and digest" state. Together, these
branches help maintain internal balance and respond to external
stimuli to ensure the body functions optimally.

Brain retraining
Refers to the process of changing neural pathways in the brain
through various techniques and exercises. The underlying idea is
that the brain is adaptable and can be modified to improve not only
its function, but the function of the whole body and nervous
system.
Cell Danger Response
The Cell Danger Response (CDR) is a concept in cellular biology
proposed by Dr. Robert Naviaux. It describes a set of protective
measures and changes that cells undergo in response to stress,
threat, or danger. The idea behind the Cell Danger Response is that
cells, when faced with various stressors such as infection, toxins, or
injury, activate a defense mechanism aimed at promoting survival
and maintaining cellular integrity.
Interoception
The sense that allows an individual to perceive and understand the
internal state of their body. It involves sensing signals and
information from within the body, such as those related to
heartbeat, breathing, hunger, thirst, temperature, and other
physiological processes. Interoception plays a crucial role in
maintaining homeostasis—the body's internal balance—and in
guiding behavior based on internal signals.
Limbic System
A complex network of structures within the brain that is involved in
various functions, including emotions, memory, and certain aspects
of behavior. It is sometimes referred to as the "emotional brain" due
to its central role in emotional responses and regulation. The limbic
system is not a distinct anatomical entity but rather a collection of

interconnected brain regions that work together to coordinate
different aspects of emotional and cognitive processing. It includes
the hypothalamus, hippocampus, and amygdala.
Maladaptive stress response
Refers to an individual’s habitual, often automatic, chronic stress
response in the brain and nervous system. Maladaptive patterns
develop as a way for the brain and nervous system to cope and
survive with fear, anxiety and trauma, but are not optimal for health
and wellbeing. The result of a maladaptive stress response is a
dysregulated nervous system and overactive limbic system.
Neuroplasticity
Neuroplasticity, also known as brain plasticity or neural plasticity, is
the ability of the brain to re-organize and adapt by forming new
neural connections throughout life. This process involves the
structural and functional changes in the brain's neural networks,
including the strengthening or weakening of existing synapses
(connections between neurons) and the formation of new
synapses.
Parts Work/Internal Family Systems (IFS)

individuals to cope with various life experiences and challenges.
The goal of Internal Family Systems therapy is to help individuals
explore, understand, and integrate these different parts in order to
promote inner harmony and well-being.

Polyvagal Theory
Polyvagal Theory is a framework developed by Dr. Stephen Porges
that provides insights into the autonomic nervous system (ANS)
and its role in shaping our physiological responses to stress, social
interactions, and emotional experiences. The theory highlights the
evolutionary development of the vagus nerve and its branches,
emphasizing their influence on the body's responses to
environmental cues and the regulation of social engagement.
Key concepts within polyvagal theory include:
1- Autonomic Nervous System (ANS): The autonomic nervous
system is responsible for regulating involuntary bodily functions,
such as heart rate, respiratory rate, and digestion. It consists of two
main branches: the sympathetic nervous system (SNS) and the
parasympathetic nervous system (PNS).
2- Vagus Nerve: The vagus nerve is a major component of the
parasympathetic nervous system and plays a crucial role in
regulating the body's internal organs. The vagus nerve has two
main branches: the ventral vagus (linked to social engagement and
communication) and the dorsal vagus (linked to immobilization
and shutdown responses).
3- Social Engagement System: According to Polyvagal Theory, the
ventral vagus nerve is associated with the social engagement
system. When this system is activated, individuals are more likely to
engage in social interactions, experience feelings of safety, and
display facial expressions that promote connection.
4- Fight-Flight-Freeze Responses: The sympathetic nervous system,
often associated with the fight-or-flight response, is activated in
situations of perceived threat or danger. In contrast, the dorsal
vagus is associated with immobilization and shutdown responses,
such as freezing or dissociation.

Polyvagal Theory suggests that the autonomic nervous system
does not operate as a simple on-off switch between the
sympathetic and parasympathetic branches. Instead, it involves a
hierarchical organization where the ventral vagus nerve supports
social engagement and adaptive responses, while the dorsal vagus
and sympathetic systems are recruited in response to increased
levels of perceived threat.
Somatics
Somatics refers to a field of study and practice that involves the
integration of the mind and body in the exploration of personal
experience, movement, and awareness. The term "somatic" comes
from the Greek word "soma," which means the living body in its
wholeness. Somatics encompasses various approaches aimed at
understanding and improving the connection between the body,
mind, and overall well-being.
Key aspects of somatics include:
1- Embodiment: Somatic practices emphasize the experience of
being present in one's body. This involves developing awareness of
bodily sensations, movements, and the felt sense of experience in
the present moment.
2- Mind-Body Connection: Somatics recognizes the
interconnectedness of the mind and body, emphasizing that
mental and emotional states are expressed and experienced
through the body. The way we move, breathe, and hold tension can
reflect our emotional and psychological states.
3- Movement Practices: Many somatic practices incorporate
movement as a means of exploring and improving the mind-body
connection. These may include mindful movement, dance, yoga,
Feldenkrais Method, and other movement therapies.

4- Body Awareness and Sensation: Somatic education often involves
cultivating awareness of bodily sensations and exploring the
sensations associated with different movements, postures, and

5- Trauma-Informed Approaches: Somatics is frequently applied in
trauma-informed care, recognizing that trauma can be stored in the
body and impact overall well-being. Somatic therapies aim to

6- Self-Regulation: Somatic practices often include techniques for
