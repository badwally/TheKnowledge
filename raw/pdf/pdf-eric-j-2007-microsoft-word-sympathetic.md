'Sympathetic Exchange',
Adam Smith, And Punishment

Introduction
Adam Smith says relatively little about justice and punishment in the Theory of
Moral Sentiments. What he does say is at times cryptic, and even the sections that
are relatively clear and well worked out rest on a moral theory which
necessitates us to look at moral action from a number of different perspectives
and over a number of different stages. It is not my intention here to derive a
theory from his, or even to claim that it is the best theory of its sort. I merely
wish to defend the internal coherence and consistency of the theory of
punishment, such as it is, from one of its most recent attacks, and in the process
to add some new insights, or rather, syntheses of his (and others) ideas.
Smith’s account of moral judgement follows that of David Hume in
asserting that "it is altogether absurd and unintelligible to suppose that the
first perceptions of right and wrong can be derived from reason" (Smith 1976a,
VII.iii.2.7; see Hume 1989, 455-70). Rather, he contends that these perceptions
have their source in our sentiments or passions, arising from an "immediate
sense and feeling" of approval or disapproval which arises from a feeling of
sympathy on the part of a spectator; firstly, with the situation of the agent

Norrie's Attack
In a recent article by Alan Norrie mounts an attack on both Smith and
the modern interpreters of Smith's philosophy when discussing the appropriate
measure of punishment. The latter Norrie criticises for overly emphasising the
influence of retributivist justice in Smith's account; Norrie provides a number
of instances in which it is considerations of utility, rather than of retributive
justice, which seem to determine the magnitude of punishment. However I
want to concentrate on his attack on Smith, whom he criticises for having no
clear idea of the distinction between what Norrie calls retributivism and
retributionism as a means of determining what is to "constitute . . . a just
distribution of punishment" (Norrie 1989, 230).
Retributivist approaches to punishment focus on the crime solely as a
measure of the criminal's wrongdoing; the aim of punishment is to extinguish
the criminal's moral responsibility in proportion to the gravity of the offence.
One crude example of retributivism is "an eye for an eye"; a more sophisticated
calculus may involve an attempt to commensurate e.g. number of years in
prison with severity of personal injury suffered. Norrie points out that such an
attempt to determine the appropriate degree of punishment is backward-
looking , "the returning of a right to a criminal... criminal desert is something owed
to the criminal for what he has done in the past" (Norrie 1989, 231, his italics). On
the other hand, retributionist approaches to justice concentrate not on the
criminal and his moral culpability, but on the effect of the crime on the victim
and society. "[P]unishment is justified . . . in terms of the satisfaction-effect that
the punishment will have for the victim" (Norrie 1989, ibid.). This is a forward-
looking theory of punishment, "a theory relating to the future effects of
punishment on the victim or third parties" (Norrie 1989, ibid.).
Norrie perceives these twin measures of appropriate punishment as
expressing a dichotomy between principles of justice and utility, which he then
applies to Smith's two stages of moral judgement. On this view, stage one, the
judgement of propriety, depends upon retributivist considerations of justice.
Stage two, the judgement of merit or demerit, is concerned with retributionist
considerations of utility. Norrie's criticism ammounts to asserting that these
two stages are mutually contradictory and incommensurable: if one principle

Whilst Norrie's use of the retributivist/retributionist terminology provides a
useful point of comparison with modern ideas of punishment, my contention
essentially is that such language is too narrow to capture the particular nature
of Smith’s theory of morals, justice and punishment.
Resentment and Propriety
In Smith’s theory, punishment depends on resentment: "[H]e ...appears to
deserve punishment, who in the same manner is to some person or persons the
natural object of resentment which the breast of every reasonable man is ready
to adopt and sympathise with" (Smith 1976a, II.i.2.3). But, as was earlier
suggested, for Smith, punishment is the second part of a two stage process - it
does not come into operation until the hurdle of the first stage is crossed. Smith
himself says
[i]f...there has been no impropriety in the motives of the agent, if, on the contrary, the
affections which influenced his conduct are such as we must necessarily enter into, we can have
no sort of sympathy with the person who suffers (Smith 1976a, II.i.3.1 - he restates this point
again at Smith 1976a, II.i.3.3, and Smith 1976a, II.i.4.3).
Thus the sentiment of resentment, from which punishment stems, is a
"compound sentiment", requiring firstly the presence of some moral
impropriety to justify the resentment.
Propriety, on Smith’s analysis, is focused on a "direct sympathy with the
affections and motives of the person who acts" (Smith 1976a, I.5.1); this is how
Smith sums it up when considering merit and demerit, and this is what Norrie
characterises as retributivist and backward-looking. Sympathy is "direct"
because arising directly from the situation in which the agent acts. Smith
believes that moral judgement starts in our being accosted by a situation.
Something surprises us about the situation, something out of the ordinary
which does not fit our expectation of "what normally goes on", and we as

As Haakonssen points out "propriety is a question of the aptness of a given

non-normal nature of the situation causes1 a sympathetic response by
prompting us to ask "What is going on?" In order to judge whether we approve
or disapprove of the agent's actions we ask how we would have responded in a
like situation, and use our imagination to put ourselves in the agent's place to
test this out. This sympathetic response is the central element in Smith's moral
theory.
Sympathy
Sympathy thus bears the weight of Smith's moral theory: it is a difficult
concept to grasp, yet it is the foundation upon which the structure of propriety
and thus moral judgement is built. It is important to recognise that whilst
sympathy is not used by Smith in its usual sense (see Raphael 1985, 29), it is
incorrect in casting about for a suitable alternative to fasten unreflectively onto
"empathy" as a direct translation (See Lindgren 1973, 21-4). Sympathy is not
merely the act of wholeheartedly adopting the feelings of another, of bringing
one's own feelings into fine tune with those of another, or even subverting
one's feelings in order to be completely overtaken by those of the other. These
and other examples of empathy focus on an unmediated one-to-one
relationship that is too direct to encapsulate Smith's concept. Rather, as Raphael
points out (1985, 29), sympathy involves "not just sharing the feelings of
another, but being aware that one shares the feelings of another." It is this
additional consciousness of sharing one's feelings that adds the extra element
which is at the heart of sympathy, and which structures the whole action of
moral judgement.
This is interestingly brought out by Lindgren in his distinction between
aesthetic and moral sympathy (Lindgren 1973, 23-5). He is keen to distinguish
Smith's concept of sympathy from our ordinary use of the word empathy by
pointing out that Smith did not envisage every act of what we might call
empathy as being subsumed under the concept of sympathy. Moral sympathy,

But, as Lindgren points out (Lindgren 1973, 24), in a situation of moral
sympathy the spectator has to acknowledge the excited passions of the actor
and attempt to make allowances by having regard to the motive and situation
which prompted them. As a spectator to a situation of moral sympathy, we are,
as it were, "passers by" disinterested witnesses to the action sharing none of the
excitement of being caught up in the encounter. But the strongly different
reaction of the agent - a reaction that is "out of the ordinary," not what we
would expect as the socially apposite response - in Smith's terminology
"improprietous" - draws our attention to the situation and invites us to try to
understand the response of the agent. Propriety, this lack of fit with what is
socially normal, this difference between the agents response to the situation
and my own, explains why we even notice the action in the first place. Moral
sympathy, however, further depends upon the passionate response4 of the agent
to what Lindgren calls "objects of practical consequence" (Lindgren 1973, 23 his
italics) to that agent. (An object is "the entire situation in which the agent finds
himself": Lindgren ibid.). Objects of practical consequence are those things
which, for instance, an agent wants to claim responsibility for - good acts
performed, e.g. an act of bravery or kindness - or avoid or mitigate
condemnation for - bad actions normally eliciting social disapproval, e.g. a
murder charge. In these cases the agent is passionately concerned about a
situation, her participation in it, and its effect on her, and she seeks approval for
(or at least a non-condemnatory understanding of) her action and the motives
behind that action. It is this passionate commitment to an interest in "objects of
practical consequence" that helps differentiate "moral" sympathy from
'aesthetic' sympathy.
It is the fact that the actor is partisanly and passionately involved in her
actions which prompts her to seek our sympathy. She is concerned about an
action relevant to her well-being, and requires us to become interested parties in
her affair, both in the sense of (she hopes) taking her side, and more generally
of being bothered - or able - to become interested in her concerns at all. As
Haakonssen observes, Smith "makes it clear in various places that sympathy is
put to work by an even more fundamental principle in human nature, the
desire to agree, to be in accord with our fellow men" (Haakonssen 1989, 49). It
is this urge to sociability, this passionate craving for social response, this
"nearly compulsory interest in other men" (Haakonssen ibid.) which directs the
search for (moral) approval outwards5. First and foremost, according to Smith,
the agent seeks the response of another.6

aesthetic sympathy) rather than enabling the change in station to cope with the
passionate response that has already been shown to be necessary to moral
sympathy. Her own unmediated viewpoint is insufficient. It is a recipe for immoral
partiality:
There are two different occasions upon which we examine our own conduct, and endeavour to

to appreciate her side. She wants to be able to stand outside herself, distance
herself from any suspicion of personal bias, and seek the sanction of an

this feat herself, it could only be by splitting herself in two, in adopting the
position of the other, that she could be sure that she has done right. In the

that the action is out of the ordinary) becomes that of the spectator comparing

to judge whether they are worthy of praise or blame. Haakonssen speaks of this
process of shifting stations as a set of three distinct sympathetic moves:
(Haakonssen 1989, 53) I suggest that this should be taken literally, in the sense
that there are necessarily distinct and structurally separate positions in Smith's
process of moral judgement: it is essential to the structure of the process that
they remain distinct, and that the sympathiser move between them. This
requires a mutual recognition on the part of both spectator and agent of the
sympathetic enterprise being undertaken. We, as spectators, must try to
understand what it is about the agent and her relation to the situation that
made her act in the way she did. We must start to move towards some
understanding of the agent's point of view. And the agent must try to lead us as
spectators to some understanding of her situation by moderating her passion to
such a pitch as we are able to go along with.14 It is this mutuality of sympathy,
this attempt to reconcile the disparity of interests through a process of give and
take on the part of spectator and actor that defines moral sympathy15.

the concept of exchange, however, is a significant extension to that of his).
Smith's account can be seen as detailing the structure that enables the
spontaneous creation of these standards.
Propriety and Sympathetic Exchange
This structure of moral judgement is quite startling; the structural
separation of the spectator and agent entails that an individual is distanced even
from herself in judging the morality of a situation. What I wish to suggest is that
sympathy is, for Smith, part of a wider social phenomenon, and when he comes
to look at society as a whole he finds himself confronted with the fact of the
isolation of self from others (and from self). I hope to show that, far from
giving different pictures of our character as social beings, the Theory of Moral
Sentiments and the Wealth of Nations put forward a unified picture of man and
society17, and further, that the sympathetic creation of propriety gains in
coherence and explanatory power when seen as sharing the aims and even the
exchange-based structure of market interaction as theWealth of Nations .
It is important to recognise that both the accidental and the social nature
of sympathetic exchange combine with the structure of sympathy in the process
of forming an organic community from its disparate elements. Here I disagree
with those who, like Reisman (1976, ch 3), claim that Smith's spectator
represents some kind of "group mind" or the personification of Durkheimian

mechanical solidarity. On the contrary, the society Smith has in mind is one of
separate, atomistic individuals, with sympathy as the method of producing
quite different form of (in Durkheim's terms, organic) solidarity. The process
here envisioned by Smith is structurally similar to that which he was later to
use in the Wealth of Nations. The earlier work is, I believe, intended to describe
the process which the later work is designed to promote. It is possible to see the
theory of value in the Wealth of Nations as a method of relating an individual to
others in the market through 'the power of purchasing; a certain command over
all the labour, or over all the produce of labour which is then in the market'
(Smith 1976b, I.v.3). Note that the extent of this power is equal to its
exchangeable value18 - the interest another takes in goods expressed by how much
labour she expends in order to acquire it19. In the Theory of Moral Sentiments, the
moderation of passions which enables the parties to exchange sympathies is
achieved by a process of ‘moral haggling’ over what pitch and kind of passion
the agent wants and the spectator is willing to see entertained. In the Wealth of
Nations Smith wanted to show how the real price of goods is determined by
exchange:
It is adjusted... by the higgling and bargaining of the market, according to that sort of rough
equality which, though not exact, is sufficient for carrying on the business of common life.
(Smith 1976b, I.v.4)
In both, the effect of moral and market exchange is to produce stability and
equilibrium in their different spheres of social interaction20
The exchange of commodities in a market produces that equilibrium
Smith calls the "natural price": the exchange of sympathy in a moral setting
produces that equilibrium Smith calls propriety. Propriety is the outcome of
exchange. It does not reside in either the position of the spectator or the agent.
As already stated, propriety is the norm of moral action in a given situation, the
spectator position is the ideal of action21 and the agent position is the self-
interested, self-absorbed (see Hope 1989, 85) position of the actor too directly
involved in the action to have an unbiased view of it. Propriety has more to do

with the manner in which the different positions inter-relate than with any one

sympathy is not accomplished "for free". Rather, the sympathetic exchange
between spectator and other has to be bargained for. What is exchanged is not
just the pitch of the passion; the agent's interest in the situation23; it is also an
actual exchange of passions (and thus positions) themselves24. The benefit of a
successful exchange is the interest of the spectator, engendering either
approval, or perhaps mitigation of disapproval. The price of failing to strike a
bargain is the inability of the other to sympathise, and either absence of
reward, or a sanction (punishment): the other remains 'other'. At best, this
entails failing to receive
[T]hat relief which nothing can afford him but the entire concord of the affections of the
spectators with his own. (Smith 1976a, I.4.6)
At worst it suggests that the agent would be so morally repugnant as not to
appear human at all25.
Propriety is the settled equilibrium of social norms which set the
expected standard of behaviour and enable social cohesion. These operations of
sympathetic exchange are unplanned, rather than the result of the rational
enterprise of social engineering. They are low-level, self-interested (see Hope
1989, 89) interactions which have the side effect of producing the minimum
rules necessary for social life. They are carried on, not only between two
individuals, but throughout society. Society - or the social and moral pressure
exerted by those individuals who comprise it - helps establish our own moral

Our continual observations on the conduct of others, insensibly lead us to form to ourselves
certain general rules concerning what is fit and proper either to be done or to be avoided.
(Smith, 1976a, III.4.7)
Individuals form personal general rules - induced out of their own particular
moral experience - to act as maxims which furnish standards for future
behaviour. But it is our interaction with other members of society that enables
to escape from our isolation as discrete atomistic human beings: because we
ourselves contain the possibility of that otherness inside us. As moral, social
beings - and for Smith, the two are structurally linked: to be moral we need a
society of at least two; to be social we need morality to curb our self-regarding
instincts - we feel the pressure of others' approval or disapproval of our
behaviour (Smith, 1976a, III. i. 6). The fact that in undertaking moral sympathy
we are forced engage in an act of sympathetic exchange with the position of the
"other," a position that is often occupied with our fellows in society ensures that
"the spectator is in some sense the artificial presence of society within the
individual" (Campbell 1971, 151). But she is not so directly: what others do may,
by induction, furnish the norm of performance: the ideal necessitates a further
reflection on what is good all things considered (see the sections on 'duty' at
Smith, 1976a, III.i.).
Punishment
The second element in Smith’s model of moral judgment is the sentiment
of merit or demerit which "arises from... an indirect sympathy with the
gratitude of the person who is acted upon" (Smith 1976a, II.i.5.1). Sympathy is
indirect because tied to the propriety of the original action. It is the absence of
propriety which causes us to look to the person acted upon. It is that an action is
‘out of the ordinary’ - that it does not "fit" the situation in which it occurs -
which prompts a spectator to turn her regard towards the person affected in order
to see if we can sympathise with her reactions. Thus,
he . . . appears to deserve punishment, who . . . is to some person or persons the natural object
of a resentment which the breast of every reasonable man is ready to adopt and sympathise with
(Smith 1976a, II.i.2.3, my italics).
Punishment is the result of "real and positive hurt to some particular
persons, from motives which are naturally disapproved of" (Smith 1976a,
II.ii.1.5). It is not the mere want of performing some good action which entitles
the resentment which leads to punishment; there must be some actual injury.
But it is the whole situation, the improper motive and the injury, which give
rise to justified resentment. And how far the resentment can be considered
justified depends on how far the spectator can go along with it. Sympathy with
a victim is structured in the same fashion as sympathy with an agent. But the

passion of the victim is the justification - not the measure - of punishment. It is
the cause of punishment. Smith is not concerned with the future extinguishing
of the victim’s resentment, as Norrie claims; rather, he is only concerned with
present resentment as a spur to punishment. So, whilst "we entirely enter into,
and thereby approve of that resentment which prompts to inflict it
[punishment]" (my italics), it is from the "hurtfulness of the action.. joined [to]
the impropriety of the affection" which serves "to call aloud for punishment"
(Smith 1976a, II.i.4.4); it is the action and motive which serve as standards for
(retributivist) punishment.
To punish... is to recompense, to remunerate... to return evil for evil that has been done’ (Smith
1976a, II.i.1.4);... ‘As every man doth, so it shall be done to him, and retaliation seems to be the
great law which is dictated to us by Nature. (Smith 1976a, II.ii.1.10)
The measure of punishment is thus not directed towards the future effect of
punishment on the victim, but rather is retributivist and backward-looking,
focused on the motive and action of the agent. The sanction remains focused on the
agent, having its source in the original lack of propriety. The measure of
punishment is not torn between two potentially conflicting standards; one
retributivist, arising from the action of the agent; one utilitarian or
retributionist, focused on the resentment of the victim. Rather, there are two
points of view, that of the victim and that of the spectator, both of which are
concerned with the appropriate amount of punishment to be meted out to the
agent in proportion to the gravity of his offence.
Whilst there is no contra-determination possible in the measure of
punishment, because the spectator’s viewpoint looks in the same direction as
that of the victim, and acts a check on the latter’s resentment, there is still the
possibility of conflict between the two levels of moral judgment. Norrie’s
second major criticism is that these two parts or stages work
so long as there is co-determination of punishment. What of those situations where they pull in
different directions, where the anger of the victim calls for punishment but the act of the agent
is not improper or vice versa, i.e. where there is contra-determination? (Norrie 1989, 233)
The exchange model of sympathy gives us the answer to this problem. We
should remind ourselves that it is not the action or motive of the agent nor the
resentment of the victim which causes punishment. While these force our
attention on the norm of performance which has been breached, the standard
against which both are judged is the ideal of performance, which is or own
spectator response to the situation. Punishment stems from a response to an
impropriety. It is the spectator’s ability to sympathise with the different points
of view, the exchange between the different stations which take place through
the position of the spectator, which enable both participants in the original
situation to 'get outside the action' and come to an objective view about what
went on. They in effect bargain sympathies in order to attract the spectator's
concurrence in their version of events.

So, in a situation of contra-determination, if the agent acts with propriety
but the "victim" still expresses resentment, the victim must moderate his
passion to solicit sympathy - otherwise he will arouse the rightful resentment
of the agent, and be punished in his turn. If the agent, however, acts with
impropriety, but the victim manifests no resentment (or too much resentment),
the victim must "moderate his passion upwards" (or downwards), otherwise the
spectator cannot enter into his passion. The ideal of performance provided
through the spectator position, and focused on the direct sympathy with the
agent which generates propriety, provides the standard against which the
resentment of the victim is measured. Resentment is concerned with whether the
victim has been hurt, and perhaps how much or in what way the victim has
been hurt. These latter are important in categorizing the offence in order to tell
us e.g. whether words were spoken with enough intimidation to characterise
them as an assault or just banter. Nonetheless, this is merely what prompts
punishment, not its measure.
Conclusion
My main purpose, in attempting to explicate Smith's theory of
punishment has been to try to defend him from the charge of incoherence
levelled against him. He has, I would agree, an almost over-complicated theory
of moral judgement, but one which is vital and responsive to the pressures
brought to bear on an agent by society at large. But I believe that the idea that
Smith viewed morality as dynamic, almost economic exchange helps, not only
the operation of sympathy and moral judgement, but allows us to see a much
closer link between the Theory of Moral Sentiments and the Wealth of Nations.
Much remains to be said about this, as about his theory of justice. However, I
do believe that to view Smith's moral theory from an "economic" perspective as
a system of "sympathetic exchange" enables a more fruitful and cohesive
account to be given of all aspects of his work, moral, economic and
jurisprudential.
