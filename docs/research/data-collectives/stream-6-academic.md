## The academy's verdict: strong theory, thin empirics, every form fragile

The literature **proposes** collective data governance far more confidently than
it **demonstrates** it works. Claims that collectives *solve* the collective-
action / coalition-stability problem were **refuted in verification** (3 of 4
refuted claims concerned the "does it work / under what conditions" question).
The proposal literature is rich; the **outcome literature is nearly absent.**
This is the same pattern as Stream 0 (failure-heavy record) and Stream 1
(substitutes problem) — convergent across dimensions.

## Three intellectual lineages

1. **Ostrom commons → Governing Knowledge Commons (GKC).** Hess & Ostrom (2007,
   MIT Press) bridge Ostrom's common-pool-resource governance to information;
   Frischmann/Madison/Strandburg (Cornell L Rev 2010; Oxford 2014) formalize it.
   Data governance is a **constructed institution** (neither market nor
   spontaneous order) requiring collective action. Ostrom's **8 design
   principles** (boundaries, congruence, collective-choice, monitoring, graduated
   sanctions, conflict resolution, recognition of rights, nested enterprises) are
   the concrete governance checklist a collective must satisfy. →
   `[[sources/web-2014-08-29-013]]`, `[[sources/web-2022-09-01-460]]`.
2. **Data-as-labor / data-dignity.** Lanier (2013); Posner & Weyl *Radical
   Markets* (2018); Arrieta-Ibarra/Lanier/Weyl et al., "Should We Treat Data as
   Labor?" (AEA P&P 2018). Individual data rights are **structurally
   insufficient**; reframe data as compensable labor and organize **data unions /
   mediators** for collective bargaining against monopsonistic platforms. →
   `[[sources/web-2018-05-10-3b3]]`. (*Radical Markets* book + radicalmarkets.com
   dead host — by reference.)
3. **MIT / Pentland data-cooperative-as-fiduciary** (Hardjono & Pentland 2019;
   "New Deal on Data"). The cooperative holds a **fiduciary** duty to members and
   runs queries via OPAL move-the-code-to-the-data. *(MIT IDE PDF + arxiv
   1912.06229 wouldn't ground; covered via Ada Lovelace `[[concepts/data-trust]]`
   + `[[concepts/mit-open-algorithms]]`; cite Hardjono & Pentland 2019 by ref.)*

Recent synthesis: **Vincent, Prewitt & Li (2025)** argue collective data
bargaining can counteract AI-driven capital concentration. →
`[[sources/arxiv-2506.10272]]`.

## The load-bearing critical scholarship

- **Jonker (2024/25), "Data protection beyond data rights"** (Internet Policy
  Review) — individual rights fail because data value is **collective and
  co-created**; but intermediaries are **powerless "without institutional
  facilitation of coordination"** (i.e., enabling regulation may be a
  precondition). → `[[sources/web-2023-09-05-c31]]` →
  `[[concepts/privacy-externalities]]`, `[[concepts/surveillance-capitalism]]`.
- **Duncan (2023), "Through thick and thin: data commons… collective data
  governance"** (TechReg) — each intermediary form faces a distinct fragility:
  trusts (scalability conflicts), DAOs (decision fatigue), cooperatives (capital
  disadvantage), unions (non-existence as formal actors). →
  `[[sources/web-2025-06-02-56c]]`.

## Read for Task 10

The academy **diagnoses** the problem well (individual rights insufficient; value
is collective) and supplies a **governance blueprint** (Ostrom/GKC principles).
But it offers **no demonstrated, durable, at-scale success** and names a fatal
fragility for every organizational form. Combined with Stream 1 (substitutes) +
Stream 5 (no funded mechanism), the academic verdict is: **collective data
governance is theoretically sound and practically unproven; success appears to
require enabling institutional/legal coordination that does not yet exist for a
commercial cross-competitor pool.** Conditions for success are an **untested
hypothesis**, not an established result.

## Open questions (→ Task 10)

1. Empirical: any operating cooperative/trust/union that achieved coalition
   stability + extracted better terms at scale, and under what conditions? (nearly absent)
2. Falsifiable success conditions — sectorally-concentrated high-value data vs
   atomized low-value streams? (intuitive but untested)
3. Is enabling regulation a necessary precondition (Jonker)? — bears on the
   Canada data-mobility-right lever (Stream 5).
