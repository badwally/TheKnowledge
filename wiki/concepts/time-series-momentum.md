# Time Series Momentum

## Summary

An asset pricing anomaly in which a security's own past returns positively predict its future returns, documented by Moskowitz, Ooi, and Pedersen (2011) across 58 liquid futures and forward contracts in equity indices, currencies, commodities, and sovereign bonds over more than 25 years of data [[sources/pdf-lasse-pedersen-2011-title-how-to]]. The effect persists for 1 to 12 months and partially reverses over longer horizons, consistent with sentiment theories of initial under-reaction and delayed over-reaction [[sources/pdf-lasse-pedersen-2011-title-how-to]]. A diversified portfolio of time series momentum strategies yields a Sharpe ratio greater than one — roughly 2.5 times the market Sharpe ratio — with little exposure to standard asset pricing factors, and performs best during extreme market moves [[sources/pdf-lasse-pedersen-2011-title-how-to]].

## Key claims

- The past 12-month excess return of each instrument is a positive predictor of its future return; this "trend" effect persists for about a year and then partially reverses over longer horizons [[sources/pdf-lasse-pedersen-2011-title-how-to]].
- 12-month time series momentum profits are positive not just on average across assets, but for every single asset contract examined — 58 in total [[sources/pdf-lasse-pedersen-2011-title-how-to]].
- The findings are robust across sub-samples, look-back periods, and holding periods [[sources/pdf-lasse-pedersen-2011-title-how-to]].
- Time series momentum is related to but different from cross-sectional momentum: cross-sectional momentum focuses on the relative performance of securities against their peers, while time series momentum focuses purely on a security's own past return [[sources/pdf-lasse-pedersen-2011-title-how-to]].
- Positive auto-covariance in futures contracts' returns drives most of both the time series and cross-sectional momentum effects; the contribution of serial cross-correlations (lead-lag effects) and variation in mean returns is small [[sources/pdf-lasse-pedersen-2011-title-how-to]].
- Time series momentum captures the returns associated with individual stock cross-sectional momentum — most notably Fama and French's UMD factor — despite being constructed from a completely different set of securities, indicating strong correlation structure between the two [[sources/pdf-lasse-pedersen-2011-title-how-to]].
- The correlations of time series momentum strategies across asset classes are larger than the correlations of the asset classes themselves, suggesting a stronger common component to time series momentum across different assets than is present among the assets themselves [[sources/pdf-lasse-pedersen-2011-title-how-to]].
- A diversified portfolio of time series momentum across all assets yields a Sharpe ratio greater than one on an annual basis, or roughly 2.5 times the market Sharpe ratio, with little correlation to passive benchmarks in each asset class or a host of standard asset pricing factors [[sources/pdf-lasse-pedersen-2011-title-how-to]].
- The abnormal returns do not appear to be compensation for crash risk or tail events; rather, returns are largest when the stock market's returns are most extreme — performing best when the market experiences large up and down moves — making time series momentum a potential hedge for extreme events [[sources/pdf-lasse-pedersen-2011-title-how-to]].
- Decomposing returns into spot price changes and roll yield, both components contribute to time series momentum, but only spot price changes are associated with long-term reversals — consistent with investors overreacting to information in the spot market while hedging pressure is more long-lived [[sources/pdf-lasse-pedersen-2011-title-how-to]].
- The partial reversal at longer horizons is consistent with sentiment theories predicting initial under-reaction followed by delayed over-reaction, as formalized by Barberis, Shleifer, and Vishny (1998), Daniel, Hirshleifer, and Subrahmanyam (1998), and Hong and Stein (1999) [[sources/pdf-lasse-pedersen-2011-title-how-to]].
- The relatively short duration of predictability (less than a year) and the magnitude of the return premium present significant challenges to the random walk hypothesis and perhaps also to the efficient market hypothesis [[sources/pdf-lasse-pedersen-2011-title-how-to]].
