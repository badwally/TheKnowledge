# Predictive Processing

## Summary

Predictive processing is the framework that treats brains as essentially prediction machines — bundles of cells that support perception and action by constantly attempting to match incoming sensory inputs with top-down expectations or predictions, achieved using a hierarchical generative model that aims to minimize prediction error within a bidirectional cascade of cortical processing [[sources/pdf-5f41a1d2e45f]]. Clark's 2013 BBS target article concludes that this "hierarchical prediction machine" approach "offers the best clue yet to the shape of a unified science of mind and action" [[sources/pdf-5f41a1d2e45f]].

## Key claims

- Perception, action, attention, and learning are unified under a single computational story whose currency is prediction error: perception and action work together to reduce prediction error by sculpting and selecting sensory inputs [[sources/pdf-5f41a1d2e45f]].
- The framework follows Helmholtz (1860) in depicting perception as probabilistic, knowledge-driven inference, in which sensory systems infer sensory causes from their bodily effects rather than reading the world off bottom-up cues [[sources/pdf-5f41a1d2e45f]].
- A bidirectional hierarchical structure allows the system to infer its own priors as it goes along: the best current model at one level provides the priors for the level below, via an "iterative estimation" procedure (Dempster et al. 1977; Neal & Hinton 1998) that co-evolves priors and models across linked layers — a version of "empirical Bayes" (Robbins 1956) [[sources/pdf-5f41a1d2e45f]].
- The approach was developed by, among others, Rao and Ballard (1999), Lee and Mumford (2003), and Friston (2005), and extended to action by Friston and colleagues (Friston & Stephan 2007; Friston et al. 2009; Friston 2010; Brown et al. 2011) [[sources/pdf-5f41a1d2e45f]].
