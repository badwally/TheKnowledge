# Mortgage rate fixation

## Summary

The share of household mortgages that is fixed-rate versus variable-rate (and the average duration of any fix) determines how quickly a rate-hike cycle's pain arrives at consumer disposable income — making it a structural cross-country lever in monetary transmission [[sources/pdf-2b2d34f2b0ff]].

## Key claims

- In the US, only about 5% of mortgages are on adjustable rates today, compared with over 20% in 2007; many households also locked in low rates in the 2020 refi wave (US 30-year hit 2.8%), so unless they move, their disposable income is not impacted by the recent rate increase [[sources/pdf-2b2d34f2b0ff]].
- In the UK, variable-rate mortgages account for only 14% today versus 70% at the start of the 2005 tightening cycle — but a further 25% of UK mortgages are fixed for only two years, making the UK more vulnerable than the US, with a delay [[sources/pdf-2b2d34f2b0ff]].
- In the larger continental-European countries fewer households have a mortgage at all, and household savings as a percentage of GDP are higher than in the US or UK, so rising rates raise disposable income for cash-savers — partially offsetting the mortgage-holder demand drag [[sources/pdf-2b2d34f2b0ff]].
- The ECB was often warned that zero interest rates would be counterproductive precisely because of the savings-share asymmetry in continental Europe [[sources/pdf-2b2d34f2b0ff]].
