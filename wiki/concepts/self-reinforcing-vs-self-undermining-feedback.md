# Self-Reinforcing vs Self-Undermining Policy Feedback

## Summary

Galvin and Thurston use this distinction — drawing on Oberlander and Weaver (2015), Weaver (2010), and related scholarship — to argue that policy feedbacks generating self-reinforcing dynamics are "but one of many possible future courses a policy might take," with self-undermining or negative feedbacks just as plausible an outcome [[sources/pdf-5e6d63eac790]].

## Key claims

- Sometimes policies fail to take hold in the first place; other times they produce self-undermining dynamics or "negative feedback effects" that cause them to "unravel" over time or be "eroded or reversed" (Oberlander and Weaver 2015: 40; Weaver 2010; Dagan and Teles 2015; Campbell 2011; Patashnik 2008; Patashnik and Zelizer 2013, as cited) [[sources/pdf-5e6d63eac790]].
- Policy makers may have little ability to influence which of these paths a policy takes, since the nature of feedback may be shaped by contextual factors including the degree of support at outset, the partisan context during enactment and implementation, design features of the policy itself, and administrative challenges (Pierson 1993; Mayhew 2002; Maltzman and Shipan 2008; Berry, Burden, and Howell 2010; Campbell 2012; Oberlander and Weaver 2015; Patashnik and Zelizer 2013; Jacobs and Mettler 2017, as cited) [[sources/pdf-5e6d63eac790]].
- Political polarization may also undermine the routine "policy maintenance" that is needed for policies to function in a crowded "policyscape" (Mettler 2016, as cited) [[sources/pdf-5e6d63eac790]].
- Negative experiences with a policy can erode support among its constituents — Soss (1999) on welfare and Weaver and Lerman (2010) on citizens' experiences with the carceral state are cited as evidence [[sources/pdf-5e6d63eac790]].
- Even when Democrats deliberately used welfare reform to move mass opinion toward investment in anti-poverty programs, the effort failed to significantly change public opinion (Soss and Schramm 2007, as cited) [[sources/pdf-5e6d63eac790]].
- The Medicare case is hailed as the archetypal example of self-reinforcing feedback — Lyndon B. Johnson signed Medicare into law in 1964 "in spite of the opposition of the medical community" and wagered that senior citizens would mobilize in continued support (Patashnik and Zelizer 2013: 1071–1072, as cited) — but the source frames it as one outcome among many, not the default [[sources/pdf-5e6d63eac790]].
