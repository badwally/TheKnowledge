# Submerged Policies

## Summary

A category of public policies — drawn from the political-science literature on visibility and traceability — characterized by the difficulty constituencies have linking the benefits they receive to the government program that delivered them; Galvin and Thurston use the concept to explain why even popular policies often fail to mobilize supportive constituencies on a scale relevant to party-building [[sources/pdf-5e6d63eac790]].

## Key claims

- It is difficult for constituencies to form when policies lack visibility or traceability to the government — a condition that applies to many of the "submerged policies" that make up the contemporary American policy landscape (Arnold 1990; Mettler 2011; Morgan and Campbell 2011, as cited) [[sources/pdf-5e6d63eac790]].
- Thurston (2015) and Thurston (Forthcoming) are cited as qualifying the visibility/traceability requirement [[sources/pdf-5e6d63eac790]].
- Visibility and traceability can themselves undermine support, as the Affordable Care Act case illustrates: ACA opinion became more favorable for those who enrolled in state insurance marketplaces and more negative for those who did not gain insurance (Jacobs and Mettler 2017, as cited) [[sources/pdf-5e6d63eac790]].
- Some of the decline in favorability toward the ACA came among people living in states that had opted out of Medicaid expansion — voters may have incorrectly attributed the failure of a highly visible federal program to the creators of the program rather than to the state governments that decided not to implement it [[sources/pdf-5e6d63eac790]].
- Obama's 2009 stimulus illustrates submergence by design: funds went to "helping states avoid drastic cuts in public services and public employees; unemployment benefits, food stamps, and other assistance for victims of the downturn; and tax cuts for 95 percent of American workers," and public-works money went "more toward fixing stuff that needed fixing… than building new stuff" — the stimulus financed 22,000 miles of road improvements and only 230 miles of new roads in its first year (Plotz 2012, as cited) [[sources/pdf-5e6d63eac790]].
