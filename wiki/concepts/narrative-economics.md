# Narrative economics

## Summary

Narrative economics is the field — championed by Robert Shiller — that argues economic theory must incorporate the contagion of stories affecting individual and collective economic behavior; Bhargava, Lou, Ozik, Sadka, and Whitmore (2022) operationalize the program empirically by quantifying narratives from large-scale digital media coverage and showing that narrative indicators contain predictive information for market returns beyond traditional macro indicators [[sources/pdf-19445750a197]].

## Key claims

- Shiller (2019) advocates that to improve understanding of economy and financial markets, economists must look beyond typical economic indicators to incorporate stories that affect individual and collective economic behavior [[sources/pdf-19445750a197]].
- Humanities and social-sciences disciplines have long appreciated the importance of stories in explaining historical events; economic research has primarily relied on modeling tools that focus on feedback loops and multiplier effects and has lagged in incorporating prevailing narratives [[sources/pdf-19445750a197]].
- Recent traction for the field is in part driven by Shiller's Narrative Economics (2019), expanding on previous work spanning decades including Shiller (1984), and has called for a more serious quantitative analysis of narratives [[sources/pdf-19445750a197]].
- Empirically, narratives are challenging to study because they "are deeply human phenomena" — quantification entails reducing complex and multi-dimensional narratives to numbers fed to statistical modeling [[sources/pdf-19445750a197]].
- Earlier empirical papers used smaller datasets and simple word-frequency measures: Tetlock (2007) constructed a measure of media pessimism from a single Wall Street Journal column over 1984–1999; Shiller (2019) used Google Ngram and ProQuest word frequencies; Manela and Moreira (2017) constructed a text-based uncertainty measure from front-page WSJ articles starting in 1890 and found high-uncertainty periods are associated with high future stock returns [[sources/pdf-19445750a197]].
- Recent NLP and machine-learning advances have allowed larger unstructured datasets: Mai and Pukthuanthong (2021) extract Shiller narratives from nearly seven million New York Times articles over 150 years and find a Panic narrative is most important for predicting market returns; Calomiris and Mamaysky (2019) group Reuters words from 1996–2015 into topic clusters (markets, governments, corporations, commodities, credit) and show predictive ability is context-specific; Engle et al. (2020) construct a climate-change news index from media coverage and use it to build a hedging mimicking portfolio [[sources/pdf-19445750a197]].
- Bhargava et al. (2022) position their contribution as constructing narrative indicators for a large set of narratives from a novel dataset built on thousands of digital media articles, developing an empirical framework to identify which narratives drive markets, and applying these indicators to asset allocation and narrative-hedging portfolios [[sources/pdf-19445750a197]].
