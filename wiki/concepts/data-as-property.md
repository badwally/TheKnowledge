# Data as Property

## Summary

"Data as property" is the contested legal-theoretic claim that data — the content of information, as distinct from its physical embodiment or any underlying copyrightable work — is or should be subject to property rights [[sources/web-2019-01-23-bbd]]. The framing is increasingly invoked in connection with the data generated by connected cars, industrial machines, AI systems, toys, and other IoT devices, in which numerous parties (device users, manufacturers, third parties, regulators) have overlapping interests [[sources/web-2019-01-23-bbd]].

## Key claims

- Stakeholders in digital markets routinely frame data-access disputes as ownership questions: businesses assert and demand that they own data, individual data subjects assume they own data about themselves, and policy makers and scholars debate how to redistribute ownership rights to data [[sources/web-2019-01-23-bbd]].
- The conceptual referent of "data" — the content of information — exists separately from three categories that ARE subject to property rights: works of authorship and databases (intellectual property), physical embodiments of information such as data on a computer chip (personal property), and the physical objects or intangible items to which information relates [[sources/web-2019-01-23-bbd]].
- The rationale lawmakers historically used to justify property rights — incentivizing investment and improvement in works of authorship, databases, land, and chattels — does not apply to data as such, because that rationale presumes a discrete improvable item to which property rights can attach [[sources/web-2019-01-23-bbd]].
- US and EU law, despite notably divergent attitudes to privacy, property, and individual freedoms, both deliberately refrain from granting property rights in data, protecting competing interests through an "intricate net" of existing laws instead [[sources/web-2019-01-23-bbd]].
- Determann (2019) argues that new property rights in data are not suited to promote better privacy, innovation, or technological advances, and would more likely suffocate free speech, information freedom, science, and technological progress [[sources/web-2019-01-23-bbd]].
- The normative conclusion of the "no one owns data" position is that the rationales for propertizing data are not compelling and are outweighed by the rationales for keeping data "open," so no new property rights need to be created for data [[sources/web-2019-01-23-bbd]].
