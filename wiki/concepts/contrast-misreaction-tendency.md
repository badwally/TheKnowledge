# Contrast-Misreaction Tendency

## Summary

Munger's sixteenth psychological tendency: because the nervous system registers contrast in what is perceived rather than absolute scientific units, cognition — like perception — is routinely misled by mere contrast, causing errors ranging from overpaying for car accessories to the "boiling frog" pattern in which consecutively small steps toward disaster each present so small a contrast that the brain fails to register the cumulative trend [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].

## Key claims

- "Few psychological tendencies do more damage to correct thinking" [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- Small-scale: a man buys an overpriced $1,000 leather dashboard because the price seems low compared to a concurrent $65,000 car purchase [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- Large-scale: a woman with terrible parents marries a man who would be judged satisfactory only in comparison to those parents, ruining her life [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- Real estate brokers deliberately show three awful houses at ridiculously high prices, then a merely bad house at a moderately too-high price — and "boom, the broker often makes an easy sale" [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- Retailers create highly artificial high prices then advertise their standard price as a big reduction; even when people know this manipulation is being attempted, it often still triggers buying [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- The "boiling frog" pattern: "When a man's steps are consecutively taken toward disaster, with each step being very small, the brain's Contrast-Misreaction Tendency will often let the man go too far toward disaster to be able to avoid it" because each step presents so small a contrast from his present position [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- Ben Franklin's aphorism captures the principle: "A small leak will sink a great ship" — and the brain so often misses the functional equivalent of such a small leak [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- The perceptual basis: a magician removes a wristwatch without the wearer feeling it by applying high-contrast touch pressure elsewhere on the body, "swamping" the wrist pressure below perception; the hot/cold water hand experiment demonstrates the same contrast error in temperature perception [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
