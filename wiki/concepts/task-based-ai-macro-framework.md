# Task-Based AI Macro Framework

## Summary

A framework from Acemoglu and Restrepo (2018, 2019b, 2022), applied in Acemoglu (2024) to evaluate the macroeconomic effects of AI, in which production requires a continuum of tasks allocated between capital and labor based on comparative advantage; AI's effects are decomposed into four channels — extensive-margin automation, task complementarity, deepening of automation, and new task creation — and macro consequences are disciplined by Hulten's theorem [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].

## Key claims

- Production of a unique final good requires a set of tasks with measure N, combined via a CES production function with elasticity of substitution sigma between tasks [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- Each task can be produced by either capital or labor, which are perfect substitutes within a task but have different task-specific productivity schedules; labor has comparative advantage in higher-indexed tasks [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- A threshold I partitions tasks: those at or below I are produced by capital (automated), those above by labor [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- Automation (increase in I) has two opposing effects on wages: a positive productivity effect and a negative displacement effect; the net effect is ambiguous in general [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- Task complementarities (increases in labor productivity gamma_L(z) in labor-intensive tasks) may not raise wages much when sigma < 1, and always reduce the labor share — just like automation [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- The empirically plausible value of sigma is approximately 0.5, per Humlum (2021), which is close to the US capital share of about 0.4, implying that task complementarities will have very limited wage effects [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- New task creation (increase in N) always increases wages and raises the labor share of national income, making it the only channel that unambiguously benefits labor [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- Consumer welfare is proportional to TFP divided by (1 minus capital share); GDP increases from rising capital-output ratios overstate welfare gains because the extra investment comes out of consumption [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
