# Strategy Count vs Net Sharpe Ratio

## Summary

The mathematical relationship — used by ClearAlpha as the central justification for the multi-manager structure — that increasing the number of lowly correlated strategies in a fund raises its Net Sharpe Ratio, such that a portfolio of 20 strategies each with a standalone Net Sharpe of 0.75 can yield a fund Sharpe Ratio of over 2.0 [[sources/pdf-7663c24d3159]].

## Key claims

- The success of the multi-manager model depends less on accurately forecasting individual portfolio managers' performance and more on reaping the advantages of diversification by implementing numerous, mostly independent strategies simultaneously [[sources/pdf-7663c24d3159]].
- A multi-manager fund comprising 20 lowly correlated strategies — each having a standalone Net Sharpe Ratio of 0.75 — would yield a multi-manager fund Sharpe Ratio of over 2.0 [[sources/pdf-7663c24d3159]].
- It is not necessary for a multi-manager fund to have a single "rock star" portfolio manager (defined as a manager who consistently delivers a Net Sharpe Ratio of 2.0 or more) for the fund overall to deliver a Net 2.0 Sharpe Ratio [[sources/pdf-7663c24d3159]].
- For this to be mathematically true, it is critical that the strategies are very lowly correlated to one another [[sources/pdf-7663c24d3159]].
- The model assumptions used to generate the relationship: average strategy Net Sharpe Ratio of 0.75, average strategy volatility of 10% annualized, fixed fund expenses of 5% per year, 20% incentive allocation, and 3x leverage; the relationship — that Net Sharpe Ratio increases as a function of number of strategies — holds regardless of these specific assumptions as long as the strategies are not perfectly correlated [[sources/pdf-7663c24d3159]].
