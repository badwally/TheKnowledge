# Rock Star PM Myth

## Summary

ClearAlpha's argument that the success of multi-manager funds does not rely on having "rock star" portfolio managers — defined as managers who consistently deliver a Net Sharpe Ratio of 2.0 or more — but on the structural diversification math of running many lowly correlated strategies simultaneously [[sources/pdf-7663c24d3159]].

## Key claims

- ClearAlpha defines a "rock star" portfolio manager as one who consistently delivers a Net Sharpe Ratio of 2.0 or more [[sources/pdf-7663c24d3159]].
- Counterintuitively, the success of multi-manager funds does not rely on having rock star portfolio managers; while it is crucial to have portfolio managers capable of generating profits, the presence of rock star managers is not as vital as commonly thought [[sources/pdf-7663c24d3159]].
- High-performing managers are beneficial but cannot account for the intrinsic advantages of multi-manager fund structures that drive outperformance compared to other hedge fund structures [[sources/pdf-7663c24d3159]].
- Rock star managers may not consistently perform well, yet multi-manager funds have historically outperformed through numerous iterations of portfolio manager cohorts [[sources/pdf-7663c24d3159]].
- Predicting whether a portfolio manager will perform well ahead of time is extremely difficult — many would argue impossible — because markets evolve and strategies that have worked historically can face difficult or different environments going forward; ClearAlpha invokes Niels Bohr's epigraph "Prediction is very difficult, especially about the future" to make the point [[sources/pdf-7663c24d3159]].
