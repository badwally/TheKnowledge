# Demand- vs. Supply-Driven Inflation Framework

## Summary

The analytical decomposition — laid out in Apollo Chief Economist Torsten Sløk's December 2022 "2023 Economic and Capital Markets Outlook" — that the 2021–22 wave of US inflation can be attributed to a combination of demand-driven factors (the Covid fiscal response: stimulus checks, expanded unemployment benefits, childcare tax credits, PPP loans) and supply-driven factors (Covid-era supply-chain disruption, cost of imported goods especially from China, and shipping costs from ports to trucking), with profound implications for the right policy response [[sources/pdf-d196e853ebc0]].

## Key claims

- Apollo argues the upward pressure on US inflation "is the result of a combination of demand- and supply-driven factors" [[sources/pdf-d196e853ebc0]].
- Those who think inflation is mostly demand-driven point to the fiscal response to Covid in the US: stimulus checks, higher unemployment benefits, childcare tax credits, and Paycheck Protection Program (PPP) loans — fiscal stimulus → higher disposable income → excess demand [[sources/pdf-d196e853ebc0]].
- Under the demand-driven view, the policy solution is "demand destruction," something the Federal Reserve and other central banks have been trying to engineer through rate increases [[sources/pdf-d196e853ebc0]].
- Those who think inflation is mostly supply-driven point to Covid-era supply-chain disruption: increased cost of imported goods, especially from China; increased costs of shipping from ports to trucking and otherwise [[sources/pdf-d196e853ebc0]].
- Under the supply-driven view, the rational course would have been for policymakers to just wait for those issues to sort themselves out, rather than tighten financial conditions [[sources/pdf-d196e853ebc0]].
- Apollo argues neither view alone is sufficient: the near-identical paths of US and EU inflation despite a roughly 2x-larger US fiscal response "strongly suggests that price rises have not been entirely driven by demand but at least partly by supply-chain problems associated with Covid as well" [[sources/pdf-d196e853ebc0]].
- A meta-point Apollo emphasizes: both arguments seek to explain the past — neither has been able to use its understanding to accurately predict future inflation, with consensus forecasts of lower US inflation having been repeatedly wrong over 2021–22 [[sources/pdf-d196e853ebc0]].
- Apollo attributes the forecasting failure to "a series of unexpected and monumental exogenous shocks to the economy — the Covid pandemic, unprecedented monetary stimulus, the war in Ukraine" that have flummoxed even the most finely tuned forecasting models [[sources/pdf-d196e853ebc0]].
