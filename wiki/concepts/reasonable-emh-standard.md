# Reasonable-Man Standard for EMH Tests

## Summary

Cliff Asness and John Liew's proposed reform of EMH testing — borrowing law's "reasonable man" standard to rule out unreasonable models that rationalize any apparent anomaly under the joint hypothesis — articulated in their 2014 Institutional Investor piece and reprised in "The Less-Efficient Market Hypothesis" [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].

## Key claims

- Just as in law a "reasonable man" standard is common, Asness and Liew (2014) propose it should be in deciding what models are reasonable to pair with EMH [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].
- The standard is admittedly subjective, but it rules out clearly unreasonable rationalizations — Asness's example: if companies with blue logos statistically outperform and your model is "people hate blue logos so those companies trade cheap and outperform," you are not being reasonable, and barring some other nuance the finding should be called an inefficiency [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].
- Even more substantively, "people like to talk about these stocks at cocktail parties so they overpay for them" might in some grand meta sense be "rational" if investors derive enjoyment from party chatter — but if that's what you mean by an efficient market, "you ain't being reasonable or useful" [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].
- "While it may be true that 'de gustibus non est disputandum,' at some point we have to call some tastes irrational, or the word irrational has no meaning" [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].
- Fama and French (2005) formalized this notion, showing that investor tastes for things other than risk and return will affect prices and expected returns going forward, except in the highly unlikely case where they exactly offset [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].
