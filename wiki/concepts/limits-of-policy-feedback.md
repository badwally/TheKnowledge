# Limits of Policy Feedback as a Party-Building Tool

## Summary

Galvin and Thurston's three-part argument — sketched in WP-18-16 (June 2018) — that the intellectual basis for thinking policies are good vehicles for building electoral majorities, or good substitutes for the more tedious work of organizational party-building, is "quite thin" [[sources/pdf-5e6d63eac790]].

## Key claims

- Argument 1: Policies do not always, or even very often, generate their own political supports [[sources/pdf-5e6d63eac790]].
- Argument 2: Even when policies do generate their own political supports, there is little reason to think they will cement partisan loyalties [[sources/pdf-5e6d63eac790]].
- Argument 3: Although policy-building and party-building are symbiotic and mutually dependent, they do fundamentally different things [[sources/pdf-5e6d63eac790]].
- Linking favored policies to parties requires a three-step cognitive chain — voters (a) link policies they like to the party most responsible, (b) develop strong party attachments as a result, and (c) translate those attachments into reliable voting behavior — and existing research suggests each step poses formidable obstacles [[sources/pdf-5e6d63eac790]].
- Most citizens have a very limited understanding of how policies operate, many are not aware of the policy benefits they receive, and few are able to identify which party is responsible for a given policy (Converse 1964; Iyengar and Kinder 1987; Zaller 1992; Delli Carpini and Keeter 1996; Mettler 2011, as cited) [[sources/pdf-5e6d63eac790]].
- Causation more often runs in the opposite direction: citizens' policy views are shaped by their partisanship via motivated reasoning rather than the reverse (Kunda 1990; Taber and Lodge 2006; Achen and Bartels 2016, as cited) [[sources/pdf-5e6d63eac790]].
- Obama's policy-centered approach is the empirical anchor: under his presidency Democrats lost 63 House seats, 11 Senate seats, 10 governorships, 27 state legislative chambers, and almost 1,000 state legislative seats, leaving Democrats with unified government in only 6 states by January 2017 — their lowest count since the Civil War [[sources/pdf-5e6d63eac790]].
- A year after the American Reinvestment and Recovery Act, a Pew survey found that almost two-thirds of respondents did not believe the $787 billion package created jobs; even among Democrats, only 51 percent thought it had contributed to job creation (Barr 2010, as cited) [[sources/pdf-5e6d63eac790]].
