# Great Society fiscal program

## Summary

LBJ's May 1964 agenda for an ambitious series of policy initiatives and programs aimed at ending poverty, reducing crime, and abolishing inequality; per Kai Volatility's Q2 2022 commentary, the dual fiscal spending of the Great Society alongside the Vietnam War "laid the groundwork for the initial surge of inflation" of 1965-1980 by shifting the aggregate demand curve up faster than the U.S. economy's ability to supply, and Kai Volatility uses it as the canonical fiscal-driver-of-inflation case to argue 2020-2022 inflation is structurally similar [[sources/pdf-ed56b074fb11]].

## Key claims

- President Lyndon B. Johnson laid out his agenda for a "Great Society" in May 1964, with the goals of ending poverty, reducing crime, and abolishing inequality [[sources/pdf-ed56b074fb11]].
- Until recently, the program was the largest social reform plan in U.S. history and rivaled in fiscal spending only by FDR's New Deal of the 1930s [[sources/pdf-ed56b074fb11]].
- The other large source of fiscal spending in the federal budget at the time was the war in Vietnam — and "the dual fiscal spending on both war fronts laid the groundwork for the initial surge of inflation" [[sources/pdf-ed56b074fb11]].
- "Suddenly demand was given a big shot in the arm and the aggregate demand curve shifted up. Consumer demand began to run at a feverish pitch, as government spending simultaneously accelerated. Soon demand exceeded the economy's ability to supply and everything began to cost significantly more" [[sources/pdf-ed56b074fb11]].
- By the end of 1968, the inflation rate climbed over 5% and could no longer be ignored; both the business community and consumer groups railed against the Democratic Party and demanded action, with Republicans printing "Great Society, Funny Money" protest signs and winning the presidency in a landslide [[sources/pdf-ed56b074fb11]].
- Per Kai Volatility, the Great Society is dwarfed in size by the COVID-era fiscal response of "nearly $9 Trillion and counting," whose only contemporary rival adjusted for the size of the economy is FDR's New Deal — supporting the argument that 2020-2022 inflation is a fiscal-driven structural phenomenon, not a psychological one [[sources/pdf-ed56b074fb11]].
