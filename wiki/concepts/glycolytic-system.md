# Anaerobic Glycolytic System

## Summary

The glycolytic (anaerobic lactic) system breaks down carbohydrate without oxygen to produce [[concepts/atp|ATP]] at a moderately high rate; it dominates maximal efforts in the 30-second to 2-minute window and is the source of [[concepts/lactate|lactate]] and hydrogen-ion production during hard riding [[sources/pdf-21a07e2e3e63]].

## Key claims

- The system takes around 3–6 seconds to fully kick in once exercise intensity rises (Sahlin et al., 2014) and dominates maximal efforts up to roughly 1–2 minutes [[sources/pdf-21a07e2e3e63]].
- It runs on carbohydrate — either muscle and liver glycogen, or bloodstream glucose — broken down anaerobically through glycolysis [[sources/pdf-21a07e2e3e63]].
- Pyruvate produced by glycolysis has two fates: conversion to lactate plus H⁺ and NAD⁺, or entry into the [[concepts/aerobic-energy-system|aerobic system]] for further ATP yield with only water and CO₂ as by-products [[sources/pdf-21a07e2e3e63]].
- The split between those two fates depends on oxygen availability at the working muscle and on the muscle's aerobic capacity (Grey et al., 2014) [[sources/pdf-21a07e2e3e63]].
- Even when aerobic capacity matches pyruvate production, some pyruvate will always be converted to lactate and H⁺ — training to use more fat slows glycolysis and reduces this output (Bassett & Howley, 2000) [[sources/pdf-21a07e2e3e63]].
- Accumulating H⁺ ions make continued exercise harder and contribute to rapid fatigue, though the precise fatigue signal is now thought to be a combined lactate/H⁺/ATP message rather than acidity alone (Pollak et al., 2014) [[sources/pdf-21a07e2e3e63]].
