# Housing-led monetary transmission

## Summary

The channel through which central-bank rate hikes first bite the real economy via housing markets — higher mortgage rates crimp new housing demand, weakness ripples through construction, durable-goods spending and consumer wealth, and the resulting decline in activity tames inflation [[sources/pdf-2b2d34f2b0ff]].

## Key claims

- Housing markets are usually the first sector to react when central banks touch the monetary brake [[sources/pdf-2b2d34f2b0ff]].
- Materially higher new-mortgage rates crimp new housing demand; the weakness then ripples through global construction, spending on furniture and other household durables, and — via falling house prices — consumer spending [[sources/pdf-2b2d34f2b0ff]].
- The decline in activity is the intended effect: it should tame inflation [[sources/pdf-2b2d34f2b0ff]].
- Whether the housing channel produces a deep 2008-style bust depends on prior construction excess and lending standards: subdued construction in the prior decade and stricter loan-to-value / loan-to-income ratios on recent buyers reduce the risk of a glut driving prices materially lower [[sources/pdf-2b2d34f2b0ff]].
- US housing inventories — both new and existing single-family homes for sale, seasonally adjusted — are the headline indicator JPM AM uses to gauge oversupply risk [[sources/pdf-2b2d34f2b0ff]].
