# Hormesis

## Summary

Adaptive response in which a stressor potent enough to kill or harm in chronic high doses produces strengthening when applied in short, acute bursts; Brass Monkey frames cold as "arguably the most potent of all hormetic stressors" and positions deliberate cold exposure alongside resistance training, HIIT, and intense hill climbs as eustress that drives evolutionary-style adaptation [[sources/pdf-291109666e00]].

## Key claims

- Hormesis is based on the idea that a stressor powerful enough to kill us, applied in a short-term burst, leaves us stronger as the body adapts in preparation for next time [[sources/pdf-291109666e00]].
- Eustress is the umbrella term for "good stress" — acute high-intensity stress delivered for short periods that produces hormetic adaptation, contrasted with chronic cortisol-driven life stress [[sources/pdf-291109666e00]].
- Cold is unique among eustressors because it does not trigger a meaningful cortisol release while still producing the chemical signatures of stress (norepinephrine, adrenaline) [[sources/pdf-291109666e00]].
- Hormesis explains the doing-X-daily progression — e.g., 10 push-ups per day building to 20 within a month and possibly 100 by year's end — as the body adapting to repeated short stressors [[sources/pdf-291109666e00]].
- Modern Western life (steady 22–23°c heated environments, low movement, poor breathing) leaves the body wide open to metabolic disease by removing the hormetic stressors humans evolved with [[sources/pdf-291109666e00]].
- The Nietzsche aphorism "what doesn't kill us, makes us stronger" is Brass Monkey's colloquial summary of hormesis [[sources/pdf-291109666e00]].
- Cortisol — the chronic-stress hormone that initiates fight-or-flight and raises heart rate and blood pressure — is the contrast case that makes cold attractive: minimal cortisol increase combined with reliable catecholamine release [[sources/pdf-291109666e00]].
