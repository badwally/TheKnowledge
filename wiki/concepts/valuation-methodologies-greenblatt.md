# Valuation methodologies (Greenblatt taxonomy)

## Summary

The four-method taxonomy that Joel Greenblatt presents to his Columbia Special Situation Investing class for putting a value on a company: discounted cash flow (DCF), relative value, break-up value, and acquisition value — each with its own failure mode and use case [[sources/pdf-d0b9b7e97b5c]].

## Key claims

- Greenblatt enumerates four valuation methods explicitly: DCF or intrinsic value, relative value, break-up value, and acquisition value [[sources/pdf-d0b9b7e97b5c]].
- DCF is criticized for projection sensitivity — "the terminal value can change drastically due to small changes in assumptions" — and Greenblatt uses it primarily as a reverse-engineering reality check by working backwards from price to ask what growth rate and discount rate the market is implying [[sources/pdf-d0b9b7e97b5c]].
- Relative value is criticized because the businesses being compared are often not really similar, comparable might be over- or under-valued, and "everything might be overvalued in a sector, so you are comparing one overvalued asset with another" [[sources/pdf-d0b9b7e97b5c]].
- Break-up value is illustrated by a worked example: a company with one division earning $3/share and another losing $1/share trades at $34 with EPS of $2 (P/E of 17x), but if the loss-making business were closed it would really be trading at 11 times earnings ($34/$3) [[sources/pdf-d0b9b7e97b5c]].
- Acquisition value is the price an informed strategic buyer would pay for the business, which can exceed DCF — Greenblatt's example is a discount-brokerage account base that another broker can absorb onto its existing infrastructure, justifying a higher price than standalone economics imply [[sources/pdf-d0b9b7e97b5c]].
- Greenblatt is wary of per-unit metrics that sidestep cash flow — "I don't like to see values per subscriber or x hospital beds. I still want to see the cash flows translated from the hospital beds" [[sources/pdf-d0b9b7e97b5c]].
- Greenblatt rejects the "replicating value" framing — what it would cost to recreate the business — citing the Soviet anecdote that "the communists made square wheels because they cost the same to make as round wheels" [[sources/pdf-d0b9b7e97b5c]].
- Greenblatt explicitly frames "where the stock has traded in the past" as noise — the questions are only "what is it worth?" and "where is it today (Price)" [[sources/pdf-d0b9b7e97b5c]].
