# Benchmark-Relative Active Management

## Summary

The practice of evaluating active equity-fund managers against a market-capitalization-weighted index — most often the S&P 500 — as the canonical opportunity-cost alternative. Mauboussin and Callahan (2024) frame stock-market concentration as a structural challenge for this paradigm: when a small number of stocks drive the bulk of index returns, beating the index becomes mechanically harder, even if manager skill exists [[sources/pdf-7acc9c92267c]].

## Key claims

- Opportunity cost is the conceptual anchor for active management: when evaluating a choice you should consider the cost of passing on the next-best alternative, which for actively managed equity funds is almost always a low-expense equity index fund providing broad market exposure [[sources/pdf-7acc9c92267c]].
- The U.S. SEC requires managers of mutual funds to report results alongside an "appropriate broad-based securities market index" as a benchmark [[sources/pdf-7acc9c92267c]].
- The S&P 500 is by far the most popular benchmark for U.S. equity funds and is the default reference an active manager would scout to assess the strategy they need to beat [[sources/pdf-7acc9c92267c]].
- A high percentage of equity funds that invest in large-capitalization stocks have underperformed the S&P 500 over the past decade [[sources/pdf-7acc9c92267c]].
- Mauboussin and Callahan argue "the facts about active management are not as dire as some headlines proclaim, and manager skill exists" — but acknowledge it is hard to produce excess returns relative to an index when a small number of stocks make an outsized difference in the overall results [[sources/pdf-7acc9c92267c]].
- High stock-market concentration thus increases the structural difficulty of benchmark-relative active management, independent of any judgment about manager skill [[sources/pdf-7acc9c92267c]].
