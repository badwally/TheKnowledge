# Inconsistency-Avoidance Tendency

## Summary

Munger's fifth psychological tendency: the brain's reluctance to change its programming — manifesting as status quo bias, first-conclusion bias, commitment escalation, and extreme resistance to revising prior conclusions — which Munger argues causes some of the worst decision errors in investing, business, and public life [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].

## Key claims

- The brain "conserves programming space by being reluctant to change, which is a form of inconsistency avoidance"; few people can list a lot of bad habits they have eliminated, and early-formed habits can be appraised as destiny [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- A quickly reached conclusion (from Doubt-Avoidance Tendency), combined with resistance to changing that conclusion, "will naturally cause a lot of errors in cognition for modern man" [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- Keynes observed that it was not the intrinsic difficulty of new ideas that prevented their acceptance at his university, but that the new ideas were inconsistent with old ideas in place — Munger compares this to the human egg's shut-off device that bars additional sperm after the first one enters [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- Max Planck observed that even in physics, radically new ideas are seldom really accepted by the old guard — progress comes from a new generation less brain-blocked by previous conclusions; the same happened to Einstein, who never fully accepted quantum mechanics [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- Charles Darwin is cited as "one of the most successful users of an antidote to first conclusion bias" — he trained himself early to intensively consider evidence tending to disconfirm his hypotheses, especially ones he thought were particularly good; the opposite is now called "confirmation bias" [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- The tendency makes people "patsies" of compliance practitioners: Ben Franklin would maneuver an important man into lending him a book, after which the man would admire Franklin more — because not admiring Franklin would be inconsistent with having lent the book [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- Chinese brainwashing of Korean War prisoners used this Franklin technique as its most important feature — small step by small step, often working better than torture in altering prisoner cognition [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- The reverse is equally powerful: when maneuvered into deliberately hurting someone, one tends to disapprove or hate that person — "A man never forgets where he has buried the hatchet" — which explains prisoner abuse by guards, who reciprocate hostility and then need to justify it through increased dislike [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- Courts counteract the tendency by requiring judges and juries to hear evidence from the side they will not naturally favor before deciding — a form of forced consideration of counterarguments [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- Teaching others reinforces the teacher's own beliefs through Inconsistency-Avoidance ("see one, do one, teach one" in medical education), which is constructive in clinical training but destructive when applied to dubious political notions that students pound into their mental habits [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
