# Moving Average as Roadmap, Not Signal

## Summary

The stance — articulated by Scott Redler against the common retail critique that "moving averages don't work" — that a moving average is not by itself a trading strategy or even a signal, but a piece of context that helps the trader make decisions; trades are not entered or exited purely because of a moving-average cross, but moving averages contribute disproportionately to the trader's read of trend and risk [[sources/pdf-a0072c763cf8]].

## Key claims

- Common criticisms of moving averages — "moving averages don't work" or "everyone sees the same moving averages, so they have no value" — miss what serious technicians actually do with them [[sources/pdf-a0072c763cf8]].
- A moving average is not the same as a trading strategy, or even a signal, in serious technical practice [[sources/pdf-a0072c763cf8]].
- Redler does not buy and sell purely because of a moving average; the MA is one piece of the puzzle [[sources/pdf-a0072c763cf8]].
- Despite that disclaimer, Redler rates moving averages above headlines, economic data, earnings, and "just about any indicator you can think of" [[sources/pdf-a0072c763cf8]].
- The framing implies an asymmetry: MAs are treated as the primary trend roadmap while individual signals (earnings prints, economic surprises, headlines) are treated as second-order — a position consistent with Redler's broader claim that for a beginner building net worth, moving averages would be the #1 focus [[sources/pdf-a0072c763cf8]].
- The roadmap framing is what makes the same MA visible to everyone still useful: the value comes from disciplined, repeated use of the same set of MAs (in Redler's case the 8, 21, 50, and 200 day EMAs) to manage exposure, not from any hidden signal [[sources/pdf-a0072c763cf8]].
