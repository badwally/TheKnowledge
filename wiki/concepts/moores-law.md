# Moore's Law (as AI Progress Baseline)

## Summary

The empirical regularity, originally articulated by Gordon Moore in 1965, that hardware efficiency doubles on a roughly fixed cadence; used by Hernandez & Brown as the comparison baseline against which algorithmic efficiency in neural networks and other optimization domains is measured [[sources/pdf-danny-hernandez-2025-measuring-the-algorithmic]].

## Key claims

- Moore's Law (Moore, 1965) explains much of the advance from mainframes, to personal computers, to omnipresent smartphones, and is the canonical case of a quantifiable hardware trend [[sources/pdf-danny-hernandez-2025-measuring-the-algorithmic]].
- Over the 2012–2019 ImageNet measurement window, Moore's Law-style hardware efficiency improved by approximately 11x [[sources/pdf-danny-hernandez-2025-measuring-the-algorithmic]].
- The 44x algorithmic-efficiency improvement on AlexNet-level ImageNet performance over the same period therefore outpaces hardware efficiency over that horizon [[sources/pdf-danny-hernandez-2025-measuring-the-algorithmic]].
- Over the 21-year mixed-integer-programming benchmark Bixby tracked, Moore's Law yielded an efficiency gain of approximately 1,500x — substantially less than the algorithmic 500,000x speedup observed in MIP solvers over the same period [[sources/pdf-danny-hernandez-2025-measuring-the-algorithmic]].
- Grace (2013), cited in the paper, found that across chess, Go, physics simulations, mixed-integer programming, and SAT solvers, gains from algorithmic progress have been roughly 50% to 100% as large as those from hardware progress [[sources/pdf-danny-hernandez-2025-measuring-the-algorithmic]].
- Hernandez & Brown argue that Moore's Law alone is an insufficient model of AI progress because hardware and algorithmic efficiency gains multiply, so progress models must integrate both [[sources/pdf-danny-hernandez-2025-measuring-the-algorithmic]].
