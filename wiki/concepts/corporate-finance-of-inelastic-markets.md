# Corporate Finance of Inelastic Markets

## Summary

The corporate-finance implications of the inelastic markets hypothesis — most prominently that share buybacks materially raise aggregate equity values by approximately the multiplier, contradicting the Modigliani-Miller invariance and validating the naive intuition that firm purchases of shares drive up prices [[sources/pdf-b8cec9d1ec0c]].

## Key claims

- In the traditional Modigliani-Miller world, the market value of equities does not change at all when the corporate sector buys back $1 worth of equities rather than paying $1 worth of dividends [[sources/pdf-b8cec9d1ec0c]].
- In an inelastic world, the same $1 buyback raises aggregate equity value by a tentative estimate of around $2 [[sources/pdf-b8cec9d1ec0c]].
- The naive non-economist's intuition that "if firms buy shares, that drives up the price of shares" — which a rational financial economist might call illiterate — is qualitatively correct in inelastic markets [[sources/pdf-b8cec9d1ec0c]].
- Stock buybacks have been about as large as dividend payments in the recent decade, so corporate actions account for a sizable share of equity purchases and therefore of the volatility and increase in stock-market value [[sources/pdf-b8cec9d1ec0c]].
- Prior research has shown that firms react to price signals — for example in their decisions to issue dividends or to raise funds in stocks versus bonds (Baker and Wurgler (2004); Ma (2019)) — and the inelasticity framework now lets researchers quantify how those firm actions impact the market [[sources/pdf-b8cec9d1ec0c]].
- Gabaix and Koijen frame this as the "corporate finance of inelastic markets" research avenue [[sources/pdf-b8cec9d1ec0c]].
