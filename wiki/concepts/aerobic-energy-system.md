# Aerobic Energy System

## Summary

The aerobic system uses oxygen to convert carbohydrate and fat into [[concepts/atp|ATP]], producing only carbon dioxide and water as by-products; it is the body's preferred fuel pathway for any sustained cycling effort and the dominant supplier of energy for anything longer than a few minutes [[sources/pdf-21a07e2e3e63]].

## Key claims

- The aerobic system produces ATP the slowest of the three systems but with a virtually unlimited capacity, allowing it to sustain energy production indefinitely [[sources/pdf-21a07e2e3e63]].
- Its main fuels are carbohydrate and fat; protein contributes only marginally under nearly all circumstances [[sources/pdf-21a07e2e3e63]].
- Carbohydrate must first pass through anaerobic glycolysis to pyruvate before entering aerobic oxidation, while fatty acids enter the aerobic pathway directly (after triglycerides are broken down by enzymes) [[sources/pdf-21a07e2e3e63]].
- The fat-vs-carbohydrate mix shifts with intensity: fat oxidation is slower and dominates at low intensity, while carbohydrate dominates at high intensity (Brooks & Mercier, 1994) [[sources/pdf-21a07e2e3e63]].
- The body typically stores enough carbohydrate to fuel ~1.5 hours of all-out exercise, while even very lean athletes carry enough fat to fuel several days of riding — making fat-oxidation training useful for sparing carbohydrate [[sources/pdf-21a07e2e3e63]].
- The aerobic system also resynthesises [[concepts/phosphocreatine-system|phosphocreatine]] between hard efforts, so a stronger aerobic system speeds repeat-sprint recovery [[sources/pdf-21a07e2e3e63]].
