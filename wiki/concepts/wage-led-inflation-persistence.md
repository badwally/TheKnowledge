# Wage-led inflation persistence

## Summary

The channel by which a tight labour market — in particular job vacancies exceeding the number of unemployed — keeps wage growth elevated and prevents headline inflation from receding even as goods and energy disinflate; JPM AM identifies this as where central banks went wrong when they assumed inflation would prove "transitory" [[sources/pdf-2b2d34f2b0ff]].

## Key claims

- Central banks underestimated the extent to which labour-market tightness would result in workers asking for more pay, which is the rooted cause of their inflation forecast errors [[sources/pdf-2b2d34f2b0ff]].
- Job vacancies in all major regions still exceed the number of unemployed; the vacancy-to-unemployed ratio is the key indicator JPM AM tracks for wage-pressure relief [[sources/pdf-2b2d34f2b0ff]].
- Job hiring and quits had already begun rolling over by late 2022; given that higher pay is one of the most common reasons people change jobs, this is read as a leading sign that wage growth should ease [[sources/pdf-2b2d34f2b0ff]].
- Successive Bank of England average-weekly-earnings forecasts (Aug-21, Feb-22, Aug-22, Nov-22) revising sharply upward illustrate the central-bank misread that JPM AM treats as canonical [[sources/pdf-2b2d34f2b0ff]].
- Conditional on headline inflation and wage inflation easing, JPM AM expects US rates to peak at 4.5%–5.0% in Q1 2023, ECB at 2.5%–3.0% in Q1 2023, and UK Bank Rate at 4.0%–4.5% in Q2 2023 — the latter peaking later because UK inflation is judged stickier [[sources/pdf-2b2d34f2b0ff]].
