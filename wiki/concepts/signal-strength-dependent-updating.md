# Signal-Strength-Dependent Updating

## Summary

Theoretical framework and robust empirical finding from Augenblick, Lazarus, and Thaler (2024) that people systematically overinfer from weak information and underinfer from strong information, because they know the direction in which to update their beliefs but are imprecise about the magnitude — causing them to shrink their estimate of signal strength toward an intermediate level, which produces overreaction to weak signals and underreaction to strong signals relative to a Bayesian benchmark [[sources/pdf-ned-augenblick-2025-overinference-from-weak]].

## Key claims

- The framework rests on four high-level assumptions: that people (1) pay attention to a given piece of information, (2) can easily determine its directional meaning, (3) form reasonable estimates of its strength, and (4) are at least partially aware that this estimate is imperfect [[sources/pdf-ned-augenblick-2025-overinference-from-weak]].
- In the extreme case where a person knows a signal is positive but is completely unsure about its strength, the person updates as if the news has "intermediate" strength — always updating an intermediate amount regardless of the true signal strength — leading to overreaction to weak news and underreaction to strong news [[sources/pdf-ned-augenblick-2025-overinference-from-weak]].
- In more realistic scenarios, people have a rough but imperfect guess of signal strength; the imprecision causes them to shrink their estimate toward an intermediate strength, which on average leads to the same overreaction/underreaction pattern [[sources/pdf-ned-augenblick-2025-overinference-from-weak]].
- The theoretical result holds across a general set of information structures, estimation strategies, and possibly non-Bayesian updating rules [[sources/pdf-ned-augenblick-2025-overinference-from-weak]].
- The effect is confirmed in four empirical environments: abstract bookbag-and-poker-chips experiments (Studies 1a, 1b), a naturalistic NBA basketball experiment (Study 2), sports betting markets, and financial markets [[sources/pdf-ned-augenblick-2025-overinference-from-weak]].
- In the abstract experiments, participants' average perceived signal strength rises monotonically with the true strength, but the relationship is muted — for very weak signals (diagnosticity below 3/5), participants act as if signals are twice as strong as they truly are [[sources/pdf-ned-augenblick-2025-overinference-from-weak]].
- The effect is stronger for people who exhibit more variance in their level of over- and underinference, have less task experience, score lower on a cognitive reflection test (adapted from Frederick 2005), and state they are more uncertain about their answers (adapted from Enke and Graeber 2023) — consistent with the theory's prediction that the effect is stronger for those with less precise signal-strength estimates [[sources/pdf-ned-augenblick-2025-overinference-from-weak]].
- In Study 1b, which systematically varies the prior (1/4, 1/3, and 1/2), participants exhibit modest base-rate neglect, but the core finding about signal-strength-dependent over- and underinference is not substantially affected after disentangling base-rate biases [[sources/pdf-ned-augenblick-2025-overinference-from-weak]].
- In the naturalistic NBA basketball experiment (Study 2), the same basket scored in the first quarter (weak signal) versus the fourth quarter (strong signal) produces overreaction and underreaction respectively, with the crossover from over- to underreaction occurring on average in the third quarter [[sources/pdf-ned-augenblick-2025-overinference-from-weak]].
- In financial markets, the paper finds excess volatility when information is weak, but this effect reverses with sufficiently strong information [[sources/pdf-ned-augenblick-2025-overinference-from-weak]].
- The framework helps harmonize apparently contradictory results from the experimental literature (which commonly finds underreaction in standard updating tasks with strong signals) and the empirical literature (which finds overreaction patterns such as excess volatility in asset prices) [[sources/pdf-ned-augenblick-2025-overinference-from-weak]].
- The theory is explicitly not intended as a universal explanation for all under- and overreaction; it identifies a single important mediating factor — signal strength — that parsimoniously explains behavior across a range of common situations [[sources/pdf-ned-augenblick-2025-overinference-from-weak]].
- Important violations of the framework's assumptions include cases when people (1) simply ignore very weak information, (2) are unsure of a signal's directional meaning, (3) form systematically biased strength estimates, or (4) fail to account for estimation noise [[sources/pdf-ned-augenblick-2025-overinference-from-weak]].
