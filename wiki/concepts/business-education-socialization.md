# Business-education socialization vs. selection

## Summary

The interpretive distinction Acemoglu, He, and le Maire (2022) draw between two competing explanations for the business-manager effect on wages: (i) selection of individuals already disposed to take a hard line against labor into business degrees, vs. (ii) socialization — instruction and values acquired in business-degree programs — that produces managers who do not share rents with their employees; the paper concludes the bulk of the effect is socialization, not selection [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].

## Key claims

- The Danish data are used to explore whether business-manager effects are due to the selection of individuals more prone to take a hard line against labor into business degrees, or whether instruction and socialization in business-degree programs produce managers who do not share rents [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].
- Using the role-model IV in Denmark, most of the wage and labor-share results can be explained as the treatment effect of business education rather than a selection effect [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].
- The authors interpret these findings as capturing the impact of management practices and values imparted by business schools and business degrees [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].
- They acknowledge they do not have direct evidence on these channels but suggest that two ideas commonly propagated in business schools may significantly affect the priorities and approaches adopted by managers [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].
- A few other differences between business-degree and non-business-degree managers — for example, in Denmark business-degree managers use more debt financing and are a little more likely to adopt robots — are not major channels for the wage effects per the paper's checks [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].
