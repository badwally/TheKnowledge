# 2022 bond bear market

## Summary

ING's framing of 2022 as "the biggest bear market for bonds in modern times," with late-2022 yield reversals driven partly by position squaring as bear-market positions are unwound — but with the dominant 2023 narrative being large falls in market rates once the Fed and ECB peak in Q1 [[sources/pdf-87087198976b]]; the BlackRock Investment Institute's 2023 Global Outlook adds a structural explanation, arguing that surging bond yields reflect a regime shift — the negative stock-bond correlation has flipped positive, investors will demand rising term premium for holding long-term bonds, and central banks are shrinking their bond holdings at a time of record government deficits, forcing the private sector to absorb more supply [[sources/pdf-won-2022-bii-global-outlook]].

## Key claims

- ING characterizes 2022 as "shaping up to be the biggest bear market for bonds in modern times" [[sources/pdf-87087198976b]].
- Recent late-2022 yield reversals lower are partly explained by position squaring as a "decent rump of investors" square up bear-market positions taken in 2022, requiring the buying of both duration and risk [[sources/pdf-87087198976b]].
- ING argues this position-squaring stores up problems for the turn of the year because financial conditions, especially in the US, are prone to loosening too much, driven by falls in market rates [[sources/pdf-87087198976b]].
- Because the Fed needs tighter financial conditions, market rates should be forced back up first before the broader 2023 rally in rates [[sources/pdf-87087198976b]].
- ING's biggest 2023 narrative is one of "big falls in market rates" — once the Fed and ECB peak in Q1 2023, market rates will have a "carte blanche to anticipate future cuts" [[sources/pdf-87087198976b]].
- BII argues the bond bear market reflects a structural regime shift rather than just a cyclical tightening: repeated inflation surprises sent bond yields soaring, "crushing equities and fixed income" simultaneously — a pattern that "stands in sharp contrast to the Great Moderation" [[sources/pdf-won-2022-bii-global-outlook]].
- BII argues investors will increasingly demand higher term premium for holding long-term government bonds amid high debt levels, rising supply, and higher inflation [[sources/pdf-won-2022-bii-global-outlook]].
- BII notes that central banks are shrinking their bond holdings, Japan may stop purchases, and governments are still running deficits — meaning "the private sector needs to absorb more bonds" [[sources/pdf-won-2022-bii-global-outlook]].
- BII cites the return of "so-called bond vigilantes" — referencing the September 2022 UK gilt crisis when "market forces sparked a yield surge to punish profligate UK policies" [[sources/pdf-won-2022-bii-global-outlook]].
- BII's strongest-conviction view across all tactical scenarios is to remain underweight nominal long-term government bonds [[sources/pdf-won-2022-bii-global-outlook]].
- BII argues that despite the bear market, fixed income now finally offers "income" — short-term government bonds and mortgage securities are attractive, and investment-grade credit compensates for recession risks [[sources/pdf-won-2022-bii-global-outlook]].
