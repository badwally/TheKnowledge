# Stock Market Concentration

## Summary

The share of overall equity-market capitalization held by a small number of the largest stocks — typically reported as the cumulative weighting of the top 1, 3, or 10 companies. In a market-cap-weighted index, concentration measures how mechanically dependent index returns are on a handful of names, and is therefore a structural input to the difficulty of beating the benchmark [[sources/pdf-7acc9c92267c]].

## Key claims

- In the U.S., concentration measured as the weighting of the top 10 stocks nearly doubled from 14% to 27% over the decade ending in 2023, approaching the prior peak of 30% set in 1963 [[sources/pdf-7acc9c92267c]].
- The lowest U.S. concentration over the 1950–2023 window was 12% in 1993; the level was still 14% as recently as 2014 [[sources/pdf-7acc9c92267c]].
- Dimson, Marsh, and Staunton estimate that U.S. top-10 concentration was 38% in 1900, with a 1930s level similar to that of the early 1960s — current concentration is high relative to a decade ago but has historical precedent [[sources/pdf-7acc9c92267c]].
- The rate of increase in U.S. concentration over the last decade is the most rapid since 1950, which is the source of much of the current investor disquiet [[sources/pdf-7acc9c92267c]].
- The MSCI All Country World Index shows the same pattern: top-10 weighting roughly doubled to ~19% by year-end 2023, with 9 of the 10 largest constituents being U.S. companies [[sources/pdf-7acc9c92267c]].
- Concentration continued rising in early 2024 — to 28% in the U.S. and 20% in the MSCI All Country World Index by the end of Q1 2024 [[sources/pdf-7acc9c92267c]].
- Cross-country comparison shows the U.S. is the fourth-most-diversified major equity market — Switzerland, France, Australia, Germany, South Korea, the U.K., Taiwan, and Canada are all more concentrated, while India, Japan, and China are less concentrated; Switzerland and Finland have historically reached top-10 concentrations above 80% [[sources/pdf-7acc9c92267c]].
- One academic study of 47 equity markets from 1989 to 2011 found the average top-10 weighting was 48%, with Finland's Nokia at one point representing about two-thirds of the entire Finnish market [[sources/pdf-7acc9c92267c]].
- Mauboussin and Callahan argue the question is not only "is concentration too high now?" but also "was concentration too low before?" — they answer the latter by discounting today's top-1/3/10 caps back at the market return to derive a "theoretical" past concentration that is materially higher than what was observed [[sources/pdf-7acc9c92267c]].
- High concentration makes it structurally harder to beat a market-cap-weighted benchmark when a small number of stocks drive the bulk of the return [[sources/pdf-7acc9c92267c]].
