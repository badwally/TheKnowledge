# Role-model college-major instrumental variable

## Summary

An identification strategy used by Acemoglu, He, and le Maire (2022) in Denmark to separate the treatment effect of business education from selection into business education: defining each individual's "role models" as students from the same high school and with similar academic performance one year ahead, and using the fraction of role models pursuing a business degree as an instrument for the individual's own likelihood of enrolling in a business program [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].

## Key claims

- Role models for each individual are defined as students in the same high school and with similar academic performance, but one year ahead [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].
- The strategy draws on the literature on "role model" effects in the choice of college majors — that individuals are more likely to follow the choices of their role models — including Lockwood and Kunda (1997), Lockwood (2006), Arcidiacono and Nicholson (2005), Rask and Bailey (2002), Poldin et al. (2015), and Wiswall and Zafar (2015) [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].
- The first stage confirms that role models matter for college choice: a 10% increase in the fraction of role models pursuing a business degree is associated with a 1%–3% increase in a student's likelihood of enrolling in a business program [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].
- IV estimates indicate that most of the wage and labor-share results documented in the paper can be explained as the treatment effect of business education rather than a selection effect of individuals more prone to take a hard line against labor self-selecting into business degrees [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].
- The estimation requires high school records of Danish managers to define role models, made possible by the Danish administrative-data environment [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].
