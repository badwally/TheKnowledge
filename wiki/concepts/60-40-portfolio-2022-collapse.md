# 60/40 Portfolio Collapse (2022)

## Summary

The simultaneous drawdown in 2022 of the two pillars of the canonical 60/40 portfolio (60% public equities / 40% public bonds), which Apollo's December 2022 "2023 Economic and Capital Markets Outlook" frames as a "disastrous performance" that will push "weary and battered" investors to rotate from public 60/40 portfolios into private markets in 2023 [[sources/pdf-d196e853ebc0]]; the BlackRock Investment Institute's 2023 Global Outlook provides the structural explanation, arguing that the negative correlation between stock and bond returns has flipped positive in the new regime because central banks are engineering recessions rather than rescuing growth, making the diversification assumption underlying the 60/40 design structurally broken [[sources/pdf-won-2022-bii-global-outlook]].

## Key claims

- Apollo describes 2022 as a year of "disastrous performance of 60/40 portfolios of public equities and bonds" — the simultaneous duration shock and equity drawdown breaking the typical negative stock-bond correlation that underwrites the 60/40 design [[sources/pdf-d196e853ebc0]].
- The investor response Apollo expects in 2023 is rotation into private markets: "many investors—weary and battered after a disastrous performance of 60/40 portfolios of public equities and bonds in 2022—are likely to turn to private markets as they adjust their holdings in 2023" [[sources/pdf-d196e853ebc0]].
- Apollo argues "purchase price matters" in the post-collapse environment and sees a "historic entry point in private credit and attractive opportunities in private equity for investors able to be providers of capital in a time of stressed and distressed markets" [[sources/pdf-d196e853ebc0]].
- The narrative is institutionally self-interested — Apollo is a private-markets manager — but is internally consistent with Apollo's top-down view that capital markets in 2023 "will likely remain vulnerable" with capital scarce and expensive and high-yield primary credit markets virtually shut down [[sources/pdf-d196e853ebc0]].
- BII argues the stock-bond correlation flip is structural, not episodic: "A cornerstone of portfolio construction in recent decades was that bond prices would go up when stocks sold off. We think this correlation has broken down in the new regime" [[sources/pdf-won-2022-bii-global-outlook]].
- BII identifies the causal mechanism: central banks are unlikely to come to the rescue with rapid rate cuts in recessions they engineered to bring down inflation — and policy rates may stay higher for longer than the market expects [[sources/pdf-won-2022-bii-global-outlook]].
- BII argues investors will increasingly demand more term premium for holding long-term government bonds amid high debt levels, rising bond supply (as central banks shrink holdings and governments run deficits), and persistent inflation [[sources/pdf-won-2022-bii-global-outlook]].
- BII's response differs from Apollo's: rather than rotating to private markets, BII favors short-term government bonds, mortgage securities, and investment-grade credit for income, while remaining structurally underweight long-term government bonds [[sources/pdf-won-2022-bii-global-outlook]].
