# CPI vs. PCE Scope Differences

## Summary

The explanation, summarized in the July 13, 2022 Q3-2022 IACFM presentation, that PCE inflation has lagged CPI inflation primarily because of scope: CPI measures the change in out-of-pocket expenditures of all urban households while PCE measures the change in goods and services consumed by all households and nonprofit institutions, leading PCE to weight imputed-cost categories like healthcare and financial services more heavily and weight out-of-pocket categories like shelter and energy less heavily [[sources/pdf-853a7755a470]].

## Key claims

- Per the Bureau of Labor Statistics (quoted in the presentation): "The CPI measures the change in the out-of-pocket expenditures of all urban households and the PCE index measures the change in goods and services consumed by all households, and nonprofit institutions serving households" [[sources/pdf-853a7755a470]].
- The conceptual scope difference means some items and expenditures in the PCE index are outside the scope of the CPI — for example, expenditure weights for medical care services in the CPI are derived only from out-of-pocket expenses paid by consumers, while PCE includes services paid for on behalf of consumers (employer-provided health insurance, Medicare, Medicaid) [[sources/pdf-853a7755a470]].
- CPI / PCE category weightings reported in the presentation — out-of-pocket expenditures: Energy 8% / 5%; Food (incl. away from home) 13% / 14%; Shelter 33% / 16%; Energy + Food + Shelter 54% / 34% [[sources/pdf-853a7755a470]].
- CPI / PCE category weightings reported in the presentation — categories with imputed costs: Healthcare services 7% / 16%; Financial services 0% / 5%; Healthcare + Financial services 7% / 21% [[sources/pdf-853a7755a470]].
- PCE inflation has lagged CPI inflation primarily due to its lower weighting of out-of-pocket expenditures like shelter and energy and its higher weighting of categories that reflect imputed costs like healthcare and financial services [[sources/pdf-853a7755a470]].
- PCE inflation was not formally adopted as the Federal Reserve's target inflation measure until the January 2012 FOMC meeting; prior to 2012, both PCE and CPI inflation were referenced by FOMC participants in their discussion of the Federal Reserve's price stability mandate [[sources/pdf-853a7755a470]].
- Per the IACFM presentation, CPI more accurately represents inflation in out-of-pocket expenditures, particularly for low- to middle-income consumers; has outsized headline and media relevance compared with PCE; and is a key input variable in cost-of-living adjustments, wage negotiations, and various other lagged price escalators [[sources/pdf-853a7755a470]].
- Christopher Waller, Member of the Federal Reserve Board of Governors, said at a Virtual NABE Event on July 7, 2022 (quoted in the IACFM presentation) that he likes to look at CPI alongside the Fed's headline PCE number because "CPI is more heavily weighted towards shelter" and is more consistent with what lower and moderate income groups face — they spend a much bigger share of disposable income on shelter, food, and energy than upper income groups [[sources/pdf-853a7755a470]].
- May 2022 readings cited in the presentation: CPI MoM 1.0% (12.3% annualized; 10.7% trailing 3-month annualized), Core CPI MoM 0.6% (7.8% annualized; 6.3% trailing 3-month annualized), PCE MoM 0.3% (3.9% annualized; 6.0% trailing 3-month annualized), Core PCE MoM 0.3% (3.9% annualized; 4.1% trailing 3-month annualized) [[sources/pdf-853a7755a470]].
