# Existential Risk

## Summary

Risks that threaten human extinction or could otherwise irreversibly curtail the potential of humankind; Aschenbrenner (2020) treats existential risk as the central long-run welfare object of growth-and-risk economics, modeled as a man-made hazard rate that depends on aggregate (not per-capita) consumption and safety spending [[sources/pdf-e7ff11e4ad9b]].

## Key claims

- Existential risks are those that threaten human extinction or could otherwise irreversibly curtail the potential of humankind, such as a war that permanently sends civilization back to the Stone Age; the canonical references are Bostrom (2002), Posner (2004), and Farquhar et al. (2017) [[sources/pdf-e7ff11e4ad9b]].
- Increasing attention is being paid to existential risks that could imperil human civilization, including nuclear war, extreme climate change, bioengineered pandemics, and powerful AI that could "run amok" [[sources/pdf-e7ff11e4ad9b]].
- Some philosophers (Bostrom 2003, cited by Aschenbrenner) argue that because of the potential "astronomical" value associated with the long-run future of humanity, mitigating existential risk should be of paramount concern [[sources/pdf-e7ff11e4ad9b]].
- Aschenbrenner models existential risks as fundamentally man-made: human activity creates the risk of catastrophic climate change, dangerous AI, and nuclear war, and these risks can be mitigated by investing in safety such as pollution abatement, more reliable nuclear weapon locks ("permissive action links"), and pandemic preparedness [[sources/pdf-e7ff11e4ad9b]].
- An existential catastrophe is assumed to result in permanent zero utility thereafter — exactly valid for human extinction and a valid approximation for catastrophes that irreversibly curtail human potential [[sources/pdf-e7ff11e4ad9b]].
- The probability that civilization survives to date t under a time-varying hazard rate δ_t is M_t = exp(−∫₀^t δ_s ds), and M_∞ is greater than zero if and only if ∫₀^∞ δ_s ds is bounded [[sources/pdf-e7ff11e4ad9b]].
- Snyder-Beattie et al. (2019), cited in the paper, find that natural sources of extinction risk are negligible compared to potential anthropogenic extinction risks, supporting Aschenbrenner's focus on man-made x-risk [[sources/pdf-e7ff11e4ad9b]].
- Some risks Aschenbrenner explicitly excludes from the model include a creeping, irreversible spread of global authoritarianism (Caplan 2008); the model focuses on catastrophes that would kill most people alive at the time or reduce their utility to roughly zero [[sources/pdf-e7ff11e4ad9b]].
