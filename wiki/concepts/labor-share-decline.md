# Decline in the labor share of national income

## Summary

The documented secular fall in the share of national income paid to labor across industrialized economies since roughly 1980, with proposed drivers including capital accumulation, automation, the rise of superstar firms, growing concentration and market power, and declining union bargaining power; Acemoglu, He, and le Maire (2022) add changing managerial attitudes and practices toward rent-sharing as a major contributing factor [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]]; Acemoglu (2024) further argues that AI advances — through both automation and task complementarities — will continue reducing the labor share, with the capital share predicted to increase by about 0.31 percentage points from AI rollout alone [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].

## Key claims

- In the US, the labor share in the private nonagricultural business sector used to hover around 65% between the 1950s and the 1980s but now stands below 60% [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].
- The annual growth rate of median (real) wages in the US, which was typically above 2% between the 1950s and the 1970s, has been only 0.3% since 1980 despite significant productivity growth [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].
- Karabarbounis and Neiman (2014) document the fall in the labor share across several industrialized nations over the prior three decades [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].
- The Danish corporate sector labor share fell from 69% in 1999 to 65% in 2014, per Hémous and Olsen (2020), even though the bargaining power of labor has not receded as much in Nordic countries with strong trade unions and pro-labor institutions [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].
- Many factors have been proposed as potential drivers of these changes, including capital accumulation (Blanchard, 1997; Piketty and Zucman, 2014), automation (Acemoglu and Restrepo, 2019; Acemoglu et al., 2020), the rise of superstar firms (Autor et al., 2020), growing concentration and market power (Gutiérrez and Philippon, 2017; De Loecker et al., 2020), and the declining power of unions (DiNardo et al., 1996; Farber et al., 2021) [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].
- Acemoglu, He, and le Maire argue that changing managerial attitudes and practices toward rent-sharing have been a major contributing factor: in the US, business managers can explain about 20% of the decline in the labor share, and in Denmark about 6% [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].
- In Acemoglu's (2024) task-based framework, both AI automation and task complementarities reduce the labor share when the elasticity of substitution between tasks sigma < 1 (the empirically relevant case with sigma ≈ 0.5); only the creation of new labor-intensive tasks raises the labor share [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- Acemoglu (2024) estimates the capital share of national income will increase by about 0.31 percentage points from AI rollout, confirming that AI will further widen the capital-labor income gap [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- The current US capital share stands at approximately 0.43 for the entire private business sector [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
