# Business-manager effect on wages and labor share

## Summary

The empirical regularity, documented by Acemoglu, He, and le Maire (2022) using firm-level data from the US and Denmark, that a CEO transition from a non-business to a business-degreed manager ("business manager") is followed by significant declines in firm wages and labor share without corresponding gains in output, investment, or productivity — a relative wage cut that the paper attributes to changes in rent-sharing practices [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].

## Key claims

- Within five years of the appointment of a business manager, wages decline by 6% and the labor share by 5 percentage points in the US, and by 3% and 3 percentage points in Denmark, all relative to non-business-manager firms [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].
- Firms appointing business managers are not on differential trends and do not enjoy higher output, investment, or employment growth thereafter — there is no compelling evidence that business managers are more productive than their non-business peers [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].
- The reduction in wages following the accession of a business manager raises return on assets by 3 percentage points in the US and 1.5 percentage points in Denmark, and produces about a 5% increase in stock-market values of US companies that appoint business managers [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].
- All else equal, managers with business degrees earn more than non-business managers [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].
- A switch from one non-business manager to another non-business manager has zero impact on the focal outcomes, and a switch from a non-college manager to one with a college degree also has no effect, ruling out mechanical "Hawthorne" effects of new-manager arrival or more-educated leadership [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].
- Estimates restricted to manager retirements and deaths — arguably less endogenous than other transitions — are very similar to the baseline event-study estimates [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].
- The negative wage effects apply throughout the wage and skill distribution but are larger for low-skilled workers than for high-skilled ones in both countries [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].
- Worker quits increase following a business-manager appointment, especially for more skilled workers — though those estimates are less precise than the main results — suggesting that some of the more valuable employees leave after the new manager's reign starts [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].
- In the US, the fraction of workers employed by business managers rose from 26% to 43% between 1980 and 2020; the paper's estimates indicate that business managers can explain about 20% of the decline in the labor share and approximately 15% of the slowdown of wage growth since 1980 [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].
- In Denmark, where the fraction of workers employed by business managers rose from 11% in 1995 to 19% in 2011, the mechanism accounts for 6% of the decline in the labor share [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].
- IV estimates exploiting the diffusion of business-manager appointments within region-industry-size cells are about 50% larger than the baseline event-study estimates, suggesting the baseline numbers should be considered lower bounds because business-manager practices may diffuse to nearby firms even without an explicit business-degreed appointment [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].
