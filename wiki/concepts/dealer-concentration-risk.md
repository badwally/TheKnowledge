# Dealer concentration risk

## Summary

Dealer concentration risk is the systemic vulnerability that arises when a small number of dealers handle the majority of options and derivatives order flow; Ambrus Capital argues that current U.S. options-market concentration combined with Dodd-Frank-imposed risk limits amplifies stress events [[sources/pdf-04854302f962]].

## Key claims

- Four wholesalers handle roughly 80% of all U.S. retail options contracts, and four main banks control most institutional orders, producing significant concentration risk in the U.S. derivatives market [[sources/pdf-04854302f962]].
- Per the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency's report on bank trading and derivatives activities, in Q3 2021 four large commercial banks represented 89.3% of the total banking-industry notional amounts and 78.1% of industry net current credit exposure (NCCE) [[sources/pdf-04854302f962]].
- These banks are subject to Dodd-Frank regulations imposing hard limits on the amount of risk they can warehouse, so in times of severe market stress they will mechanically pull liquidity to remain compliant — further hurting struggling securities [[sources/pdf-04854302f962]].
- During March 2020, both options and the underlying SPX E-mini futures became highly illiquid; lack of liquidity drives prices further as dealers and investors play "pass the hot potato" while simultaneously trying to buy options to curtail risk, driving volatility higher and asset prices lower [[sources/pdf-04854302f962]].
- When an institutional trader has to hedge to stay within risk limits, this is a compliance matter — so the hedging is price-insensitive, with hedges purchased even at exorbitant prices, which further polarizes volatility [[sources/pdf-04854302f962]].
