# Training Compute Scaling for Largest AI Runs

## Summary

The trend in compute consumed by the largest frontier AI training runs, which Hernandez & Brown report grew by a factor of 300,000x between 2012 and 2018 — a divergence from Moore's-Law-paced growth that motivates separately measuring algorithmic progress [[sources/pdf-danny-hernandez-2025-measuring-the-algorithmic]].

## Key claims

- Compute used by the largest AI training runs per year grew at 300,000x between 2012 and 2018, per Amodei & Hernandez (2018) as cited and built on in the algorithmic-efficiency paper [[sources/pdf-danny-hernandez-2025-measuring-the-algorithmic]].
- This pace diverges from the historical pattern of approximately Moore's-Law-level growth for training runs and is treated by the authors as a structural break in AI economics [[sources/pdf-danny-hernandez-2025-measuring-the-algorithmic]].
- Sastry et al. (2019), cited in the paper, use this divergence to argue that policymakers should increase compute funding for academia so it can continue the kinds of AI research that are becoming more expensive [[sources/pdf-danny-hernandez-2025-measuring-the-algorithmic]].
- Better measurement of training-compute trends is presented as critical to policymakers, economists, industry leaders, and researchers trying to decide how much money and attention to invest in AI [[sources/pdf-danny-hernandez-2025-measuring-the-algorithmic]].
- Money spent on AI is also rising materially: private investment in AI startups rose from $7B in 2012 to $40B in 2018, and investment via public offerings and M&A grew from $5B to $23B over the same period [[sources/pdf-danny-hernandez-2025-measuring-the-algorithmic]].
- The U.S. DOD is projected to invest $4.0B on AI R&D in fiscal year 2020, and U.S. government contract spending on AI grew from about $150M to $728M between 2012 and 2018 [[sources/pdf-danny-hernandez-2025-measuring-the-algorithmic]].
