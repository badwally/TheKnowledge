# Flow-Driven Asset Pricing

## Summary

The research program implied by the inelastic markets hypothesis that replaces the "dark matter" of asset pricing — hard-to-measure latent forces — with tangible, measurable flows and demand shocks of concrete investors [[sources/pdf-b8cec9d1ec0c]].

## Key claims

- Investor-specific flows and demand shocks are quantitatively impactful in inelastic markets, allowing researchers to replace the "dark matter" of asset pricing (whereby price movements are explained by hard-to-measure latent forces) with tangible flows and the demand shocks of different investors [[sources/pdf-b8cec9d1ec0c]].
- The framework gives a dynamic economic structure to old and recent datasets comprising holdings and flows in various segments of the market [[sources/pdf-b8cec9d1ec0c]].
- Suggests a research program in which the determinants of asset prices can be traced back to measurable demand shocks and flows of concrete investors — by studying the actions of these investors, researchers can infer their demand curves and theorize about their determinants [[sources/pdf-b8cec9d1ec0c]].
- Replaces "the mystery of apparently random movements of the stock market, hard to link to fundamentals" with the "more manageable problem of understanding the determinants of flows in inelastic markets" [[sources/pdf-b8cec9d1ec0c]].
- The capital-flow measure constructed by Gabaix and Koijen is strongly correlated with realized returns and survey expectations of returns, but only weakly correlated with macroeconomic growth [[sources/pdf-b8cec9d1ec0c]].
- Connects to the stochastic discount factor (SDF) approach: flows are primitive and the SDF is a book-keeping device to record their influence on prices, useful for getting correct risk prices in macroeconomic models including their variation due to flows [[sources/pdf-b8cec9d1ec0c]].
- The general equilibrium model in the spirit of Lucas (1978) developed in the paper calibrates well, replicating the volatility and size of the equity premium, slow mean-reversion of the price-dividend ratio, and the ability to predict stock returns with the price-dividend ratio at different horizons — making the inelastic-markets GE model competitive with habit-formation (Campbell and Cochrane (1999)), long-run-risks (Bansal and Yaron (2004)), and variable rare disasters (Gabaix (2012); Wachter (2013)) models [[sources/pdf-b8cec9d1ec0c]].
