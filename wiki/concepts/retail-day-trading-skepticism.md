# Empirical Skepticism of Retail Day Trading Profitability

## Summary

A body of academic findings positions retail day trading as a near-impossible source of consistent profits — Chague et al. (2020) reported that 97% of all individuals who day-traded the Brazilian equity futures market for more than 300 days between 2013 and 2015 lost money, and Barber et al. found that less than 1% of Taiwan-stock-exchange day traders between 1992 and 2006 could predictably and reliably earn positive abnormal returns net of fees; Zarattini and Aziz (2023) cite these results as the empirical baseline their ORB study contests [[sources/pdf-e63407c2b4f4]].

## Key claims

- Chague et al. (2020) showed it was "virtually impossible for individuals to day trade the Brazilian equity futures market" between 2013 and 2015, with the Brazilian market ranked third in the world by volume [[sources/pdf-e63407c2b4f4]].
- The Chague et al. result: 97% of all traders who traded more than 300 days in that period lost money [[sources/pdf-e63407c2b4f4]].
- Barber et al. investigated day-trader performance on the Taiwan Stock Exchange between 1992 and 2006 and concluded that less than 1% of those day traders were able to predictably and reliably earn positive abnormal returns net of fees [[sources/pdf-e63407c2b4f4]].
- Critics also frame day trading as essentially a form of gambling, arguing that success is largely based on luck rather than skill [[sources/pdf-e63407c2b4f4]].
- Zarattini and Aziz position their 2023 paper as a counter-data point: the empirical evidence for retail day-trading profitability has historically been weak, but a simple, non-optimized ORB strategy on QQQ is shown to outperform a passive benchmark with statistically significant alpha — suggesting strategy selection, not the activity itself, is the binding constraint [[sources/pdf-e63407c2b4f4]].
