# Two centuries trend-following anomaly

## Summary

Empirical finding from Lempérière, Deremble, Seager, Potters, and Bouchaud (Capital Fund Management, 2014) that trend-following strategies generate anomalous excess returns across commodities, currencies, stock indices, and bonds with a t-stat of ≈5 since 1960 (using futures) and ≈10 since 1800 (using spot proxies for commodities and indices), after accounting for the upward drift of these markets — making trend one of the most statistically significant anomalies in financial markets [[sources/pdf-be69dd282a8c]].

## Key claims

- Overall t-stat of trend-following excess returns is ≈5 since 1960 and ≈10 since 1800, after accounting for the upward drift of these markets [[sources/pdf-be69dd282a8c]].
- The effect is very stable, both across time and across asset classes [[sources/pdf-be69dd282a8c]].
- Over the 1960–2010s futures sample, the trend-following t-stat is above 2.1 for all four sectors (commodities, currencies, bonds, indices) and all decades, and above 1.6 even after de-biasing from the long-only drift [[sources/pdf-be69dd282a8c]].
- The Sharpe ratio of the diversified-pool 5-month trend-following strategy on futures since 1960 is 0.78 (t-stat 5.7), with sector Sharpes of 0.8 commodities, 0.57 currencies, 0.49 bonds, and 0.41 indices [[sources/pdf-be69dd282a8c]].
- Spot-price proxies allow the indices and commodities series to be extended back to 1800; freely-traded, liquid currency and bond series are not available before the 1973 collapse of Bretton Woods (currencies) or before liquid government-debt secondary markets (bonds) [[sources/pdf-be69dd282a8c]].
- The result is universal in two senses: it holds across asset classes and across two centuries of macro-environments and economic cycles [[sources/pdf-be69dd282a8c]].
- Capital Fund Management's pool covered 7 commodity contracts (Crude oil, Henry Hub Natural Gas, Corn, Wheat, Sugar, Live Cattle, Copper), 7 10-year bond contracts, 7 stock index contracts, and 6 currency contracts spanning Australia, Canada, Germany, Japan, Switzerland, the United Kingdom, and the United States, with prices supplied by Global Financial Data [[sources/pdf-be69dd282a8c]].
