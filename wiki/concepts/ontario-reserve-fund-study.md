# Ontario Reserve Fund Study (RFS)

## Summary

The Ontario Reserve Fund Study (RFS) is a detailed plan defined by Section 94 of the Ontario Condominium Act, 1998 that enables a condominium community to put aside enough money in its reserve fund to keep the building safe and well maintained over time. The RFS regime imposes a defined cadence, scope, planner-qualification, and disclosure pathway distinct from US state regimes [[sources/pdf-aa9367a8a4a9]]. Section 94(1) of the Condo Act requires periodic studies to determine whether the reserve fund balance and the contributions collected are adequate to provide for the expected costs of major repairs and replacements over time [[sources/pdf-c974b5dbd563]].

## Key claims

- An RFS must be completed by a qualified Reserve Fund Planner in the first year of a condominium corporation's operations [[sources/pdf-aa9367a8a4a9]]; specifically, within the year following the registration of the declaration and description [[sources/pdf-c974b5dbd563]].
- The RFS must be updated at least every three years, alternating between a Class 2 RFS (which includes a site visit) and a Class 3 RFS (no site visit) [[sources/pdf-aa9367a8a4a9]]; per the CAO Guide, the first study is a comprehensive Class 1, then Class 3 and Class 2 are done on an alternating basis at least every three years [[sources/pdf-c974b5dbd563]].
- There are three statutory types of reserve fund studies: a comprehensive study (Class 1), an updated study with a site inspection (Class 2), and an updated study without a site inspection (Class 3) [[sources/pdf-c974b5dbd563]].
- The Class 1 (comprehensive) study requires the RFS provider to physically visit the property to examine the common elements and assets, conduct a review of records, and hold interviews with the directors, employees, and agents of the condominium corporation [[sources/pdf-c974b5dbd563]].
- The RFS must establish at least a 30-year timeline for projections; the 30-year minimum is legislated, and the CAO Guide recommends that boards consider requesting a 45-year-or-longer timeline as best practice [[sources/pdf-c974b5dbd563]].
- The RFS must contain an inventory and physical analysis of all components [[sources/pdf-aa9367a8a4a9]].
- The RFS must contain an assessment of each component including its actual age or year of acquisition, expected life, estimated remaining life expectancy, and estimated repair or replacement cost [[sources/pdf-aa9367a8a4a9]].
- Under O. Reg. 48/01 §29(2), the physical analysis must include the component inventory plus, for each item: actual or estimated year of acquisition; present or estimated age; normal expected life; remaining life expectancy; estimated year for major repair or replacement; estimated cost of major repair or replacement as of the date of the study; and the percentage of that cost to be covered by the reserve fund (with adjusted cost) [[sources/pdf-c974b5dbd563]].
- A "component inventory" is defined by O. Reg. 48/01 as an inventory of each item of the common elements and assets of the condominium corporation that is expected to require a major repair or replacement within at least 30 years of the date of the study, where the cost of replacement is at least $500 [[sources/pdf-c974b5dbd563]].
- The RFS must contain a financial analysis describing the financial status of the Reserve Fund's current balance and its adequacy to cover future expenses [[sources/pdf-aa9367a8a4a9]].
- Under O. Reg. 48/01 §29(3), the financial analysis must include a description of the reserve fund's financial status as of the date of the study and a recommended funding plan projected over at least 30 consecutive years showing the minimum balance, year-by-year estimated costs at current and inflation-adjusted prices, assumed inflation and interest rates, estimated opening and closing balances, recommended contributions on a cash-flow basis, and year-over-year percentage increases in contributions [[sources/pdf-c974b5dbd563]].
- The RFS must contain a recommended funding plan [[sources/pdf-aa9367a8a4a9]].
- The Practice Guide explicitly distinguishes "expected life" from "estimated remaining life expectancy": equipment with a 10-year expected life that is well maintained may have an estimated remaining life expectancy of five years after eight years, while poorly maintained equipment may have only two years remaining after five years — "expected life minus age" does not necessarily equal estimated remaining life expectancy [[sources/pdf-aa9367a8a4a9]].
- Prior to the first RFS and the implementation of a plan for future funding, contributions to the reserve fund must be the greater of (a) 10% of the operating budget for the condominium corporation, or (b) the amount reasonably expected to provide sufficient funds for major repair and replacement based on the RFS but prior to plan implementation [[sources/pdf-c974b5dbd563]].
- The CAO Guide explicitly frames the RFS as an estimate of future costs that may under-estimate actual repair and replacement costs because unexpected expenses can arise [[sources/pdf-c974b5dbd563]].
- Within 120 days of preparing the RFS, the Board must propose a plan for future funding of the reserve fund; within 15 days, the corporation must send the Notice of Future Funding (NOFF) to owners [[sources/pdf-aa9367a8a4a9]].
- The proposed funding plan can be implemented starting 30 days from the date the NOFF was issued [[sources/pdf-aa9367a8a4a9]].
- In practice, the Reserve Fund Planner helps develop the funding plan at the same time as the RFS itself, but the Board has authority to propose an alternative funding model; variances from the RFS must then be specifically disclosed in the NOFF [[sources/pdf-aa9367a8a4a9]].
- The contribution table and cash flow table included in the NOFF are the two principal artifacts for ongoing reserve-fund management: the contribution table for budgetary contribution sizing, the cash flow table for monitoring balance vs. forecast [[sources/pdf-aa9367a8a4a9]].
