# Data Moat

## Summary

A "data moat" is the proposition that accumulation of proprietary data confers durable competitive defensibility on a firm — a narrative that has been further hyped with the recent wave of AI startups [[sources/web-2019-05-09-487]]. Andreessen Horowitz partners Martin Casado and Peter Lauten argue, from observations across their enterprise-software portfolio, that for most companies the data-moat thesis is overstated: defensibility is not inherent to data itself, and treating data as a "magical moat" can misdirect founders away from sources of defensibility that actually compound over time — verticalization, go-to-market dominance, post-sales account control, and brand [[sources/web-2019-05-09-487]].

## Key claims

- Defensibility is not inherent to data itself; the durability of any data moat depends on the lifecycle of the data journey for the target domain, not on the existence of a data corpus per se [[sources/web-2019-05-09-487]].
- Most claims of "data network effects" in enterprise software actually describe data scale effects rather than true network effects; there generally is no inherent network effect from merely having more data [[sources/web-2019-05-09-487]].
- Data scale effects often invert the economics of traditional scale: the marginal cost of unique data tends to rise while the marginal value of incremental data falls, so the moat erodes rather than compounds as the corpus grows [[sources/web-2019-05-09-487]].
- Data also goes stale (streets change, temperatures change, attitudes change), so any proprietary edge weakens over time as competitors collect comparable data and the corpus loses freshness — data, in this sense, behaves like a commodity [[sources/web-2019-05-09-487]].
- Counterexamples in which proprietary data has produced durable defensibility tend to share an industry-structure feature: scanty sources or sources reticent to provide data to more than one vendor (e.g. government buyers, pharma, Equifax, LexisNexis, Experian) [[sources/web-2019-05-09-487]].
- Where proprietary data is defensible, the moat often derives from adjacent capabilities — the cost of assembling, cleaning, and standardising public datasets; specialised domain know-how; security and compliance scrutiny passed to access sensitive data; and trusted-custodian status that earns access to increasingly sensitive data — rather than from raw data volume [[sources/web-2019-05-09-487]].
