# Margin of safety

## Summary

The practice of buying a security only at a price meaningfully below the investor's estimate of its underlying business value; in Joel Greenblatt's Columbia class, margin of safety is one of four review takeaways for the semester and the discipline that protects against errors in valuation work [[sources/pdf-d0b9b7e97b5c]].

## Key claims

- Greenblatt closes his first class with a four-point review whose final item is "We seek a margin of safety" [[sources/pdf-d0b9b7e97b5c]].
- Margin of safety operates on top of Greenblatt's price-vs-value framework: stocks bounce around a lot, Mr. Market eventually figures it out over three years, the market closes the gap, and the investor seeks a margin of safety to be paid for the wait and absorb mistakes [[sources/pdf-d0b9b7e97b5c]].
- Greenblatt operationalizes margin of safety in his own DCF practice by working backwards — throwing in a bunch of numbers like growth rate and discount rate to find what the future price implies, then asking whether his assumptions can be justified — which forces him to use conservative numbers [[sources/pdf-d0b9b7e97b5c]].
- If Greenblatt cannot figure out a critical input (like growth rate or discount rate) for a company, he passes — "if it is hard for me to figure it out, I go onto the next one" — preserving margin of safety by avoiding the analysis where it cannot exist [[sources/pdf-d0b9b7e97b5c]].
