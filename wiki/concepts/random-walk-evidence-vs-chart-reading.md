# Random-walk evidence against chart reading

## Summary

The empirical case Thorp and Kassouf marshal in chapter 1 of "Beat the Market" (1967) against technical analysis: a Dow-Theory backtest underperforms random buying-and-selling on a per-annum basis, and self-described chartists shown the first half of a randomly selected chart with the issuer hidden produce predictions "no better than those of someone making random guesses" [[sources/pdf-9da6062f0265]].

## Key claims

- Per Richard Durant's "What Is the Dow Theory?", $100 invested in the Dow-Jones industrial average in 1897 would have grown to $11,237 by 1956 if these stocks were sold and repurchased whenever the Dow Theory gave the appropriate signal — equivalent to 8.3% compounded annually [[sources/pdf-9da6062f0265]].
- By comparison, the University of Chicago's Center for Research in Security Prices found that random buying and selling of stock from 1926 to 1960 would have averaged a 9% gain per annum, "about what the Dow Theory claims to have earned by design" [[sources/pdf-9da6062f0265]].
- Kassouf reports a personal experiment: he selected pages at random from a chart book, covered the name of the corporation and the last half of the chart, and asked self-described chart readers what price change the "pattern" indicated — "their 'predictions' were no better than those of someone making random guesses" [[sources/pdf-9da6062f0265]].
- The book argues that chart reading "seems scientific but it isn't," and uses the Dow-Theory comparison and chart-prediction test as evidence that technicians' configurations (heads and shoulders, triangles, wedges, fans) do not provide actionable information about future prices [[sources/pdf-9da6062f0265]].
- The book uses these findings to motivate replacing reliance on individual price prediction with a hedging strategy on convertible securities, where "we do not need to predict prices of individual securities in order to win" [[sources/pdf-9da6062f0265]].
