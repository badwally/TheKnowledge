# Monetary policy dominance era (1981-2021)

## Summary

Per Kai Volatility's Q2 2022 commentary, the 40-year regime running from Volcker's tightening cycle in 1981 through the secular decline in interest rates ending in 2021, during which monetary policy was the dominant macro lever and unconstrained monetary stimulus drove secularly rising inequality, the secular hollowing of the U.S. middle class, and the political populism that made the post-COVID return to fiscal dominance inevitable [[sources/pdf-ed56b074fb11]].

## Key claims

- The era is bracketed by "40 years of monetary policy dominance & secularly declining interest rates from 1981-2021" [[sources/pdf-ed56b074fb11]].
- During this era inequality (Gini coefficient and top-10% wealth share) increased secularly, with monetary policy dominance taking hold "in the mid to late 1970s" [[sources/pdf-ed56b074fb11]].
- Low interest rates during the era represented cheap capital to the investment class, and the overwhelming majority of Fed stimulus "ended up in the hands of the wealthy and corporations, which have used the capital to maximize profits" [[sources/pdf-ed56b074fb11]].
- Cheap investment capital fueled secular globalization and historic technological innovation, generating record corporate profits — but the trends came "directly at the cost of the domestic labor class," with employee compensation as a share of gross domestic income dropping by nearly 20% over the half-century [[sources/pdf-ed56b074fb11]].
- The 2009 Tea Party and 2011 Occupy Wall Street movements were the first populist responses to the era's inequality, but neither was able to gain sufficient traction to force structural change — and "monetary policy remained dominant from 2011-2020" [[sources/pdf-ed56b074fb11]].
- Per Kai Volatility, after 40 years of secularly declining rates, "the world likely faces its first secular inflationary period in two generations," structurally driven by populism rather than psychology — the regime change ending the era [[sources/pdf-ed56b074fb11]].
