# Revenue Ruling 70-604 Rollover Election

## Summary

The Revenue Ruling 70-604 rollover election is the formal IRS-defined procedure by which a non-exempt membership organization — typically a homeowners association or condominium association filing Form 1120 under IRC §277 — defers immediate taxation of excess member assessment income by applying the excess to the following year's assessments [[sources/web-2026-01-01-a9a]]. Under the increasingly restrictive IRS interpretation reported by Gary Porter, CPA, the election is fragile: omission of any of five required elements destroys it, the rollover must be absorbed in the immediately-following tax year (not perpetually deferred), and an unabsorbed rollover is taxed in the year of original election (not the subsequent year), invalidating the election ab initio and requiring an amended return for the original year [[sources/web-2026-01-01-a9a]].

## Key claims

- A proper Rev. Rul. 70-604 election requires five elements: (1) election by the members, not the board of directors; (2) election documented in writing; (3) election made prior to filing the tax return; (4) consideration in the subsequent year of the amount rolled over; (5) "absorption" of the rollover in the subsequent year [[sources/web-2026-01-01-a9a]].
- Omission of any of the five elements destroys the election [[sources/web-2026-01-01-a9a]].
- The first three elements are satisfied by the association approving the election at its annual meeting (before year end, or after year end but before the tax return is filed) and documenting the approval either in the annual meeting minutes or, preferably, as a separate resolution [[sources/web-2026-01-01-a9a]].
- The CPA must document the actual amount of the rollover in Schedule M-1 of the tax return; failure to do so means no one can replicate the calculations or confirm that a rollover occurred [[sources/web-2026-01-01-a9a]].
- The "absorption" requirement is the most crucial in IRS's eyes: the amount rolled over must be absorbed in the subsequent year by generating a membership loss equal to the rollover amount [[sources/web-2026-01-01-a9a]].
- The ruling's operative language is singular ("...have the excess applied to the following year's assessments"); IRS treats this as a one-year rollover only, not a perpetual rollover scheme [[sources/web-2026-01-01-a9a]].
- Worked example: a $50,000 rollover from year 2001 to 2002 requires a $50,000 membership loss in 2002; absent that loss, any excess carryover from 2001 is taxed [[sources/web-2026-01-01-a9a]].
- Under the IRS Special Industry Group Liaison's new interpretation, an unabsorbed rollover is taxed in the original election year (not the subsequent year), invalidating the election retroactively and requiring an amended return for the original year [[sources/web-2026-01-01-a9a]].
- The IRS interpretation analogizes the rollover to IRC §§1031 and 1034: completing only the first part of the transaction (election) without the second part (absorption) invalidates the rollover treatment as if no election were ever made [[sources/web-2026-01-01-a9a]].
- The IRS Special Industry Group Liaison is the same personnel writing future Revenue Rulings, Private Letter Rulings, and serving as Tax Court technical experts for the HOA industry — making this restrictive interpretation effectively binding on practitioners [[sources/web-2026-01-01-a9a]].
- Where a Rev. Rul. 70-604 election is disallowed in audit, the only escape route is for the tax preparer to plead ignorance and invoke Rev. Rul. 83-74 to amend the returns to Form 1120-H [[sources/web-2026-01-01-a9a]].
- In many post-disallowance cases, the resulting tax under §277 is far greater than the tax that would have been owed had Form 1120-H been filed originally [[sources/web-2026-01-01-a9a]].
- Practitioner posture from Gary Porter, CPA: Rev. Rul. 70-604 should be used "as little as possible, and with extreme caution in the future" — it is not the universal cure-all that many CPA practitioners previously believed [[sources/web-2026-01-01-a9a]].
- The IRS audit disallowance pattern is not driven by 70-604 ceasing to be a viable alternative, but by associations and tax practitioners abusing the ruling — failing to adhere to its language, failing to document the rollover, and failing to consider the subsequent year "recovery" of amounts rolled over [[sources/web-2026-01-01-a9a]].
