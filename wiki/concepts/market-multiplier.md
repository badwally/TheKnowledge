# Market Multiplier (M ≈ 5)

## Summary

The empirical and theoretical result of Gabaix and Koijen (2021) that investing $1 in the U.S. stock market increases the market's aggregate value by approximately $5; estimates range from $3 to $8, and the multiplier is the inverse of the macro equity demand elasticity of approximately 0.2 [[sources/pdf-b8cec9d1ec0c]].

## Key claims

- Empirical estimate: investing $1 in the stock market increases the market's aggregate value by about $5, with estimates between $3 and $8; the authors use $5 for simplicity in the theory and discussion [[sources/pdf-b8cec9d1ec0c]].
- The price impact is linear and symmetric: selling $2 worth of equities (buying $2 worth of bonds) decreases the valuation of aggregate equities by $10 [[sources/pdf-b8cec9d1ec0c]].
- A flow of 1% as a fraction of the value of equities implies that the value of the equity market goes up by 5% — the mirror image of the low aggregate equity demand elasticity of 0.2 [[sources/pdf-b8cec9d1ec0c]].
- Robustness: the price impact multiplier remains around 5 across specifications and data sets, including the Flow of Funds and 13F filings [[sources/pdf-b8cec9d1ec0c]].
- Implication for buybacks: if the corporate sector buys back $1 worth of equities rather than paying $1 worth of dividends, the value of equities rises by a tentative estimate of around $2 — qualitatively validating the naive intuition that "if firms buy shares, that drives up the price of shares" [[sources/pdf-b8cec9d1ec0c]].
- The multiplier is what makes flows in the market quantitatively impactful: a model in which prices are simply the present value of future dividends would predict a multiplier near zero, about 100 times smaller than the empirical estimate [[sources/pdf-b8cec9d1ec0c]].
