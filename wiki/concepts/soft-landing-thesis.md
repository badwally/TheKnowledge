# Soft-Landing Thesis (2023)

## Summary

The argument — most explicitly made by Apollo Chief Economist Torsten Sløk in the December 2022 white paper "2023 Economic and Capital Markets Outlook" — that the Fed has a higher-than-consensus probability of engineering a soft landing in 2023 because inflation is coming down without a sharp increase in the unemployment rate, reducing the need for further "demand destruction" on the part of the Fed [[sources/pdf-d196e853ebc0]].

## Key claims

- The decline in inflation in late 2022 is taking place without a sharp increase in the unemployment rate, which points to a higher probability that the Fed might engineer a much-desired soft landing of the US economy [[sources/pdf-d196e853ebc0]].
- The soft landing was "a scenario that many thought very unlikely just a few months ago" — i.e., the consensus had moved toward stagflation as 2022 ended [[sources/pdf-d196e853ebc0]].
- The sequencing of how the Fed reaches its dual mandate (taming inflation and maintaining full employment) is key for capital markets: receding inflation first, moderating employment later means the need for "demand destruction" on the part of the Fed decreases [[sources/pdf-d196e853ebc0]].
- The fact that inflation is coming down before any deterioration in the labor market is "very important for markets and for the economic outlook" — with less pressure on the Fed to forcefully fight inflation going forward, the likelihood of a soft landing in 2023 "is real" [[sources/pdf-d196e853ebc0]].
- This thesis is offered against a Bloomberg consensus as of November 14, 2022 that the US was "standing at the brink of possible stagflation — rising prices paired with declining economic growth" [[sources/pdf-d196e853ebc0]].
- A less aggressive Fed — or a potential Fed "pivot" in 2023 — should be bullish for asset prices (public and private) ranging from rates to credit to equities, but capital markets will likely remain vulnerable in 2023 as capital remains scarce and expensive [[sources/pdf-d196e853ebc0]].
