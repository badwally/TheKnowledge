# Basic research

## Summary

Research conducted on the frontiers of knowledge without regard for potential profit or foreseeable commercial application — also called fundamental research — and characterized by long time horizons, high failure rates, and orientation toward big, hard questions to which nobody knows the answers [[sources/pdf-stanford-2026-the-stanford-emerging]].

## Key claims

- Universities and U.S. national laboratories are the only institutions that conduct research on the frontiers of knowledge without regard for potential profit or foreseeable commercial application [[sources/pdf-stanford-2026-the-stanford-emerging]].
- Basic research takes years, sometimes decades, to bear fruit and often fails, because it asks questions like "What are the principles of quantum physics?" and "How does the human immune system work?" [[sources/pdf-stanford-2026-the-stanford-emerging]].
- The U.S. government is the only funder capable of making the large and risky investments in basic science conducted at universities (and national laboratories) that are essential for future applications [[sources/pdf-stanford-2026-the-stanford-emerging]].
- Federal funding for basic research is directed at work that has no foreseeable profit but addresses national issues for the public benefit, seeks to advance basic understanding, and can take a longer-term view to pursue moonshot ideas, in contrast to private-sector R&D's shorter-term focus and commercial viability [[sources/pdf-stanford-2026-the-stanford-emerging]].
- Examples of breakthroughs that the SETR 2026 attributes to years of federal investment in fundamental university research include the internet, radar, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) machines, and the Global Positioning System (GPS); today's AI revolution is described as having begun fifty years ago with university research into neural networks [[sources/pdf-stanford-2026-the-stanford-emerging]].
- Google is cited as a concrete example, having emerged from a National Science Foundation grant to Stanford professors who were conducting fundamental research on digital libraries in 1993, when there were 100 total websites on Earth [[sources/pdf-stanford-2026-the-stanford-emerging]].
