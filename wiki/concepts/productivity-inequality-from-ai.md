# Productivity Inequality from AI

## Summary

The empirical and theoretical pattern that AI's productivity effects are unevenly distributed across workers and demographic groups. In materials R&D, AI tools disproportionately benefit high-ability workers and widen the productivity distribution — contradicting earlier LLM findings of compression [[sources/pdf-22a560609b73]]. At the macro level, Acemoglu (2024) shows theoretically that even when AI improves low-skill workers' productivity in certain tasks, ripple effects can increase rather than reduce inequality, and empirically finds that AI will not reduce between-group wage inequality despite being more equally distributed across demographics than prior automation technologies [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].

## Key claims

- In materials R&D, the bottom third of scientists saw minimal gains from AI while top-decile output rose 81%, more than doubling 90:10 inequality [[sources/pdf-22a560609b73]].
- This pattern contrasts with prior LLM evidence (Brynjolfsson et al. 2023; Dell'Acqua et al. 2023; Noy and Zhang 2023; Peng et al. 2023; Cui et al. 2024) showing AI compresses the productivity distribution in mid-skill writing and coding [[sources/pdf-22a560609b73]].
- The contrast suggests the "leveling-up" effect is context-dependent, not a general feature of AI: when AI generates novel candidates that require expert judgment to filter, gains accrue to skilled evaluators [[sources/pdf-22a560609b73]].
- Because the model unlocks breakthroughs beyond the capabilities of even highly trained scientists, findings alleviate concerns that AI will contribute only to low-value tasks [[sources/pdf-22a560609b73]].
- Organizational adaptation can amplify the inequality: 83% of scientists fired in the lab's workforce adjustment were in the bottom quartile of judgment [[sources/pdf-22a560609b73]].
- Acemoglu (2024) proves theoretically that an increase in the productivity of low-skill workers in certain tasks can lead to higher rather than lower inequality: when sigma < 1 and task prices fall sharply, high-skill workers reallocate from tasks where AI raised low-skill productivity to tasks where their comparative advantage yields a higher relative wage (omega > lambda_H/lambda_U) [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- Empirically, Acemoglu (2024) finds AI exposure is more equally distributed across demographic groups than pre-AI automation, so wage effects are less adverse — but there is no evidence AI will reduce inequality [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- The between-group standard deviation of log wages increases slightly from 0.35 to 0.36 under AI exposure [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- Low-education women, especially white native-born low-education women, are the most likely demographic group to experience real wage declines from AI [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
