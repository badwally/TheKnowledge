# Triune brain model

## Summary

The triune brain model, proposed by Paul MacLean in the 1960s, describes the human brain as evolving in three functional layers — reptile, limbic, neocortex — and is the neurological frame Tom Hougaard uses to argue that the conscious, logical part of the trader's mind is not the one actually in charge during a trade [[sources/pdf-1590d4650297]].

## Key claims

- In the 1960s, neuroscientist Paul MacLean proposed that the human brain has evolved with three areas of function: the reptile brain, the limbic brain, and the neocortex [[sources/pdf-1590d4650297]].
- The neocortex is the area that deals with logic — the part of the mind the trader can hear, the part the trader thinks is in charge — but it is not [[sources/pdf-1590d4650297]].
- The limbic brain deals with belonging and status; brokers' marketing (BMW/Mercedes badges, well-dressed actors, screen-filled offices) targets this layer to imply that opening an account confers high-status group membership [[sources/pdf-1590d4650297]].
- The reptile brain — the base self — is really in charge: the layer that detects threat (a wobble in the stomach, a vibration in the lower back) and triggers a fight-or-flight response [[sources/pdf-1590d4650297]].
- The reptile brain has only one function — to protect the trader, whether the trader wants it to or not [[sources/pdf-1590d4650297]].
- This produces a structural conflict for traders: a system that protected the caveman from death guarantees that the modern trader will not survive in markets unless the trader can learn to overcome it — and overcoming it begins with accepting pain [[sources/pdf-1590d4650297]].
