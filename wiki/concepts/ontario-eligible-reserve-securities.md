# Ontario Eligible Reserve Fund Securities (Section 115(5))

## Summary

Section 115(5) of the Ontario Condominium Act, 1998 statutorily restricts the universe of securities in which a condominium corporation may invest its reserve fund. As of the October 2017 source-of-record, the eligible-securities definition is narrower than the comparable BC and Alberta regimes: Ontario limits reserve-fund investments to bonds, debentures, GICs, deposit receipts/notes, or similar instruments issued or guaranteed by the Government of Canada or a province, or issued by an Ontario institution insured by CDIC or DICO [[sources/web-2026-01-01-3bf]].

## Key claims

- Section 115(5) of the Condominium Act, 1998 defines eligible securities as bonds, debentures, guaranteed investment certificates (GICs), deposit receipts or notes, or similar instruments [[sources/web-2026-01-01-3bf]].
- Eligible instruments must either be issued or guaranteed by the Government of Canada or one of the Provinces, or be issued by an institution located in Ontario insured by the Canada Deposit Insurance Corporation (CDIC) or the Deposit Insurance Corporation of Ontario (DICO) [[sources/web-2026-01-01-3bf]].
- CDIC-eligible GICs are sold at par, return principal at maturity, and pay an annual coupon; terms-to-maturity cannot exceed 5 years (a CDIC requirement); longer-term conventional GICs paid the highest rates, averaging approximately 2.00% at the time of writing in 2017 [[sources/web-2026-01-01-3bf]].
- Several financial institutions offer CDIC-eligible market-linked GICs paying a lower-than-prevailing-rate annual coupon plus a variable return linked to a market index; these typically have no secondary market and no guarantee that the variable return will exceed zero [[sources/web-2026-01-01-3bf]].
- Government-backed bonds can be sold above or below par and with or without a coupon; bonds are more liquid than GICs but introduce price risk — a 1% rise in interest rates produces approximately a 10% decline in the value of a bond with a ten-year average duration [[sources/web-2026-01-01-3bf]].
- A board purchasing bonds should evaluate yield-to-maturity, which accounts for both current price and coupon payments, to avoid yield-chasing losses from purchasing long-term bonds above par [[sources/web-2026-01-01-3bf]].
- The average rate of return on the Section 115(5) eligible-investment universe is at or below inflation (Total CPI 2.0% at the time of the 2017 writing per Bank of Canada), meaning a corporation can at best maintain its purchasing power by laddering longer-term securities, or otherwise lose purchasing power waiting for rates to rise [[sources/web-2026-01-01-3bf]].
- The Ontario eligible-securities definition is narrower than BC's Strata Property Regulation §6.11 (which permits some Canadian corporate bonds and fixed-income ETFs holding ≥98% investment grade in addition to GICs and government bonds) and narrower than Alberta's authorized-investments regime (which permits US/Canadian/UK government bonds, municipal bonds, some Canadian corporate bonds, preferred shares, and dividend-paying common shares up to 15% of the portfolio) [[sources/web-2026-01-01-3bf]].
- The source author advocates for expansion of the eligible-securities definition to allow Ontario condominium corporations to better diversify risk while optimizing returns; this remained unimplemented as of October 2017 [[sources/web-2026-01-01-3bf]].
