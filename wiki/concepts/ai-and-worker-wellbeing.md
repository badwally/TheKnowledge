# AI and Worker Wellbeing

## Summary

Evidence that AI's productivity gains in scientific work come at a wellbeing cost, with the majority of researchers reporting reduced job satisfaction driven by skill underutilization and decreased creativity—even among the productivity "winners" [[sources/pdf-22a560609b73]].

## Key claims

- 82% of scientists in the materials lab report an overall decline in wellbeing after AI introduction [[sources/pdf-22a560609b73]].
- Researchers experience a 44% reduction in satisfaction with the content of their work [[sources/pdf-22a560609b73]].
- The satisfaction effect is fairly uniform across the productivity distribution, showing that even high-ability "winners" from AI face costs [[sources/pdf-22a560609b73]].
- Top concerns cited by respondents are skill underutilization and reduced creativity [[sources/pdf-22a560609b73]].
- Findings challenge the view that AI primarily automates tedious tasks and frees humans for more rewarding activities [[sources/pdf-22a560609b73]].
- Enjoyment from improved productivity partially offsets the negative effect, especially for high-ability scientists, but does not reverse it [[sources/pdf-22a560609b73]].
- Hands-on AI experience nearly doubles belief in AI's ability to enhance productivity, while concerns over job loss remain constant [[sources/pdf-22a560609b73]].
- The number of researchers planning to reskill rises by 71% after AI exposure [[sources/pdf-22a560609b73]].
- Domain experts surveyed in the lab did not anticipate the effects documented in the paper [[sources/pdf-22a560609b73]].
