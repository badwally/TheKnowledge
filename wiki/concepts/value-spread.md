# Value Spread

## Summary

The ratio of the valuation of expensive stocks to that of cheap stocks — when high, investors are paying a lot for their favorite stocks versus those they dislike, and vice versa; introduced in Asness, Friedman, Krail, and Liew (2000) during the dot-com bubble and used by Cliff Asness in "The Less-Efficient Market Hypothesis" as the empirical anchor for the claim that U.S. stock-market efficiency has deteriorated [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].

## Key claims

- Defined as the ratio of valuation (using whatever measure you favor) of expensive stocks to cheap stocks; when it is high, investors are paying a lot for their favorite stocks versus those they dislike, and vice versa [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].
- Introduced by Asness, Friedman, Krail, and Liew in 2000, during the dot-com bubble [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].
- Asness's primary version: price-to-book of the expensive 30% of stocks divided by that of the cheap 30% only among large caps, using French's 3×2 sort on price-to-book × market equity [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].
- For roughly 50 years (1950 through almost 2000), the series was well-behaved, varying from about 6× to just over 3× [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].
- It then exploded in 1999-2000 to "heights unimagined beforehand," as the prices of already-expensive stocks soared versus those of cheap stocks [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].
- Returns to value strategies — both simple price-multiple-based strategies and more "holistic" strategies that also incorporate "quality" — were crushed at "never-seen-before levels" during the dot-com peak, then made more than all of that back in the years after the peak [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].
- The same exercise using price-to-cashflow from Ken French's website produces a graph with essentially the same pattern, with the dot-com peak just edging out the COVID peak [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].
- Adding small stocks to the mix changes little; global versions yield highly similar results [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].
- Going back further than 1950 the series is quite unstable [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].
- Asness uses two versions: the simple Fama-French price-to-book version going back to 1950, and a quant-style version (multiple measures of value, taking out as much of the industry bet as possible) over a shorter sample period [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].
