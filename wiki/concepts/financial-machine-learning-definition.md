# Financial Machine Learning (Three-Part Definition)

## Summary

Working definition of machine learning adopted by Kelly and Xiu's 2023 survey, following Gu et al. (2020b): a combination of high-dimensional models, regularization for model selection and overfit mitigation, and efficient algorithms for searching among many candidate specifications — explicitly distinguishing machine learning from traditional econometrics by openness to flexible, often nonlinear functional representations [[sources/pdf-e2068d89c2a7]].

## Key claims

- Per Gu et al. (2020b), "the definition of machine learning is inchoate and the term is at times corrupted by the marketing purposes of the user"; Kelly and Xiu adopt their three-part definition explicitly [[sources/pdf-e2068d89c2a7]].
- Part (i): a diverse collection of high-dimensional models for statistical prediction [[sources/pdf-e2068d89c2a7]].
- Part (ii): "regularization" methods for model selection and mitigation of overfit [[sources/pdf-e2068d89c2a7]].
- Part (iii): efficient algorithms for searching among a vast number of potential model specifications [[sources/pdf-e2068d89c2a7]].
- Under this definition, financial machine learning amounts to "a set of procedures for estimating a statistical model and using that model to make decisions" — and at its core need not be differentiated from econometrics or statistics more generally [[sources/pdf-e2068d89c2a7]].
- Kelly and Xiu cite Israel et al. (2020) for the observation that many ideas underlying machine learning have lived comfortably under the umbrella of statistics for decades [[sources/pdf-e2068d89c2a7]].
- Part (i) of the definition points out that machine learning brings an open-mindedness to functional representations that are highly parameterized and often nonlinear, contrasting with small models that are rigid and oversimplified but yield comparatively precise parameter estimates and ease of interpretation [[sources/pdf-e2068d89c2a7]].
