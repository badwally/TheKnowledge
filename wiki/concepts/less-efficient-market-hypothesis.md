# Less-Efficient Market Hypothesis

## Summary

Cliff Asness's contention, articulated in his paper of the same name, that for the relative pricing of different common stocks the U.S. equity market has gotten less informationally efficient over his 30+-year career, particularly over medium horizons — with the implication that disciplined, value-based stock picking is both riskier and likely more rewarding long term [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].

## Key claims

- The hypothesis is restricted to relative pricing of common stocks, particularly over medium horizons; the absolute pricing of the whole market (e.g., is the S&P 500 way too expensive?) is treated as a related but different issue [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].
- Asness offers three hypotheses for why markets have become less efficient and argues that technologies such as social media are likely the biggest culprit [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].
- The implication is that investors willing to take the other side of these inefficiencies should rationally be rewarded with higher expected returns, but also greater risks [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].
- A "whiggish" assumption that markets would get more efficient over time as information speed and access improved and trading costs fell — Asness's own initial assumption — is mistaken because it confuses speed with accuracy [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].
- The piece is framed as "as much an op-ed as it is quantitative research" because tests of changes in EMH are even harder than tests of EMH itself [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].
- The empirical centerpiece is the value spread — the ratio of valuation of expensive stocks to cheap stocks — which was well-behaved between roughly 6× and 3× from 1950 through almost 2000, then exploded to "heights unimagined beforehand" in 1999-2000, with similarly elevated readings during COVID [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].
- Once one accepts that markets are not perfectly efficient, "how efficient?" and "Has this changed through time?" become legitimate empirical questions — and there is no reason to expect the level of efficiency to be constant through time [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].
