# Incentive-Caused Bias

## Summary

The first and most extensively discussed of Munger's twenty-five psychological tendencies: the phenomenon whereby a person with an acculturated, decent nature drifts — both consciously and subconsciously — into immoral or irrational behavior in order to get what incentives drive him toward, rationalizing his bad behavior along the way; Munger argues that the power of incentives is so extreme that even people in the top five percent of understanding it consistently underestimate it [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].

## Key claims

- Munger places this tendency first in his framework because "almost everyone thinks he fully recognizes how important incentives and disincentives are in changing cognition and behavior. But this is not often so" [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- The Federal Express case demonstrates incentive redesign: the night shift's performance improved dramatically only when it was paid per shift rather than per hour, allowing employees to go home when all planes were loaded [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- The Xerox case: Joe Wilson discovered that a new superior machine was underselling its older inferior predecessor because the commission arrangement gave salesmen a perverse incentive to push the inferior product [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- A surgeon in Lincoln, Nebraska removed normal gallbladders for years, rationalizing to himself that "the gall bladder was the source of all medical evil" — an extreme case of cognitive drift driven by incentives, but present in lesser strength in every profession and every human being [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- Economists have long known the phenomenon under the name "agency cost" — "just as grain is always lost to rats, employers always lose to employees who improperly think of themselves first" [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- Ben Franklin's maxim in *Poor Richard's Almanack* captures the correct precaution: "If you would persuade, appeal to interest and not to reason" [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- Antidotes include: (1) fear professional advice especially when it is especially good for the advisor, (2) learn the basic elements of your advisor's trade, and (3) double-check, disbelieve, or replace much of what you're told [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- The cash register, invented by Patterson, was "a great moral instrument" because it made dishonest behavior hard to accomplish — and bad behavior is intensely habit-forming when rewarded, per Skinner [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- Sound accounting is a critical institutional antidote; Westinghouse lost billions on hotel construction loans because its accounting system let lending officers show extremely high starting income from loans far riskier than historical norms, creating a nearly insane incentive structure [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- The Defense Department made it a felony for contracting officers to sign cost-plus-a-percentage-of-cost contracts after much truly awful experience with misbehaving contractors — yet much of the rest of the world still operates under such systems [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- B.F. Skinner's work on reinforcement schedules proved that random distribution of rewards kept conditioned reflexive behavior in place longest, partially explaining man's gambling compulsion — though Skinner overclaimed for incentive superpower to the point of diminishing his own reputation [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- "Perhaps the most important rule in management is 'Get the incentives right'" [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
