# 90% Days (Desmond)

## Summary

A market-breadth intensity indicator developed by Paul F. Desmond of Lowry's Reports from 69 years of NYSE data (1933–2002), which identifies panic selling and panic buying by requiring that BOTH the volume ratio and the points ratio reach 90% or more on the same day; the combination of multiple 90% Downside Days followed by a 90% Upside Day (or back-to-back 80% Upside Days) produces a high-probability signal that a major trend reversal has begun, typically identifying the reversal far earlier than fundamental, economic, or conventional technical methods [[sources/pdf-bd-2002-microsoft-word-dow]].

## Key claims

- A **90% Downside Day** is defined as a day on which Downside Volume equals 90.0% or more of the sum of Upside plus Downside Volume, AND Points Lost equals 90.0% or more of the sum of Points Gained plus Points Lost [[sources/pdf-bd-2002-microsoft-word-dow]].
- A **90% Upside Day** is defined as the mirror: Upside Volume equals 90.0% or more of total volume, AND Points Gained equals 90.0% or more of total points [[sources/pdf-bd-2002-microsoft-word-dow]].
- The critical distinction is **intensity** rather than activity — prior analysts had attempted to define panic selling in terms of extreme volume, massive declining stocks, or large numbers of new lows, but those definitions do not stand up under critical examination [[sources/pdf-bd-2002-microsoft-word-dow]].
- Almost all periods of significant market decline in the 69-year record contained at least one, and usually more than one, 90% Downside Day [[sources/pdf-bd-2002-microsoft-word-dow]].
- 90% Downside Days do not usually occur as a single incident on the bottom day but typically occur on a number of occasions throughout a major decline, often spread apart by as much as thirty trading days [[sources/pdf-bd-2002-microsoft-word-dow]].
- Days of panic selling cannot by themselves produce a market reversal — as the Law of Supply and Demand would emphasize, it takes strong Demand, not just a reduction in Supply, to cause prices to rise substantially [[sources/pdf-bd-2002-microsoft-word-dow]].
- The reversal pattern is complete when investors rush back in and produce a 90% Upside Day, or on rare occasions two back-to-back 80% Upside Days [[sources/pdf-bd-2002-microsoft-word-dow]].
- **Back-to-back 80% Upside Days** are relatively rare except at reversals from a major market low, making them a valid substitute for a single 90% Upside Day in the pattern (examples: December 31, 1974 / January 2, 1975; August 1–2, 1996; November 9–12, 1990) [[sources/pdf-bd-2002-microsoft-word-dow]].
- **Back-to-back 90% Upside Days** (e.g., May 31 and June 1, 1988) are even rarer and have usually been registered near the beginning of important intermediate and longer-term trend rallies [[sources/pdf-bd-2002-microsoft-word-dow]].
- In approximately half the cases in the 69-year record, the confirming 90% Upside Day occurred within five trading days or less of the market low [[sources/pdf-bd-2002-microsoft-word-dow]].
- A single isolated 90% Downside Day does not by itself have long-term trend implications, since they often occur at the end of short-term corrections — but should be viewed as an important warning that more could follow [[sources/pdf-bd-2002-microsoft-word-dow]].
- It usually takes time and significantly lower prices for investor psychology to reach the panic stage; therefore a 90% Downside Day that occurs quickly after a market high is most commonly associated with a short-term correction [[sources/pdf-bd-2002-microsoft-word-dow]].
- Market declines containing two or more 90% Downside Days often generate a series of additional 90% Downside Days — investors should not assume they can ride out such a decline without taking defensive measures [[sources/pdf-bd-2002-microsoft-word-dow]].
- Impressive, big-volume "snap-back" rallies lasting two to seven days commonly follow quickly after 90% Downside Days and can be advantageous for nimble traders, but longer-term investors should view snap-back rallies as opportunities to move to a more defensive position [[sources/pdf-bd-2002-microsoft-word-dow]].
- Investors should be wary of upside days on which only one component (Upside Volume or Points Gained) reaches 90% while the other falls short — such rallies are often short-lived [[sources/pdf-bd-2002-microsoft-word-dow]].
- The longer it takes for buyers to rush in after the market low, the more investors should look for other confirmatory evidence of a market reversal [[sources/pdf-bd-2002-microsoft-word-dow]].
- Historical track record of the pattern at major bottoms: seven 90% Downside Days during the 1962 decline (90% Upside Day three days after the low); six during 1970 (one day after the low); fourteen during 1973–74 (back-to-back 80% Upside Days sixteen days after the low); three during 1980 (one day after the low); two before the 1987 bottom plus aftershock sequences in October–January; five during 1990 (back-to-back 80% Upside Days twenty days after the low); three during 1998 (five trading days after the low) [[sources/pdf-bd-2002-microsoft-word-dow]].
- The 1990 record contains a false 90% Upside Day on August 27, after which the DJIA moved sideways for two weeks before dropping to new lows — demonstrating the record is not perfect [[sources/pdf-bd-2002-microsoft-word-dow]].
- As of February 2002, the 2000–2002 bear market had produced seven 90% Downside Days and a number of back-to-back 80% Downside Days, but no confirming 90% Upside Day — leaving the reversal pattern incomplete [[sources/pdf-bd-2002-microsoft-word-dow]].
- Desmond argues these observations have allowed investors to (1) avoid buying too soon in a rapidly declining market, and (2) identify many major turning points in their very early stages — usually far faster than other forms of fundamental or technical trend analysis [[sources/pdf-bd-2002-microsoft-word-dow]].
