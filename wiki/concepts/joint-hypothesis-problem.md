# Joint Hypothesis Problem

## Summary

The methodological problem that empirical tests of the Efficient Market Hypothesis cannot disentangle whether market prices reflect all information from the model used to specify how information should be incorporated into prices — so a rejection of the joint hypothesis tells the researcher that one or the other (or both) is wrong, but not which [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].

## Key claims

- EMH's basic formation — "prices reflect all information" — is intuitive, but testing it requires a model, formal or informal, of what and how information should be incorporated into prices [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].
- One can therefore only test the "joint hypothesis": prices reflect all information AND abide by the analyst's model of what and how information should be incorporated [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].
- If that joint hypothesis is rejected by the data, "you generally don't know which one, or both, were at fault" [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].
- Cliff Asness and John Liew's response to the joint hypothesis problem in Institutional Investor was to inject the word "reasonable" into the process — analogous to law's "reasonable man" standard — to rule out unreasonable models that rationalize any apparent anomaly [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].
- Asness argues the problem makes tests of how EMH has changed through time even harder than tests of EMH itself [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].
