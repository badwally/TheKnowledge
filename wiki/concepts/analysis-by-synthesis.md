# Analysis-by-Synthesis

## Summary

Analysis-by-synthesis is the cognitive-psychological tradition — informed by Helmholtz and developed by MacKay (1956), Neisser (1967), and Gregory (1980) — in which the brain understands its sensory input not by accumulating low-level cues bottom-up but by trying to *synthesize* (predict) the current suite of cues from its best models of their possible causes [[sources/pdf-5f41a1d2e45f]]. Yuille and Kersten (2006) review this paradigm; Clark cites it as the direct ancestor of modern hierarchical predictive coding [[sources/pdf-5f41a1d2e45f]].

## Key claims

- The mapping from low- to high-level representation (e.g., from acoustic to word-level) is computed using the reverse mapping, from high- to low-level representation (Chater & Manning 2006) [[sources/pdf-5f41a1d2e45f]].
- The paradigm reframes perception as a constructive, top-down inference problem rather than a bottom-up feature-detection problem [[sources/pdf-5f41a1d2e45f]].
- Analysis-by-synthesis was operationalized in computational neuroscience through the Helmholtz Machine (Dayan et al. 1995; see also Hinton & Zemel 1994) and related connectionist architectures [[sources/pdf-5f41a1d2e45f]].
