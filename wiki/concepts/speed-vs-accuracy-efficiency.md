# Speed vs. Accuracy in Market Efficiency

## Summary

Cliff Asness's distinction between two different things one might mean by "market efficiency" — the speed at which new information gets reflected in prices versus the accuracy of price levels before and after that information is reflected — and his argument that the past few decades' gains in speed are largely irrelevant to the medium-term efficiency thesis [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].

## Key claims

- Over the last 20-40 years the ubiquity and speed of available information has continuously grown, and trading costs have rapidly come down — making the "whiggish" assumption that markets would get more efficient over time intuitive [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].
- But this assumption mistakes speed for accuracy: it's hard to imagine new information doesn't impact stock prices faster than in the past, but speed doesn't imply the level of prices before or after the new information was particularly accurate [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].
- Aside from higher-frequency traders, speed has never been the issue — basic value and momentum strategies have always had turnover that makes nanosecond-like speed irrelevant [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].
- If you take most medium-term holding-period backtests and lag their trading a full week from when the decision was made, there is only a modest deterioration — a strategy that holds stocks for 9-12 months should not fall apart with a one-week delay; if it does, it was really a one-week strategy, not a one-year one [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].
- Trading costs, while they have fallen, are still not "free" — Asness argues that "payment for order flow" probably offers a better deal than the past but is not done for "eleemosynary reasons" [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].
- Speed of information has consequences and can make the market more efficient in one sense, but is largely irrelevant to the medium-term efficiency hypothesis [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].
- Some commentators blame short-term traders, particularly short-term "quants," for less efficient markets; Asness rejects this — medium- to long-term efficiency is not something short-term traders can affect very much, though he agrees markets have gotten less rational for other reasons [[sources/pdf-c25a6984aba0]].
