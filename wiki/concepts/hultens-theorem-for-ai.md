# Hulten's Theorem Applied to AI

## Summary

The application of Hulten's (1978) theorem — that in competitive constant-returns-to-scale economies, aggregate TFP gains equal the sum of task-level cost savings weighted by each task's GDP share — to discipline macro forecasts of AI's productivity effects; Acemoglu (2024) uses this to show that even with generous assumptions, AI's 10-year TFP gains are capped at 0.53–0.66% [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].

## Key claims

- Hulten's theorem states that dlnTFP = integral of chi(z) * pi_L(z) dz, where chi(z) is the GDP share of task z and pi_L(z) is the productivity improvement or cost savings in that task from AI [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- The theorem applies regardless of whether AI's effects come through automation or task complementarities, since both produce task-level cost savings [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- Total GDP effects add a capital accumulation term: dlnY = dlnTFP + s_K * dlnK, where s_K is the capital share [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- Using Eloundou et al. (2023) exposure data: 20% of US labor tasks are exposed to AI when weighted by wage bill share [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- Using Svanberg et al. (2024): among exposed tasks, only 23% can be profitably automated within 10 years, yielding 4.6% of GDP as the share impacted [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- Average labor cost savings of 27% (mean of Noy and Zhang 2023 at 40% and Brynjolfsson et al. 2023 at 14%), converted to overall cost savings of 14.4% using industry labor shares of 0.535 [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- Baseline TFP calculation: 0.046 * 0.144 = 0.66% over 10 years, or approximately 0.064% per annum — "nontrivial but modest" and far below Goldman Sachs (7% GDP) and McKinsey (1.5–3.4% annual GDP growth) forecasts [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- When the easy/hard task distinction is incorporated (72.6% easy at 14.4% cost savings, 27.4% hard at 3.7%), TFP drops to 0.53% [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- GDP estimates range from 0.93–1.16% (proportional capital growth) to 1.4–1.56% (large investment boom) over 10 years [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- Even the 0.66% figure is likely an upper bound because it ignores low AI adoption rates (less than 1.5% of US businesses had any AI investment in 2019), J-curve adjustment costs, and the difficulty of hard-to-learn tasks [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
