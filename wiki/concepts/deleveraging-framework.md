# Deleveraging framework

## Summary

Andrew Constan's diagnostic framework for classifying and predicting the duration, intensity, and consequences of any market deleveraging episode; it defines a deleveraging as the inelastic selling of risky and/or long-term assets combined with inelastic buying of short-term risk-free assets, then asks four structured questions — who is deleveraging, how leverage providers are involved, whether the real economy is threatened, and whether policymakers will react — to determine whether the episode will be brief and buyable or prolonged and damaging [[sources/pdf-f139d7bd1924]].

## Key claims

- A deleveraging is an inelastic selling of risky and/or long-term assets and a similar inelastic buying of short-term risk-free assets [[sources/pdf-f139d7bd1924]].
- Unlevered investors sell assets and reinvest in cash, creating inelastic demand for short-term treasuries; levered investors are forced to sell to fund margin calls or stop out risk positions, generating demand for short-term assets, collateral for margin calls, and repayments to leverage providers [[sources/pdf-f139d7bd1924]].
- During such periods the inelastic supply of assets and demand for short-term treasuries meets highly elastic counterflow, resulting in large price dislocations [[sources/pdf-f139d7bd1924]].
- The framework asks four diagnostic questions: (1) Who is deleveraging — a single investor, a cohort, a broad set, or systemic? (2) How are leverage providers involved — are they able and willing to provide leverage, or are they themselves deleveraging? (3) Does the episode threaten the real economy — will growth and inflation be impacted, is financial stability risk threatened? (4) Will policymakers react — will central banks cut rates, provide funding programs, or purchase assets, and will fiscal authorities act? [[sources/pdf-f139d7bd1924]].
- Common market reactions across all deleveragings: risky assets fall, short-term safe assets rally, two-year yields fall (pricing in cuts), yield curve bull steepens, equity implied volatility spikes, high-yield credit spreads widen, funding and safe-haven currencies rally [[sources/pdf-f139d7bd1924]].
- To conclude that a deleveraging is over, "one has to believe that the deleveraging is by a few concentrated forced sellers, will have no impact on the real economy and will be offset by strong central bank action to address possible contagion from the concentrated deleveraging if necessary" [[sources/pdf-f139d7bd1924]].
- Many deleveragings result in difficulty for private-sector banks and a recession, which is then offset by significant and broad policymaker action; those episodes are long-lasting, and the policymaker actions only occur after a significant drawdown in risky assets and a weak economy [[sources/pdf-f139d7bd1924]].
- Applied to 13 historical deleveraging episodes spanning 1987 to 2023, the framework yields a taxonomy: equity events (1987), credit events (1989–1991), duration events (1994, 2022 LDI, 2023 SVB), contagion events (1997), leverage events (1998 LTCM), bubble events (2000–2002), bank events (2007–2009 GFC), geopolitical events (2010–2014 European debt), currency events (2015 China), monetary events (2018 Fed overtightening), and exogenous events (2020 Covid) [[sources/pdf-f139d7bd1924]].
- Constan's August 2024 assessment: the post-FOMC/BOJ/refunding deleveraging is broad but not systemic, financial institutions are not contributing, the economy is not being impacted, and central banks are unlikely to change policy — making 1987 the best historical comparable, without the drama of a crash but with a more leveraged system and less room to ease [[sources/pdf-f139d7bd1924]].
