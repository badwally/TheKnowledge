# Policy Feedback Theory

## Summary

A tradition in political science holding that new policies, once implemented, can "create a new kind of politics" by setting in motion self-reinforcing processes that effectively "lock in" policies, bolster their political supports, and alter subsequent political developments — a premise widely held in both the academy and the public sphere as the mechanism by which legislative success translates into electoral success [[sources/pdf-5e6d63eac790]].

## Key claims

- The notion that policies generate feedback effects which result in electoral benefits for parties is so commonly held that it has become "almost an unstated premise of political thinking" [[sources/pdf-5e6d63eac790]].
- The intellectual lineage Galvin & Thurston cite includes Schattschneider 1935, Lowi 1964, Pierson 1993 and 2000, Skocpol 1993b, Mettler 1998, Hacker 2002, and Campbell 2003 [[sources/pdf-5e6d63eac790]].
- Sophisticated theories take a step further to explain how policies can incorporate earlier opponents as supportive constituencies and reconfigure the political terrain in ways that make their reversal unlikely [[sources/pdf-5e6d63eac790]].
- Recent scholarship (Anzia and Moe 2016) extends the framework strategically: "if policies do indeed make politics, rational politicians have opportunities to use policies to create a future structure of politics more to their advantage" — including specifically "the structure of partisan politics" [[sources/pdf-5e6d63eac790]].
- The party-building upshot summarized by Mettler and SoRelle (2014): "Policies may foster partisan identities associated with the protection of particular public programs and, in the process, enable parties to mobilize voters who rely on them, thus turning those parties into devoted defenders" [[sources/pdf-5e6d63eac790]].
- The theory has taken hold in the public sphere as well — left-leaning writers urge policymakers to enact policies "aimed at creating reinforcing feedback loops that will further empower progressive policymaking," and the same loops are feared on the right (e.g., William Kristol's 1993 warning about Clinton's health care plan relegitimizing middle-class dependence on government) [[sources/pdf-5e6d63eac790]].
- Voters themselves articulate the link: in Ohio and Michigan in 2012, voters said they felt they "owed" Obama for the GM/Chrysler bailout and planned to "thank" him with their votes [[sources/pdf-5e6d63eac790]].
