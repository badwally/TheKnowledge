# Time of Perils

## Summary

The thesis, formalized by Aschenbrenner (2020), that humanity may be living through a unique transitional period in which it has developed technologies advanced enough to threaten its permanent destruction but has not yet grown wealthy enough to spend sufficiently on safety; under moderate scale-effect parameters, existential risk follows a Kuznets-style inverted U-shape over economic development [[sources/pdf-e7ff11e4ad9b]].

## Key claims

- Under a model of directed technical change with moderate parameters, existential risk follows a Kuznets-style inverted U-shape over economic growth [[sources/pdf-e7ff11e4ad9b]].
- This grounds the intuition of thinkers such as Sagan (1994) and Parfit (2011) that human civilization could be passing through a unique "time of perils" — advanced enough to create technologies that threaten permanent destruction, but not yet wealthy enough to spend much on safety [[sources/pdf-e7ff11e4ad9b]].
- During this time of perils, accelerating growth initially increases risk but, perhaps counterintuitively, improves the chances of humanity's survival in the long run [[sources/pdf-e7ff11e4ad9b]].
- Conversely, even short-term stagnation substantially hurts the chances of humanity's survival in the long run [[sources/pdf-e7ff11e4ad9b]].
- If the scale effect of existential risk is too large and the returns to research diminish too rapidly, it is impossible to avert an eventual existential catastrophe [[sources/pdf-e7ff11e4ad9b]].
- In that latter case, the social planner may choose the "end of growth" — stagnation in consumption — if the marginal utility of consumption diminishes rapidly, but humanity is doomed to soon destruction regardless [[sources/pdf-e7ff11e4ad9b]].
- The time-of-perils dynamic emerges because risk increases with the scale of consumption-side activity (e.g., emissions, biotech, AI) but the willingness to pay for safety rises faster as wealth grows past a threshold, eventually pushing risk down [[sources/pdf-e7ff11e4ad9b]].
