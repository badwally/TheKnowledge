# Long and Variable Lags

## Summary

A monetary-policy framing for the delayed and uncertain transmission of a Fed rate change to its impact on the real economy; in Jim Caron's December 12, 2022 Morgan Stanley outlook chapter it is the central organizing concept for 2023 fixed-income strategy because the impact of 2022 hikes will work through to asset valuations only with a lag, turning inflation policy into a delicate balancing act between full employment and stable prices [[sources/pdf-bf1afc73ac8f]].

## Key claims

- Per Jim Caron, "rate hikes work, and there are often 'long and variable lags' between a monetary policy action and its impact on the economy" — phrasing he attributes to the Fed itself [[sources/pdf-bf1afc73ac8f]].
- Caron uses the lag framing to argue that the impact of 2022 rate hikes will greatly influence asset valuations in the new year — i.e., much of 2022's tightening is still ahead of the market as of late 2022 [[sources/pdf-bf1afc73ac8f]].
- Caron argues that Fed policy operating with long and variable lags has the potential to cut back earnings, increase default risks, widen credit spreads, and increase the chances for a recession [[sources/pdf-bf1afc73ac8f]].
- The lag concept is the theoretical basis for Caron's expectation that the Fed will hold rates around 5% for an extended period: aggressive action now may eliminate the need to act more aggressively later, creating greater damage [[sources/pdf-bf1afc73ac8f]].
- Caron's investment implication: with peak inflation likely behind us, high-quality bonds may benefit, while riskier assets and lower-quality credit may suffer — a positioning consistent with a still-tightening real economy under the lag [[sources/pdf-bf1afc73ac8f]].
