# Motivated Reasoning

## Summary

The cognitive tendency by which citizens accept information that aligns with their existing beliefs and disregard information that does not — invoked by Galvin and Thurston as the mechanism that reverses the assumed causal arrow of policy feedback theory: rather than develop strong party attachments as a result of favored policy benefits, citizens' policy views are shaped by their partisanship [[sources/pdf-5e6d63eac790]].

## Key claims

- Citizens engage in "motivated reasoning," meaning they tend to accept information that aligns with their existing beliefs and disregard information that does not (Kunda 1990; Taber and Lodge 2006; Achen and Bartels 2016, as cited) [[sources/pdf-5e6d63eac790]].
- Party identification and elite partisan frames powerfully shape how information is processed, including information about public policies (Green et al 2002; Gaines et al 2007, as cited) [[sources/pdf-5e6d63eac790]].
- The implication for policy feedback: more often, causation runs in the opposite direction from what policy-feedback theory presumes — citizens' policy views are shaped by their partisanship rather than the reverse [[sources/pdf-5e6d63eac790]].
- Citizens may still be able to appreciate policy effects and associate them with a party under certain conditions — when issues are salient, elite cues are strong, and partisan frames are well constructed (Lupia 1994; Lupia and McCubbins 1998; Kuklinski et al 2000; Lau and Redlawsk 2001; Druckman 2004; Chong and Druckman 2007, as cited) — but in a competitive, polarized environment their ability to durably link policy benefits to support for a particular party is likely limited [[sources/pdf-5e6d63eac790]].
