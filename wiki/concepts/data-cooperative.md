# Data Cooperative

## Summary

A data cooperative is a nonprofit, member-owned organization that holds and administers personal data on behalf of its members, who govern the entity democratically and share in any value generated from the data [[sources/web-2021-05-05-125]]. More broadly, data cooperatives are a type of data intermediary that uses long-established cooperative legal models and global regulatory affordances to manage data with or on behalf of members (data producers) for the benefit of members or for trade with external queriers (data users) [[sources/web-2024-04-04-ad6]].

## Key claims

- Data cooperatives emerged as a user- or worker-owned alternative to platform capitalism, asserting that data and the economic and social gains derived from it should be owned by users or workers rather than centralized in a handful of large platform companies [[sources/web-2024-04-04-ad6]].
- Cooperatives following the International Cooperative Alliance (ICA) definition operate under seven core principles: voluntary membership, democratic member control, economic participation of members, autonomy and independence, education/training/information, cooperation among cooperatives, and concern for community [[sources/web-2024-04-04-ad6]].
- Cooperatives are differentiated by ownership group — worker-owned, producer-owned, or consumer-owned — and existing data cooperatives tend to be producer-owned, since the members providing the data also benefit from its trade [[sources/web-2024-04-04-ad6]].
- Cooperatives are a specific legal entity in most countries globally, distinguishing data cooperatives from data commons, which do not identify with the international cooperative movement or its corresponding legal affordances [[sources/web-2024-04-04-ad6]].
- Some entities calling themselves data cooperatives are in fact other kinds of data intermediary, because they are not constituted as cooperative legal enterprises [[sources/web-2024-04-04-ad6]].
- The largest and most successful data cooperatives to date are health data cooperatives, where individuals upload health data to inform their doctors and support scientific research while retaining granular, revocable consent over downstream use [[sources/web-2024-04-04-ad6]].
- Membership may require contributing data and/or paying monetary dues; cooperatives may also be cooperatives of organizations rather than individuals, as proposed for construction management [[sources/web-2024-04-04-ad6]].
- Cooperative managers and boards owe fiduciary duties of management, care, loyalty, and obedience to members [[sources/web-2024-04-04-ad6]].
- Cooperatively held data systems generally organize around three user roles: personal data storers / cooperative members, queriers (users trading for access to run queries and algorithms against cooperative data), and the data cooperative itself, which brokers storage, security, and financial trade [[sources/web-2024-04-04-ad6]].
- Technical systems for data cooperatives must address at least four issues: sharing management, consent systems, security (covering spoofing, tampering, repudiation, information disclosure, denial-of-service, and elevation-of-privilege threats), and state policy requirements for data protection and data intermediaries [[sources/web-2024-04-04-ad6]].
- A global patchwork of national privacy regulations makes setting up international cooperatives difficult and limits most to national operations; "federated data cooperatives" (cooperatives of cooperatives) have been proposed as a workaround [[sources/web-2024-04-04-ad6]].
- Data cooperatives are presently under-resourced and under-researched, hampered by challenges in sustainable economic models, building user trust in data sharing, and limited government financial support [[sources/web-2024-04-04-ad6]].
- Existing examples include MIDATA (health), SAOS and GISC (agriculture), with potential applications in the non-profit sector and small-business domains [[sources/web-2024-04-04-ad6]].
