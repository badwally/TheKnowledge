# Moving-Average Reclaim as Strength Signal

## Summary

The price-action pattern in which a stock, index, or ETF that has fallen below a key moving average then closes back above it "with authority" is read by Scott Redler as a statement of strength rather than weakness — a sign that momentum has shifted back to the upside and that whatever bearish news drove the break is either priced in or not as bad as feared [[sources/pdf-a0072c763cf8]].

## Key claims

- When a stock, index, or ETF reclaims the 8 & 21 day EMAs with authority, Redler treats it as a signal that the bulls are retaking control and that traders should pay attention [[sources/pdf-a0072c763cf8]].
- The reclaim is read as a statement of strength because it shifts momentum back to the upside, telling the trader that the bearish news driving the break is either priced in or not as bad as expected [[sources/pdf-a0072c763cf8]].
- SPY's June 30, 2016 post-Brexit reclaim of the 8 & 21 day EMAs (after falling from $210.87 to $188.65 on the vote) is the canonical example: SPY briefly retested the EMAs after reclaiming them and then rallied above $219 [[sources/pdf-a0072c763cf8]].
- Redler's framing positions market-priced reclaim signals above narrative/headlines: "our opinions didn't matter as much as the market's" — the reclaim is a market-revealed preference that overrides headline-derived bearishness [[sources/pdf-a0072c763cf8]].
- The TSLA Cybertruck case study works as an inverted reclaim signal: the stock did not break the 8 & 21 day EMAs at all on the unveiling backlash, and the failure-to-break itself was the read that the Cybertruck wasn't the disaster the media implied [[sources/pdf-a0072c763cf8]].
- A brief retest of the reclaimed EMAs (as in SPY post-Brexit) does not invalidate the reclaim signal — it is the rule rather than the exception in Redler's case studies [[sources/pdf-a0072c763cf8]].
