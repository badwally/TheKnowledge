# Gamma reflexivity

## Summary

Gamma reflexivity is the feedback loop created when option dealers' delta-hedging activity moves the underlying price, which in turn changes the option's delta and forces further hedging trades; Ambrus Capital identifies it as a primary mechanism converting growing option-market activity into latent equity-market fragility [[sources/pdf-04854302f962]].

## Key claims

- Large institutions — pension plans, insurance companies, hedge funds, and other end users — tend to be net buyers of equity derivatives, with most of those orders sent to market makers who become short the derivatives [[sources/pdf-04854302f962]].
- To maintain a delta-neutral portfolio, market makers offset their derivative risk by purchasing or selling equities, which inherently moves the price of the underlying asset further [[sources/pdf-04854302f962]].
- The change in the underlying price changes the derivative's delta (via gamma), forcing the market maker to buy or sell more of the underlying — in certain situations creating a feedback loop that can propel asset prices further than people realize, in both directions [[sources/pdf-04854302f962]].
- Net effect: small moves are quickly reversed, giving investors false confidence, but a large market shock can cause the system to fall apart very quickly; the growing derivatives market increases the likelihood of an exogenous event triggering this dynamic [[sources/pdf-04854302f962]].
- Gamma reflexivity's stabilizing effect is reduced on option-expiration dates because dealers unwind hedges as large option positions roll off, causing increased volatility and a corresponding drop in price [[sources/pdf-04854302f962]].
- Ambrus Capital draws an analogy between modern dealer gamma hedging and 1980s portfolio insurance: both are price-insensitive selling regimes that can pull liquidity when prices fall, with mechanically very similar destabilizing implications [[sources/pdf-04854302f962]].
