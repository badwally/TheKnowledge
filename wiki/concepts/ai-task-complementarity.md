# AI Task Complementarity

## Summary

One of the four channels through which AI affects production in Acemoglu's (2024) task-based framework: AI increases the productivity of labor in tasks it is already performing — through better information, subtask automation enabling specialization, or direct productivity augmentation — but in the empirically relevant case where sigma < 1, this may not raise wages much and always reduces the labor share [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].

## Key claims

- Task complementarity raises the marginal physical product of labor, but equilibrium wages depend on the value of marginal product, which adjusts for task price declines [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- When sigma < 1 (tasks are gross complements, as empirical evidence suggests), task prices decline more than the increase in physical productivity; the net wage effect can be essentially zero when sigma approximately equals the capital share s_K [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- With sigma ≈ 0.5 and s_K ≈ 0.4 in the US, Acemoglu concludes that task complementarities or labor-augmenting improvements "will not raise wages much" [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- Even when task complementarities increase wages via the productivity effect, they reduce the labor share — just like automation [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- Examples include AI providing better information to workers (e.g., generative AI helping customer service associates) and automating subtasks to enable human specialization (e.g., GitHub Copilot writing subroutines while programmers focus on architecture and debugging) [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- For productivity measurement purposes, the automation-vs-complementarity distinction matters less: both produce task-level cost savings that can be aggregated via Hulten's theorem [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- The three experimental studies (Peng et al., Noy and Zhang, Brynjolfsson et al.) all contain both automation and task complementarity elements at the subtask level [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
