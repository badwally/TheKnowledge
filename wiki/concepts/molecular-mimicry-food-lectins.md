# Molecular Mimicry via Food Lectins

## Summary

Proposed immunological mechanism — described in Dr. Carla Kuon's UCSF Long COVID Group Medical Visit handout — by which dietary lectins entering systemic circulation bind to selective self-tissue targets, altering the structure of self-tissue so that the immune system no longer recognizes it as self and mounts an autoimmune response; the handout asserts this process is present in all cases of auto-immune disease and is the rationale behind the lectin-elimination diet protocol [[sources/pdf-5bef1fceb745]].

## Key claims

- Lectins are technically proteins in foods that bind to carbohydrates [[sources/pdf-5bef1fceb745]].
- The problem with food lectins is that they can enter the systemic circulation and bind to selective tissue targets/organs [[sources/pdf-5bef1fceb745]].
- Through this mechanism, lectins alter the structure of self-tissue by the addition of the lectin, yielding a new structure that the immune system may not be able to recognize as self [[sources/pdf-5bef1fceb745]].
- This alteration increases the likelihood of an auto-immune response against self-tissue; the process is called molecular mimicry [[sources/pdf-5bef1fceb745]].
- The handout asserts molecular mimicry is present in all cases of auto-immune disease [[sources/pdf-5bef1fceb745]].
- Different food lectins tend to have affinity for binding to particular tissue targets; the most commonly problematic dietary lectins come from the nightshade and legume families [[sources/pdf-5bef1fceb745]].
- Reference text cited: Brostoff & Challacomb, "Food Allergy and Intolerance" — described as an immunology textbook containing a chart of lectin-tissue affinities adapted into the handout [[sources/pdf-5bef1fceb745]].
