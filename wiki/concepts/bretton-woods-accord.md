# Bretton Woods Accord

## Summary

A post-World War II international monetary agreement that pegged major currencies to the U.S. dollar (and the dollar to gold at $35 per ounce), allowing one-percent fluctuation bands enforced by central-bank intervention; the accord re-established economic stability in post-war Europe and Japan but ultimately failed, with the world defaulting to free-floating currencies in 1973 [[sources/pdf-4b87a8059b38]].

## Key claims

- The Bretton Woods Accord was a proposal undertaken towards the end of World War II to peg major currencies to the U.S. dollar [[sources/pdf-4b87a8059b38]].
- Under Bretton Woods, the dollar was in turn pegged to gold at $35 per ounce [[sources/pdf-4b87a8059b38]].
- The accord allowed currencies to fluctuate by one percent on either side of the standard, mandating that respective central banks intervene if the fluctuation was outside those limits [[sources/pdf-4b87a8059b38]].
- The accord accomplished the goals of its charter to re-establish economic stability in post-war Europe and Japan, but ultimately failed [[sources/pdf-4b87a8059b38]].
- Other similar agreements were attempted in the following decades but ultimately, in 1973, the world defaulted to free-floating currencies [[sources/pdf-4b87a8059b38]].
- All major currencies now move independently of other currencies, traded by hedge funds, banks, brokerage houses, corporations, and individuals — speculatively, transactionally, or for currency-risk hedging [[sources/pdf-4b87a8059b38]].
- The Lehman manual cites gftforex.com as the source of this historical framing [[sources/pdf-4b87a8059b38]].
