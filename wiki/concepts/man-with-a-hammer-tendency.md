# Man-With-a-Hammer Tendency

## Summary

Munger's name for the cognitive distortion whereby mastery of one tool or framework causes a person to see every problem as amenable to that tool — from the folk saying "To a man with only a hammer every problem looks pretty much like a nail" — which he argues ruined B.F. Skinner's personal reputation and afflicts smart people in every discipline [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].

## Key claims

- Named after the folk saying: "To a man with only a hammer every problem looks pretty much like a nail" [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- B.F. Skinner is the primary cautionary example: despite conducting some of the best psychology experiments ever done (ingenious use of rats and pigeons, demonstrating that random distribution of rewards kept reflexive behavior in place longest), Skinner lost most of his personal reputation by (a) overclaiming for incentive superpower to the point of believing he could create a human utopia with it and (b) displaying hardly any recognition of the power of the rest of psychology [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- Munger compares Skinner to Yale Law School's "Eddie Blanchard" about whom Harvard professors said: "Poor old Eddie Blanchard, he thinks declaratory judgments will cure cancer" [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- The tendency does not exempt smart people — it afflicts anyone who doesn't watch out [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- The primary antidote is maintaining a broad latticework of mental models across multiple disciplines: skills attenuate with disuse (Use-It-or-Lose-It Tendency), and reducing the number of practiced skills causes a man to "naturally drift into error from man with a hammer tendency" as he creates gaps in the latticework of theory he needs for understanding new experience [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- Munger argues the early psychology professoriate itself fell victim to man-with-a-hammer tendency by restricting its approach to one narrow type of repeatable laboratory experiment aimed at one psychological tendency at a time — creating an Availability-Misweighing Tendency grounded in a preference for easy-to-control data [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
