# Cyclical vs Structural Inflation

## Summary

The binary framing Zoltan Pozsar offers in his August 1, 2022 "War and Interest Rates" dispatch: today's inflation is either cyclical (a messy re-opening after Covid, exacerbated by excessive stimulus, in which case it has peaked and the Fed is near peak hawkishness) or structural (a messy transition to a multipolar world order driven by economic war, in which case inflation has barely started and could be understood as an outright instrument of war) [[sources/pdf-6ae97b9c27c7]]; the BlackRock Investment Institute's 2023 Global Outlook independently arrives at the structural side of this debate, arguing that production constraints — aging workforces, geopolitical rewiring of supply chains, and the net-zero energy transition — have replaced the Great Moderation with a new regime of persistent above-target inflation that central banks cannot resolve with rate hikes alone [[sources/pdf-won-2022-bii-global-outlook]].

## Key claims

- The expected path of Western policy rates rests on two hopes: that inflation is about to peak, and that we are near peak hawkishness — and if the first view is right, the second view is right too [[sources/pdf-6ae97b9c27c7]].
- The risk with the cyclical view is that it assumes a stable world with no geopolitical risk premia where demand management is more powerful than supply, when in fact "we live in an unstable world where geopolitical risk premia are rising and where supply-side issues are more powerful than demand management" [[sources/pdf-6ae97b9c27c7]].
- If inflation is driven by the economic war rather than stimulus, then we are not at peak hawkishness either — "if the first view is wrong, the second view is wrong too" [[sources/pdf-6ae97b9c27c7]].
- Getting right where inflation goes from here is "a matter of perspective": cyclical (re-opening + stimulus) or structural (multipolar transition with two great powers challenging U.S. hegemony) [[sources/pdf-6ae97b9c27c7]].
- Under the structural view, inflation could be understood as an outright instrument of war — Pozsar invokes Lenin: "the best way to destabilize the capitalist system [is] to debauch the currency" [[sources/pdf-6ae97b9c27c7]].
- Pozsar's diagnosis: today's inflation "is more about supply and less about demand" and "is more about geopolitics than (domestic) politics" — placing him on the structural side [[sources/pdf-6ae97b9c27c7]].
- Western central banks will not have any good options to slay structural inflation: they can reduce demand by raising rates, but if supply curves shift inward faster than demand curves, rates alone will not be enough [[sources/pdf-6ae97b9c27c7]].
- BII argues three long-term structural trends will keep production capacity constrained: aging populations causing worker shortages, persistent geopolitical tensions rewiring globalization and supply chains, and the transition to net-zero carbon emissions causing energy supply-demand mismatches [[sources/pdf-won-2022-bii-global-outlook]].
- BII expects central banks to eventually back off from rate hikes as economic damage becomes reality, but expects inflation to cool and stay "persistently higher than central bank targets of 2%" [[sources/pdf-won-2022-bii-global-outlook]].
- BII frames the supply-side constraint as a "brutal trade-off" for central banks: either crush demand to match reduced supply capacity (requiring a deep recession) or live with more inflation [[sources/pdf-won-2022-bii-global-outlook]].
