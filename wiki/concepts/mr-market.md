# Mr. Market

## Summary

The personification of stock-market quotations as a manic-depressive counterparty; in Joel Greenblatt's Columbia class, Mr. Market is the conceptual fulcrum on which his entire investment process rests, with the guarantee that "if you do good valuation work and you are right, Mr. Market will pay you back" — inefficient over one to two years, but rational and "has to get it right" over two to three [[sources/pdf-d0b9b7e97b5c]].

## Key claims

- Mr. Market's behavior is irrational in the short term and rational in the long term — "in the short term, one to two years, the market is inefficient. But in the long-term, the market has to get it right—it will pay you back in two to three years" [[sources/pdf-d0b9b7e97b5c]].
- The fact pattern Greenblatt uses to introduce Mr. Market is the disparity between the 52-week high and low of large-cap stocks like IBM, Cisco, EBay, KKD, and Google — "this has been the least volatile period in many years, and there have been 100% moves over one to two year periods" while underlying values have not moved comparably [[sources/pdf-d0b9b7e97b5c]].
- Greenblatt's stance toward the cause of Mr. Market's volatility is operational, not theoretical: "The answer is I don't know and I don't care... I just want to take advantage of prices away from value" [[sources/pdf-d0b9b7e97b5c]].
- The investor's job is to be patient enough to let Mr. Market come around: "You don't have to look at the next quarter, the next six months, if you do good valuation work... Mr. Market will pay you. In the long-term Mr. Market eventually gets it right; he is very rational" [[sources/pdf-d0b9b7e97b5c]].
- Greenblatt offers four guideposts for the semester that all flow from Mr. Market: stocks bounce around a lot, Mr. Market eventually figures it out over three years, the market closes the gap, and the investor seeks a margin of safety [[sources/pdf-d0b9b7e97b5c]].
