# Megacap Regulatory Headwind

## Summary

Andrew Slimmon's argument that the U.S. government's desire to abate the dominance of megacap S&P 500 stocks — most recently expressed via increased regulatory scrutiny — has historically been the structural force that ends megacap dominance cycles, and is now combining with sharply slower bolt-on M&A and premium valuations to argue for the S&P 500 equal-weighted to outperform the cap-weighted index in 2023 [[sources/pdf-bf1afc73ac8f]].

## Key claims

- Per Andrew Slimmon, "what has historically stunted the growth of the mega S&P 500 stocks is the U.S. government's desire to abate their dominance—and we are now seeing that with increased regulatory scrutiny" [[sources/pdf-bf1afc73ac8f]].
- Bolt-on acquisitions have boosted megasized companies' sales growth, averaging roughly 100/year during the 2010s, but only 22 acquisitions had been completed year-to-date 2022 (per Goldman Sachs US Weekly Kickstart, November 11, 2022) — a sharp drop in inorganic growth fuel [[sources/pdf-bf1afc73ac8f]].
- Slimmon argues slowing growth rates combined with premium valuations will allow the S&P 500 equal-weighted to continue to outperform the S&P 500 cap-weighted [[sources/pdf-bf1afc73ac8f]].
- Historical anchor: at their peak in 2000 the five largest tech-related stocks (Microsoft, Cisco, Nokia, Intel, IBM) comprised just over 20% of the S&P 500; they bottomed five years later at only 5% of the index — Slimmon's reference template for what regulatory pressure plus de-rating can do [[sources/pdf-bf1afc73ac8f]].
- Current setup: at their 2022 peak the five largest tech stocks (Apple, Microsoft, Google, Amazon, Tesla) comprised roughly 25% of the index; Slimmon notes 30x average valuations "will never reach the triple-digit valuations of 2000," but that the regulatory dimension is where the past most resembles the present [[sources/pdf-bf1afc73ac8f]].
- Slimmon explicitly reduces megacap exposure on this combined valuation/regulatory/M&A-slowdown argument [[sources/pdf-bf1afc73ac8f]].
