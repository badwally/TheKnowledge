# US–EU Inflation Near-Identical (2020–2022)

## Summary

Apollo's empirical observation that US and European inflation traveled almost identical paths from January 2019 to October 2022 — even though the US fiscal response to Covid was roughly twice the size of the EU's as a share of GDP — which Apollo cites as strong evidence that price rises were not entirely driven by demand and that supply-chain problems associated with Covid were at least a partial cause [[sources/pdf-d196e853ebc0]].

## Key claims

- US and European inflation over the past two years have been almost identical despite the fiscal response to Covid being double the size in the US relative to Europe [[sources/pdf-d196e853ebc0]].
- Apollo's exhibit for the period 2020–2021 puts the US federal surplus/deficit at –15% of GDP in 2020 and –12% in 2021, versus the Euro Area 19's –7% in 2020 and –5% in 2021 [[sources/pdf-d196e853ebc0]].
- If inflation were primarily a monetary problem, a much more aggressive fiscal response in the US should have led to higher headline and core inflation in the US than in Europe [[sources/pdf-d196e853ebc0]].
- That divergence didn't happen; in fact, by mid-to-late 2022 US inflation had begun to moderate while EU inflation continued to surge [[sources/pdf-d196e853ebc0]].
- The similar path of inflation in the US and Europe "strongly suggests that price rises have not been entirely driven by demand but at least partly by supply-chain problems associated with Covid as well" [[sources/pdf-d196e853ebc0]].
- The point matters because if supply-chain issues were the primary culprit — increased cost of imported goods (especially from China), increased costs of shipping from ports to trucking and otherwise — then "the rational course would have been for policymakers to just wait for those issues to sort themselves out" rather than engineer demand destruction [[sources/pdf-d196e853ebc0]].
