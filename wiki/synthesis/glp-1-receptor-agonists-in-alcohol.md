# GLP-1 receptor agonists in alcohol and substance use disorders

## Synthesis

### Neurochemical substrate

GLP-1-producing neurons in the nucleus tractus solitarius project directly to the ventral tegmental area (VTA) and nucleus accumbens (NAcc), providing the anatomical substrate by which a gut-brain peptide can gate mesolimbic dopamine signalling [[sources/pubmed-22128031]]. A recently characterised inhibitory GLP-1 circuit in the lateral septum modulates reward processing and alcohol intake in rodents, showing that relevant circuitry extends beyond the canonical VTA-NAcc axis [[sources/pubmed-40245495]]. Mechanistic reviews converge on a model in which GLP-1R activation dampens phasic dopamine responses to drug- and alcohol-paired cues while engaging stress and homeostatic systems that contribute to craving [[sources/pubmed-29337226]] [[sources/pubmed-40843757]] [[sources/pubmed-35300299]].

### Preclinical evidence — alcohol

In rodent models, GLP-1R agonism reliably reduces alcohol consumption and relapse-like drinking. Brain-site-specific microinjections of exendin-4 demonstrate that mesolimbic GLP-1R activation is sufficient to inhibit alcohol intake and operant responding for palatable food [[sources/pubmed-33352692]]. Semaglutide reduces alcohol intake and relapse-like drinking in both male and female rats [[sources/pubmed-37295046]], and a GLP-1 + amylin receptor agonist combination produces further reductions in alcohol consumption in both sexes, suggesting additive engagement of appetitive systems [[sources/pubmed-39639536]]. Reviews summarising the broader preclinical literature describe convergent reductions in alcohol-induced accumbal dopamine release, conditioned place preference, and operant self-administration across analogues [[sources/pubmed-29337226]] [[sources/pubmed-40722294]].

### Preclinical evidence — other substances

Beyond alcohol, exendin-4 reduces cocaine self-administration in mice [[sources/pubmed-26072178]], and mechanistic reviews of GLP-1R modulation in cocaine use disorder describe convergent suppression of psychostimulant reward [[sources/pubmed-35300299]]. Translational reviews place these findings inside a broader framework in which GLP-1 signalling reduces the reinforcing effects of nicotine, opioids, and psychostimulants, framing GLP-1R agonism as an emerging pharmacotherapeutic target for substance use disorders [[sources/pubmed-39032839]] [[sources/pubmed-37063267]] [[sources/pubmed-40302255]] [[sources/pubmed-40843757]] [[sources/pubmed-40616879]].

### Human clinical and observational evidence

Human evidence is heterogeneous but converging. A systematic review of GLP-1 RAs in substance use disorders reports signals of reduced consumption across multiple substances while noting small sample sizes and methodological variability [[sources/pubmed-41552827]]. A systematic review focused on nicotine cessation in psychiatric populations finds preliminary support for GLP-1 RAs as adjuncts and emphasises the need for randomised trials [[sources/pubmed-39529123]]. Mixed-methods social media analyses identify spontaneous self-reports of reduced alcohol, nicotine, and stimulant use among GLP-1 users, alongside changes in compulsive behaviours and libido [[sources/pubmed-38928616]]. Pharmacoepidemiologic work contrasts elevated post-bariatric addiction risk with reduced addiction risk on GLP-1 therapies, supporting a pharmacological rather than weight-loss-mediated mechanism [[sources/pubmed-39818408]]. Narrative and IUPHAR-style reviews summarise early-phase clinical trials in alcohol use disorder reporting modest reductions in heavy-drinking days and craving with semaglutide and liraglutide [[sources/pubmed-40722294]] [[sources/pubmed-39032839]] [[sources/pubmed-40302255]].

### Open questions and gaps

The candidate set contains few primary randomised controlled trials; most human evidence is observational, retrospective, or extracted from systematic reviews. Dose-response relationships, durability after discontinuation, and efficacy in stimulant and opioid use disorders remain under-characterised. Mechanistic dissection of which circuits — mesolimbic, lateral septum, hypothalamic, or extended amygdala — carry the anti-addiction effect is incomplete [[sources/pubmed-40245495]] [[sources/pubmed-40843757]].
