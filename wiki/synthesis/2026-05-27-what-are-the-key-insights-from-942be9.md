While the specific "ASME A17.1-2025: Safety Code for Elevators and Escalators - ANSI Blog" article is not included in the provided sources, the sources contain extensive engineering guidelines regarding elevator safety codes—including ASME standards and the Canadian equivalent, CSA B44—and how condominium boards must integrate code updates into long-horizon proactive planning. 

Here are the key insights drawn from the sources regarding elevator management and safety code compliance:

**1. Budgeting for Code-Driven "Get Ahead" Costs**
When elevator safety codes (such as CSA B44 or ASME) are updated, the new regulations bring extensive changes. For example, recent code updates have introduced mandates for "Unintended Car Movement" and "Ascending Car" protection [1] [[nlm:50f51b59-1118-4cc3-852c-4cf86bba8a35]], larger refuge spaces and new railings on the car top [2] [[nlm:50f51b59-1118-4cc3-852c-4cf86bba8a35]], and strict requirements for backup emergency lighting and cab glass types [3] [[nlm:50f51b59-1118-4cc3-852c-4cf86bba8a35]]. While these codes are generally not retroactive for older, operating elevators, any major modernization at the end of the system's useful life will require full compliance with the newest code [4, 5]. Long-horizon capital planning must account for these "Get Ahead Costs"—which are the premium capital expenses required to adapt the building to new safety codes and standards [6, 7].

**2. Identifying "Technological Obsolescence" as a Failure Risk**
In proactive capital planning, an elevator does not need to suffer a mechanical breakdown to require a replacement; it may simply become technologically obsolete [8] [[nlm:97fc8200-55c5-433c-853e-b5861dd8ace0]]. Condition assessments reveal that many existing elevators use controls or motor drives from manufacturers that are no longer in business or no longer support older products [9, 10]. Proactive boards must track the supply chain for their specific elevator controls and plan for a full modernization before replacement parts become impossible to source, which would result in prolonged, unbudgeted downtime.

**3. Mandatory ASME/OSHA Inspections and Specialty Engineering**
Because elevators are critical life-safety infrastructure, the American Society of Mechanical Engineers (ASME) and the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) mandate specific, periodic testing and inspections [11] [[nlm:3bd1b45f-1cfd-4abb-afe6-5ae98bafbe46]]. A standard visual reserve study inspection is insufficient for long-term planning. Proactive boards must utilize certified elevator contractors for routine maintenance [12] [[nlm:3bd1b45f-1cfd-4abb-afe6-5ae98bafbe46]], and they should commission a comprehensive review by a dedicated elevating device engineer (for example, around year 25) to confirm the true remaining service life of controllers and safety devices before executing a massive capital replacement [13] [[nlm:97fc8200-55c5-433c-853e-b5861dd8ace0]].

**4. Analyzing Maintenance Contracts to Forecast Capital Needs**
Proactive planning requires tracking the financial efficiency of aging systems. Older elevator technologies, such as fixed-speed AC motors or hydraulic drives, suffer from poor ride quality, high energy inefficiency, and environmental risks like hydraulic oil leaks [14, 15]. Furthermore, boards must monitor their elevator maintenance contracts. If an association has a "maintenance-only" contract that excludes parts, and the monthly billing for extra repairs begins to skyrocket, it is a leading indicator that the system is failing and a major capital modernization must be accelerated [16] [[nlm:50f51b59-1118-4cc3-852c-4cf86bba8a35]].
