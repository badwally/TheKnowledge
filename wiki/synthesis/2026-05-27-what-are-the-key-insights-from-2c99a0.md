# What are the key insights from "Bill Text" in the context of Condominium and HOA management with an emphasis on integrated long-horizon proactive planning? The source describes: SB 721 Building standards: decks and balconies: inspection.

## Synthesis

**1. The "Condo Exemption" and Condo Conversion Mandates**
The most critical insight for HOA management within the text of SB 721 is that the law explicitly **excludes** existing Common Interest Developments (condominiums and HOAs) from its specific provisions [1] [[sources/web-2018-09-17-ca9]]. Instead, SB 721 applies primarily to multifamily rental buildings [2] [[sources/web-2018-09-17-ca9]]. 

However, it heavily impacts long-horizon planning for **condominium conversions**. If a rental building subject to SB 721 is proposed for conversion into condominiums to be sold to the public, the required exterior elevated element inspections must be fully conducted prior to the first close of escrow [3] [[sources/web-2018-09-17-ca9]]. Furthermore, written confirmation that all recommended repairs have been completed must be submitted to the Department of Real Estate as a condition of receiving a final public report and certificate of occupancy [1, 3]. This forces developers to front-load capital repairs and ensures newly formed HOA boards inherit a safe, documented baseline rather than hidden deferred maintenance. 

*(Note: Existing condominiums in California are governed by SB 721's companion legislation, SB 326, which requires a 9-year cycle and a 95% statistical confidence sampling).*

**2. Establishing an Institutional Baseline and Data Continuity**
A major challenge in long-horizon HOA planning is the loss of institutional memory when boards and managers turn over. SB 721 combats this by legally mandating the creation of longitudinal data. 
*   **Creating the Baseline:** The inspection report must contain photographs, test results, and narratives specifically detailed enough to "establish a baseline of the condition of the components inspected that can be compared to the results of subsequent inspections" [4] [[sources/web-2018-09-17-ca9]]. 
*   **Mandatory Retention:** To ensure this data integrates into long-term capital planning, copies of all inspection reports must be maintained in the building owner’s permanent records for at least two inspection cycles (12 years) and must be disclosed to buyers during any subsequent sale [5] [[sources/web-2018-09-17-ca9]].

**3. The Shift to Cyclical, Sample-Based Predictive Inspections**
The legislation forces building operators to abandon the "fix-it-when-it-breaks" methodology. 
*   **Targeted Infrastructure:** It mandates inspections of "exterior elevated elements" (balconies, decks, stairways, walkways) located more than six feet above ground that rely substantially on wood or wood-based products for structural support [6] [[sources/web-2018-09-17-ca9]]. 
*   **The 6-Year Cycle:** Inspections must be completed by January 1, 2025, and recur strictly every six years thereafter [2, 4]. 
*   **Statistical Sampling:** Inspectors are required to physically evaluate the performance of load-bearing components and associated waterproofing elements using a sample size of at least 15 percent of each type of exterior elevated element [7] [[sources/web-2018-09-17-ca9]].

**4. Accelerated Repair Economics and Financial Penalties**
SB 721 imposes strict deadlines that require operating entities to maintain high financial liquidity or robust reserves, as deferring maintenance is no longer a legal option once an issue is identified.
*   **Emergency Repairs:** If an inspector identifies an immediate threat to occupant safety, the owner must immediately prevent access, perform emergency shoring or repairs, and notify the local enforcement agency within 15 days [4, 8, 9].
*   **Standard Repairs:** For non-emergency corrective work, the building owner must apply for a permit within 120 days of receiving the report, and then has exactly 120 days to complete the repairs once the permit is approved [9, 10].
*   **Punitive Financial Risks:** Failing to comply with these repair timelines triggers aggressive enforcement. Local authorities will assess civil penalties of $100 to $500 per day until the repairs are completed, and these penalties can be recorded as a building safety lien against the property [10, 11].
