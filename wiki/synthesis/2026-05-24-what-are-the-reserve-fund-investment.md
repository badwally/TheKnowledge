### Consolidated Insights & Analysis

**1. Investment Rules & Risk Tolerance**
The acceptable risk profile for reserve investments varies significantly by jurisdiction. [[sources/web-2022-01-01-a78]]
* **The U.S. approach** heavily prioritizes absolute principal protection. California explicitly bans stocks or high-risk investments [13] [[sources/web-2022-01-01-a78]]. Both Florida and New Jersey legally permit the commingling of operating and reserve funds *for investment purposes only*, provided they are accounted for separately [1, 25]. 
* **The Canadian approach** specifies "eligible securities" at a provincial level. Ontario strictly limits investments to government-backed bonds or CDIC/FSRA-insured GICs and mandates a formal written investment plan corresponding to the cash-flow needs dictated by the reserve study [36-38]. British Columbia permits a slightly higher risk tolerance, allowing investments in A-rated corporate bonds and BBB-rated fixed-income ETFs [50, 51]. Alberta is an outlier, allowing up to 15% of a reserve portfolio to be invested in dividend-paying common shares [54] [[sources/web-2026-01-01-3bf]].

**2. Audit and Disclosure Frameworks**
* **U.S. Disclosures:** Financial reporting requirements in states like Florida are tiered based on revenue (e.g., compilations for $150k+, reviews for $300k+, audits for $500k+) [5, 6]. New Jersey has introduced rigorous new standards requiring a 30-year funding plan showing the balance hitting zero, allowing associations to fund at 85% only if they provide a conspicuous 20-point font disclosure to all unit owners and prospective buyers [27, 28]. [[sources/pdf-house-bill-2025-h0913er-docx]] [[sources/pdf-fc88aa6c2822]]
* **Canadian Disclosures:** Canada generally leans toward mandatory annual audits. In Ontario, an audit is mandatory for any corporation with more than 25 units unless unanimously waived [42] [[sources/pdf-994c881efb49]]. Ontario also utilizes a standardized "Notice of Future Funding" (NOFF) document that must be distributed to owners within 15 days of the board proposing a funding plan based on the triennial reserve fund study [71, 72].

**3. Lender Review Mechanics (Fannie Mae & Freddie Mac Impact)**
The collapse of the Champlain Towers South in Surfside, FL prompted massive changes to US lending guidelines. Effective August 2026, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac are eliminating the "limited review" process for condos over 10 units, meaning **all transactions will undergo a full lender review** [9] . Effective January 2027, associations must contribute a **minimum of 15% of their annual operating budget to reserves**, unless an updated reserve study (conducted within the last 36 months) justifies a lower amount using a full or threshold funding model [10, 11]. "Baseline" funding (targeting a near-zero balance) is no longer permitted by these lenders [11, 73]. Failure to comply results in "non-warrantable" status, causing properties to drop in value by an estimated 5% to 30% [74] [[sources/web-2026-03-24-bb6]]. 

**4. Tax Treatment Divergence**
* **United States:** Under IRS Form 1120-H, HOAs and Condominiums are taxed on "non-exempt function income" at a flat rate of 30% (after a $100 deduction). **Interest earned on reserve funds is strictly considered taxable income** regardless of the nature of the upcoming capital projects [8, 75]. [[sources/web-2026-05-15-040]]
* **Canada:** Condominium corporations are classified as Not-For-Profit Organizations (NPOs). They are generally exempt from annual corporate income tax [45] [[sources/pdf-aa9367a8a4a9]]. However, they must still file T2 returns and, if they generate over $10,000 in investment income or hold over $200,000 in assets, they must file a T1044 NPO Information Return (and CO-17.SP in Quebec) [44] [[sources/web-2025-11-06-d1c]].
