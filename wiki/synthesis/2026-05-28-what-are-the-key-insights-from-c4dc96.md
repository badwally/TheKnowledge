# Ozempic, GLP-1 Drugs &amp; The Truth About Weight Loss | Former FDA Chief Explains

**Channel:** Live Well Be Well with Sarah Ann Macklin  
**Duration:** PT56M22S  
**Views:** 5358  
**Published:** 2025-

## Synthesis

**The Battle Between Reward and Aversive Circuits**
Former FDA Chief Dr. David Kessler explains that obesity is fundamentally driven by neural reward circuits that have been hijacked by hyper-palatable, ultra-processed foods [1, 2]. He clarifies that **dopamine is not simply a pleasure molecule, but a "wanting" and learning molecule that locks in environmental and emotional cues** (such as the time of day or feelings of stress), creating a powerful, cue-induced compulsion to eat [3, 4]. 

To break this addiction, GLP-1 medications target the brain's aversive circuits—specifically in the hindbrain (the area postrema and nucleus tractus solitarius, which contain the brain's vomiting center) [5] [[nlm:1f3fde93-f23a-42fb-993d-9df14b706863]]. By intentionally dosing the medication to push a patient right to the **"edge of nausea," the drugs successfully tamp down and overpower the hyperactive reward and addiction circuits**, similar to how food poisoning creates a strong aversion to a specific meal [6-8].

**The Dangers of "Microdosing" and Vanity Weight Loss**
In stark contrast to some other creators' views on microdosing, both Dr. Kessler and the host express deep concern over the growing trend of individuals **"microdosing" or manipulating doses just to lose the last few pounds for cosmetic reasons** [9, 10]. Dr. Kessler stresses that these powerful medications were explicitly designed to treat visceral adiposity and severe metabolic dysfunction, not for vanity weight loss among people with healthy BMIs [11, 12].

Taking these drugs without proper medical supervision or to achieve cosmetic thinness carries severe risks, including **profound malnourishment and the potential to trigger eating disorders**. Because patients often consume fewer than 1,000 calories a day while on the medication, Dr. Kessler warns that this drastic reduction can essentially mimic "forced anorexia" [12, 13].

**Dosing Titration and Long-Term Behavioral Change**
Pharmaceutical companies have optimized the dosing and titration of these drugs to sustain that critical "edge of nausea" without pushing the patient into full gastrointestinal illness [6] [[nlm:1f3fde93-f23a-42fb-993d-9df14b706863]]. However, because patients typically only stay on the medication for 8 to 9 months, **the drug simply acts to put the food addiction on ice** [8, 14]. 

Once the medication is stopped, the addictive reward circuits remain intact, and the weight will inevitably return [15] [[nlm:1f3fde93-f23a-42fb-993d-9df14b706863]]. Therefore, the ultimate goal of the treatment is to use the window of silenced cravings to actively change one's relationship with food, build a support system, and implement sustainable nutritional and behavioral tools [15-17].
