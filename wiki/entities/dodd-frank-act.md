# Dodd-Frank Act

## Summary

U.S. financial-regulation statute passed in 2008 to improve regulatory measures protecting the public from insurance companies' and banks' risky behavior; Ambrus Capital argues that its bank risk limits, while a net benefit, have introduced second-order microstructure effects that amplify stress cascades [[sources/pdf-04854302f962]].

## Key facts

- Passed in 2008 to improve regulatory measures to protect the public from insurance companies' and banks' risky behavior [[sources/pdf-04854302f962]].
- Changed how large banks operate on Wall Street by capping the amount of risk they can assume at any given time — a rule Ambrus Capital characterizes as a net benefit for financial markets [[sources/pdf-04854302f962]].
- Pre-Dodd-Frank, a bank trader had a cushion of risk and could use the bank's balance sheet to fund a position that moved against them; under the new framework, traders can only have a certain amount of risk on their book at one time [[sources/pdf-04854302f962]].
- When traders are forced over their risk limits due to market movement, they must hedge or exit very quickly — and this hedging is price-insensitive (a compliance matter), so hedges are often purchased at exorbitant prices, which further moves the asset and polarizes volatility [[sources/pdf-04854302f962]].
- Combined with options-dealer concentration (four banks representing 89.3% of total banking-industry notional, per the OCC's Q3 2021 report), the Dodd-Frank framework can mechanically pull liquidity from struggling securities during market stress [[sources/pdf-04854302f962]].
