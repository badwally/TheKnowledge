# Zoltan Pozsar

## Summary

Strategist at Credit Suisse Economics in the Investment Solutions & Sustainability division; author of three 2022 dispatches summarized in this corpus — the May 13, 2022 "Ride of the 'Volkyries'," which argues the Fed is pursuing demand destruction through negative wealth effects and that volatility and illiquidity are by design [[sources/pdf-3d3d42a0717a]], the August 1, 2022 "War and Interest Rates," which reframes today's inflation as the consequence of an escalating economic war between the consumer-driven West and the production-driven East and identifies three pillars of the low-inflation world (cheap U.S. immigrant labor, cheap Chinese goods, cheap Russian gas) whose collapse will keep inflation structurally high [[sources/pdf-6ae97b9c27c7]], and the August 24, 2022 "War and Industrial Policy," which extends the thesis by arguing that the divorce of Chimerica and Eurussia and the formation of a Russia-China "Chussia" plus a wider Eurasian "TRICKs" alliance is the macro driver of de-globalization-fueled inflation, naming the regime change "The Great Reflation" [[sources/pdf-503a04772afb]].

## Key facts

- Listed contributor on the May 13, 2022 Credit Suisse dispatch "Ride of the 'Volkyries'," with phone 212 538 3779 and email zoltan.pozsar@credit-suisse.com [[sources/pdf-3d3d42a0717a]].
- Also listed as the contributor on the August 24, 2022 Credit Suisse Economics dispatch "War and Industrial Policy," published under Investment Solutions & Sustainability / Global, with the same phone (212 538 3779) and email (zoltan.pozsar@credit-suisse.com) [[sources/pdf-503a04772afb]].
- Listed contributor on the August 1, 2022 Credit Suisse Economics dispatch "War and Interest Rates," published under Investment Solutions & Sustainability / Global, with the same phone (212 538 3779) and email (zoltan.pozsar@credit-suisse.com) [[sources/pdf-6ae97b9c27c7]].
- Author of an earlier dispatch titled "Volcker Moment" published February 16, 2022, which made the policy case for the Fed to inject volatility into markets in order to control domestic services inflation through asset prices [[sources/pdf-3d3d42a0717a]].
- Visited Bill Dudley for an interview during the summer of 2008 to join the Markets Group of the Federal Reserve Bank of New York after sending Dudley a brief note describing concerns about the financial plumbing [[sources/pdf-3d3d42a0717a]].
- Frames the May 13, 2022 dispatch around five topics: the Fed call versus the Fed put, monetary heroes and anti-heroes, the Fed's impossible trinity, the need to "invert" thinking about recession risks, and the Fed's options to inject more volatility [[sources/pdf-3d3d42a0717a]].
- Argues the Fed has a singular mission of slaying inflation and won't be intimidated by curve inversions and asset price corrections, but will be emboldened by them to hike more than what is priced [[sources/pdf-3d3d42a0717a]].
- Explicitly declines to provide a target for the S&P 500 but reasons through three reference levels: at 4,000 the Fed seems unsatisfied, at 3,500 the post-pandemic gains would be erased, and at 2,500 some pre-pandemic gains would also be eaten into [[sources/pdf-3d3d42a0717a]].
- Recommends the Fed "stop talking" — hike 50 bps and sell $50 billion of 10-year notes the day after, and put an end to press conferences — to keep the market on its toes [[sources/pdf-3d3d42a0717a]].
- Flags the crypto sell-off as an "unexpected bonus" for the Fed, albeit one to watch for spillovers into STIR markets through the "par problems" of some stablecoins [[sources/pdf-3d3d42a0717a]].
- In the August 24, 2022 dispatch, asserts that "the low inflation world had three pillars: cheap immigrant labor keeping nominal wage growth 'stagnant' in the U.S., cheap Chinese goods raising real wages amid stagnant nominal wages, and cheap Russian natural gas fueling German industry and Europe more broadly" [[sources/pdf-503a04772afb]].
- Adopts Niall Ferguson's term "Chimerica" for the U.S.-China commercial union and coins "Eurussia" for the EU-Russia gas-and-industry union, framing both as having broken down through messy divorces [[sources/pdf-503a04772afb]].
- Frames the China-Russia "special relationship" as "Chussia" — a marriage of commodities and industry uniting the largest commodity producer (Russia) and the factory of the world (China), potentially in control of Eurasia [[sources/pdf-503a04772afb]].
- Coins "TRICKs" — Turkey, Russia, Iran, China, North Korea — as the Eurasian "alliance of the sanctioned" getting ever closer economically and militarily, which forces the friends of the U.S. to play pragmatically [[sources/pdf-503a04772afb]].
- Cites Dale Copeland's book "Economic Interdependence and War" and its "theory of trade expectations" as the analytical backbone for understanding both the conflict and the inflation outlook [[sources/pdf-503a04772afb]].
- Argues that "if trust drove globalization, and globalization drove 'The Great Moderation,' distrust will drive de-globalization, and de-globalization 'The Great Reflation'" [[sources/pdf-503a04772afb]].
- Argues that the post-divorce path from an "L"-shaped recession to an "L/" recovery must come from investments with help from the government, because consumption cannot generate the "/" without the old import "spouses" [[sources/pdf-503a04772afb]].
- Identifies three "moments of reckoning" exposing supply-chain fragility: depleted U.S. artillery stockpiles in Ukraine (annual U.S. production of 155mm artillery shells would last only about two weeks at Ukraine consumption rates), multi-year replacement delays at Raytheon (Stingers) and ASML (lithography), and Taiwan's blockade demonstrating that chip supply can be cut [[sources/pdf-503a04772afb]].
- Cites the ASML CEO Peter Wennick anecdote that the executive of a very large industrial conglomerate told him "they're buying washing machines to rip out the chips to put them into industrial modules" as evidence that chip supply has hit industrial production [[sources/pdf-503a04772afb]].
- Argues that forecasts of a rapid deceleration of inflation are "naively optimistic" because the TRICKs poking holes in Pax Americana — which underwrote globalization and lowflation — mean inflation is a big risk [[sources/pdf-503a04772afb]].
- Uses an autobiographical anchor — being eleven years old when Viktor Orban demanded Soviet troop withdrawal from Hungary on June 16, 1989, just months before the Fall of the Berlin Wall on November 9, 1989 — to argue that geopolitical "Damascene Conversions" do happen and that South Korea's "strategic ambiguity" could be a precursor to its own Orban-like moment [[sources/pdf-503a04772afb]].
- In the August 1, 2022 dispatch, opens with the thesis that "war is inflationary" and that wars come in many forms — hot wars, cold wars, and Pippa Malmgren's "hot wars in cold places" (cyberspace, space, deep underwater) — to which Pozsar adds "corridors of power" in Washington, Beijing, and Moscow, where great powers are waging "hot economic wars" involving the flow of technologies, goods, and commodities [[sources/pdf-6ae97b9c27c7]].
- Argues in the Aug 1 dispatch that "inflation did not start with the hot war in Ukraine, but the war did fan the inflationary currents that had been under way already," and that understanding today's inflation as the result of an escalating economic war and a lingering pandemic is important — for if war and zero-Covid policies stay, the view that inflation is mostly cyclical is wrong [[sources/pdf-6ae97b9c27c7]].
- Reports in the Aug 1 dispatch having visited over 150 clients in eight European capitals over six weeks, finding that the expected path of Western policy rates rests on two hopes: that inflation is about to peak, and that we are near peak hawkishness [[sources/pdf-6ae97b9c27c7]].
- States in the Aug 1 dispatch that the low inflation world stood on three pillars: cheap immigrant labor keeping U.S. service-sector wages stagnant, cheap goods from China raising living standards amid stagnant wages, and cheap Russian gas powering German industry and the EU more broadly [[sources/pdf-6ae97b9c27c7]].
- Frames Russia and China as the "main guarantors of macro peace" — Russia as a "G-SIB of Commodities" and China as a "G-SIB of Factories" — providing all the cheap stuff that was the source of deflation fears in the West, which gave central banks the license for years of QE [[sources/pdf-6ae97b9c27c7]].
- Argues in the Aug 1 dispatch that central banks have gone from fighting deflation with asset price inflation to fighting inflation with asset price deflation, adapting to a world that has gone from "too much stuff and not enough demand" to "not enough stuff and too much demand" [[sources/pdf-6ae97b9c27c7]].
- Asserts in the Aug 1 dispatch that today's inflation "is more about supply and less about demand" and "is more about geopolitics than (domestic) politics" [[sources/pdf-6ae97b9c27c7]].
- Argues the policymakers to follow are no longer central bankers but heads of state at the pinnacle of power — "translating speeches from Russian and Chinese and catching high-fives as opposed to fist bumps are becoming more important than parsing subtle grammatical nuances in central bankers' policy utterances" [[sources/pdf-6ae97b9c27c7]].
- Recommends in the Aug 1 dispatch that to understand the path of inflation, one should "read less Friedman and Schwartz and much-much more Brzezinski and Mackinder," because the special relationship between China and Russia covers most of Eurasia, and Eurasia has most of the stuff needed to meet inflation targets in the West [[sources/pdf-6ae97b9c27c7]].
- Frames the "general mobilization" lens as a stress test of the war-economy thesis: Russia mobilizing industry for the war effort, France's Macron calling for "general mobilization" to save resources, China mobilizing through zero-Covid lockdowns, and Powell pursuing a "general mobilization" of Paul Volcker's legacy [[sources/pdf-6ae97b9c27c7]].
- Argues in the Aug 1 dispatch that whether Jerome Powell will be remembered by economic historians "as Paul Volcker or Arthur Burns depends on the course of the economic war" [[sources/pdf-6ae97b9c27c7]].
- Frames the choice as between two views of inflation: cyclical (a messy re-opening after Covid, exacerbated by excessive stimulus, in which case inflation has peaked) or structural (a messy transition to a multipolar world order, in which case inflation has barely started) [[sources/pdf-6ae97b9c27c7]].
- Quotes Lenin in the Aug 1 dispatch — "the best way to destabilize the capitalist system [is] to debauch the currency" — to argue that under the structural view, inflation could be understood as an outright instrument of war [[sources/pdf-6ae97b9c27c7]].
- Cites Pippa Malmgren's observation that Peter Drucker delayed publishing "The End of Economic Man: The Origins of Totalitarianism" until 1939 because he was frightened to say WWII was about to begin, with Malmgren stating she now feels his angst as she writes of her belief that "we have already entered WWIII" [[sources/pdf-6ae97b9c27c7]].
- Distinguishes peacetime macro investing — practiced by George Soros, Stanley Druckenmiller, Paul Tudor Jones, and Louis Bacon during the post-Cold War era and trading the first three of the four prices of money (par, interest, foreign exchange) — from wartime macro investing that drives the fourth price of money: the price level and its derivative, inflation [[sources/pdf-6ae97b9c27c7]].
- Cites two figures who practiced macro investing during conflict: John Maynard Keynes during the interwar period (drawing on "The Economic Consequences of the Peace") and Nathan Meyer Rothschild who "loaded up on gilts when he learned before everyone else of Wellington's victory over Napoleon at the Battle of Waterloo" [[sources/pdf-6ae97b9c27c7]].
- Diagnoses today's inflation as "the 'revenge' of headline inflation, combined with an extremely tight labor market in the service sector in the U.S." — driven by protectionist immigration policy in the U.S. (or Brexit in the U.K.), early retirements, much less global labor mobility due to the pandemic, and the war in Ukraine pushing food and energy prices [[sources/pdf-6ae97b9c27c7]].
- Argues that in a structurally tight labor market, food and energy prices — basic, everyday necessities — "are especially dangerous" because workers demand higher wages not when discretionary items like TV screens and cars cost more, but when necessities cost more [[sources/pdf-6ae97b9c27c7]].
- Pre-announces in the Aug 1 dispatch a forthcoming series on what it will take to fix the supply-side issues of the West, why "Bretton Woods III" is the East's "natural" response to the West's supply-side fixes, and why central banks won't be able to get out of QE (no QT) even as inflation is likely to remain high [[sources/pdf-6ae97b9c27c7]].
