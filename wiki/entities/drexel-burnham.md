# Drexel Burnham

## Summary

Investment bank that dominated the liquidity provision and intermediation of the high-yield bond market in the 1980s; its February 1990 bankruptcy — driven by DOJ and SEC charges for insider trading and other illegal activities — combined with the concurrent S&L industry liquidation to freeze the credit pipes for below-investment-grade borrowers, triggering a credit-event deleveraging that presaged the 1990 recession [[sources/pdf-f139d7bd1924]].

## Key facts

- Dominated the liquidity provision and intermediation of the high-yield bond market [[sources/pdf-f139d7bd1924]].
- Many of Drexel's clients were Savings and Loan institutions [[sources/pdf-f139d7bd1924]].
- Was defending itself from a slew of charges by the DOJ and SEC regarding insider trading and other illegal activities [[sources/pdf-f139d7bd1924]].
- The pressure ultimately caused the firm to declare bankruptcy in February 1990 [[sources/pdf-f139d7bd1924]].
- The bankruptcy, combined with the S&L industry's government-funded liquidation (costing over $160 billion), resulted in a freezing of the credit pipes for below-investment-grade borrowers — "a major buyer became a forced liquidator, and the bank that dominated the liquidity provision and intermediation of the high yield was abruptly shut down" [[sources/pdf-f139d7bd1924]].
- After the bankruptcy, Salomon Brothers and a couple of other desks were asked by Drexel traders to bid competitively for their inventory [[sources/pdf-f139d7bd1924]].
- Per Constan, the Drexel inventory liquidation was "my first experience of a fire sale of assets by a major end user and liquidity provider" [[sources/pdf-f139d7bd1924]].
- The equity drawdown associated with this deleveraging began July 16, 1990, lasted three months, peaked at 19.02%, and recovered in 119 days after the low [[sources/pdf-f139d7bd1924]].
- Per Constan, this deleveraging episode "was a classic credit boom bust cycle with a meaningful impact on the real economy" [[sources/pdf-f139d7bd1924]].
