# William McChesney Martin

## Summary

Federal Reserve chair preceding the Volcker era; per Kai Volatility's Q2 2022 commentary, the central figure of the 1967 "William McChesney Martin Moment" — a less-discussed episode in which the Fed ratcheted the funds rate from 3.5% to 10.5% over June 1967 to June 1969 (a 7-point increase), only to see an 11-month recession follow with inflation barely affected, foreshadowing the structural limits of Fed-rate-tool inflation-fighting when inflation is driven by political forces [[sources/pdf-ed56b074fb11]].

## Key facts

- Per Kai Volatility, was Fed chair during the "William McChesney Martin Moment" of 1967, a precursor to the heralded "Volcker Moment" of 1979 that history has largely forgotten [[sources/pdf-ed56b074fb11]].
- From June 1967 to June 1969 the Federal Reserve under Martin ratcheted the fed funds rate up "a gut-wrenching 7%" from 3.5% to 10.5% [[sources/pdf-ed56b074fb11]].
- The recession that followed lasted for 11 months, beginning in December 1969 and ending in November 1970 [[sources/pdf-ed56b074fb11]].
- "Despite mounting unemployment, unlike in the case of Volcker's heralded dual recession, inflation was barely affected" — a result Kai Volatility uses to argue that demand-side Fed tightening has structural limits when inflation is driven by political forces [[sources/pdf-ed56b074fb11]].
- Listed alongside Arthur Burns and William Miller as one of three pre-Volcker Fed chairs whose stories "are all too conveniently sullied or brushed under the rug" by modern Fed-credibility narratives, despite their actions being "literally the same" as Volcker's [[sources/pdf-ed56b074fb11]].
- After the Martin tightening cycle ended, faith was lost in the Federal Reserve as the economy declined into stagflation, leading directly to the 1971 Nixon price-and-wage freeze [[sources/pdf-ed56b074fb11]].
