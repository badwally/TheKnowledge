# Federal Reserve

## Summary

The U.S. central bank, whose May 2022 posture Zoltan Pozsar characterizes as a singular mission to slay inflation — a Fed that is "writing a call option on risk assets" and will keep tightening financial conditions until yields rise, stocks fall further, and housing turns, with the volatility injected by design rather than a cause for concern [[sources/pdf-3d3d42a0717a]]; in his August 1, 2022 follow-up Pozsar reframes the Fed's effort as a "general mobilization" of Paul Volcker's legacy and warns that whether Powell is remembered as Volcker or Arthur Burns depends on the course of an economic war the Fed cannot control with rates alone [[sources/pdf-6ae97b9c27c7]]; the July 13, 2022 Q3-2022 IACFM presentation makes a parallel prescriptive argument that a stagflationary environment requires restrictive monetary policy and that prematurely easing — as Arthur Burns did in the 1970s — has been a serious policy mistake [[sources/pdf-853a7755a470]]; per ING's December 2022 outlook, the Fed signaled in late 2022 a step down to 50 bp incremental rate hikes with a higher ultimate rate than indicated in September, with officials suggesting they may not cut rates until 2024, even as softer October inflation, bad housing data, and weaker business confidence had led markets to anticipate rate cuts from H2 2023 [[sources/pdf-87087198976b]]; per Fidelity International's late-2022 Investment Outlook 2023, the Fed appears set on raising rates significantly beyond neutral with no pivot expected until meaningful deterioration in hard data, raising the risk that overtightening turns a "standard" recession into an "inflation bust" given that the financial system cannot tolerate positive real rates for any material length of time [[sources/pdf-90e7f9350657]]; per Morgan Stanley's December 12, 2022 "2023 Investment Outlook," Jim Caron frames 2023 as a year of "long and variable lags" with the Fed planning to hold policy rates around 5% for an extended period — even if the economy slips into a mild recession — while Eric Stein argues a Fed "pivot trade" was already starting to get priced in by December 2022 even though the pivot itself had not yet happened, and would be the major factor for fixed-income markets in 2023 [[sources/pdf-bf1afc73ac8f]]; per Apollo's December 2022 "2023 Economic and Capital Markets Outlook," Chief Economist Torsten Sløk reads the Fed's six 2022 hikes (taking the funds rate from 0.25% to 4.0%) as already showing signs of working, sees "peak rates" in mid-2023 around 5.5%, and argues a Fed "pivot" in 2023 should be bullish for asset prices since inflation is coming down before any deterioration in the labor market — making a soft landing "real" [[sources/pdf-d196e853ebc0]]; and per the BlackRock Investment Institute's 2023 Global Outlook, central banks are "on course to overtighten policy" and are "deliberately causing recessions" to tame inflation driven by production constraints that monetary policy cannot resolve — leaving a brutal trade-off between crushing demand to close the output gap (requiring a deep recession) or living with persistently above-2% inflation [[sources/pdf-won-2022-bii-global-outlook]].

## Key facts

- Pozsar argues the Fed's singular mission as of May 13, 2022 is slaying inflation, having chosen price stability over the competing mandates of full employment and financial stability [[sources/pdf-3d3d42a0717a]].
- Pozsar argues the Fed is now in the business of writing a call option on risk assets — not just stocks but housing and crypto as well — rather than the traditional Fed put [[sources/pdf-3d3d42a0717a]].
- Has two types of volatility-payload buttons it can push: talking tough (a string of rate hikes, a string of 50 bps hikes, and possibly 75 bps if things don't cool down) and shifting from passive QT to active QT, where the Fed controls the amount of duration each dollar of balance sheet shrinkage delivers into the market [[sources/pdf-3d3d42a0717a]].
- Per Pozsar, the verbal-tightening strategy was "working wonderfully" as of mid-May 2022: yields and mortgage rates higher, stocks lower [[sources/pdf-3d3d42a0717a]].
- Wants to see further tightening in financial conditions per Chair Powell's press conference, despite the recent stock market correction and rise in mortgage rates [[sources/pdf-3d3d42a0717a]].
- Pozsar argues the Fed leads the market in this dance — it cannot follow it by definition — and that rallies could beget more forceful pushback from the Fed [[sources/pdf-3d3d42a0717a]].
- Pozsar argues the Fed will not be deterred by extreme volatility and lack of liquidity in markets but will be emboldened by it, since volatility is "napalm for risk assets" in service of the inflation-slaying mission [[sources/pdf-3d3d42a0717a]].
- Cuts may have to wait until the Fed is certain that inflation is surely dead — signs of a recession might not mean immediate rate cuts to ramp demand back up [[sources/pdf-3d3d42a0717a]].
- In Pozsar's August 1, 2022 dispatch, the Fed is described as having gone from waging a war against deflationary impulses coming from the globalization of cheap resources (labor, goods, and commodities) to "cleaning up" the inflationary impulses coming from a complex economic war [[sources/pdf-6ae97b9c27c7]].
- Pozsar describes Powell's effort as a "general mobilization" of Paul Volcker's legacy — Powell will either succeed in establishing his credibility as an inflation fighter or cause long-term damage to the Fed's credibility [[sources/pdf-6ae97b9c27c7]].
- Whether Jerome Powell will be remembered by economic historians "as Paul Volcker or Arthur Burns depends on the course of the economic war" [[sources/pdf-6ae97b9c27c7]].
- Per Pozsar, in a war-economy frame Western central banks will not have any good options to slay inflation: they can reduce demand by raising rates, but if supply curves shift inward faster than demand curves the Fed's rate tool is structurally limited [[sources/pdf-6ae97b9c27c7]].
- Pozsar describes the U.S. as the only large economy not engaged in "general mobilization" — instead using diplomacy to extract more for less (more OPEC production, Russian production for less, Chinese goods without tariffs) — leaving inflation as the Fed's singular focus du jour [[sources/pdf-6ae97b9c27c7]].
- Per the July 13, 2022 Q3-2022 IACFM presentation, a stagflationary environment is fundamentally different from a typical recession and therefore requires a different monetary policy response — restrictive policy to reduce inflation rather than easing to stimulate demand [[sources/pdf-853a7755a470]].
- The IACFM presentation argues prematurely easing monetary policy in a stagflationary environment when real GDP growth slows with inflation remaining high "has been a serious policy mistake," citing the Burns era as the precedent for unanchored inflation expectations and lost inflation-fighting credibility [[sources/pdf-853a7755a470]].
- The IACFM presentation reads PCE inflation as having been formally adopted as the Federal Reserve's target inflation measure only at the January 2012 FOMC meeting; prior to 2012, both PCE and CPI inflation were referenced by FOMC participants in their discussion of the Federal Reserve's price stability mandate [[sources/pdf-853a7755a470]].
- Christopher Waller, a member of the Federal Reserve Board of Governors, said at a Virtual NABE Event on July 7, 2022 (quoted in the IACFM presentation): "I look a lot at CPI and I know PCE headline is our number but I like both of them. The CPI is more heavily weighted towards shelter ... that's more consistent with what lower and moderate income groups face" — a posture the IACFM materials use to argue the Fed should monitor CPI alongside PCE [[sources/pdf-853a7755a470]].
- Per ING's December 2022 outlook, the Fed signaled a step down to 50 bp incremental rate hikes alongside a higher ultimate rate than indicated back in September 2022 [[sources/pdf-87087198976b]].
- Per ING, Fed officials suggested they may not cut rates until 2024 given concern about stickiness in key service-sector components of inflation [[sources/pdf-87087198976b]].
- ING's James Knightley reads the late-2022 hawkish rhetoric as the result of concern that recent steep falls in Treasury yields and the dollar, coupled with narrowing credit spreads, were loosening financial conditions — the exact opposite of what the Fed wanted to see [[sources/pdf-87087198976b]].
- ING argues Fed forward guidance "needs to be taken with huge handfuls of salt given their recent track record" [[sources/pdf-87087198976b]].
- ING's base case in December 2022 is that softer October core inflation, bad housing-market data, and weaker business confidence have led markets to anticipate Fed rate cuts from H2 2023 — "in line with our long-held view" [[sources/pdf-87087198976b]].
- Per Fidelity International's late-2022 Investment Outlook 2023, the Fed appears set on raising rates significantly beyond neutral levels to bring inflation under control [[sources/pdf-90e7f9350657]].
- Per Fidelity's Salman Ahmed, a Fed pivot is not expected until there is a meaningful deterioration in hard data — especially inflation and the labour market [[sources/pdf-90e7f9350657]].
- Per Fidelity, the Fed is too focused on backward-looking data, especially the labour market — by the time labour-market weakness shows up it may already be too late for the US economy [[sources/pdf-90e7f9350657]].
- Per Fidelity's Salman Ahmed, the financial system cannot take positive real rates for any material length of time given high levels of debt before financial stability becomes an issue, with real rates already positive and parts of the yield curve pushing toward pre-GFC levels [[sources/pdf-90e7f9350657]].
- Per Fidelity, if the Fed stays true to its current word and doesn't stop until inflation is back near 2 per cent, a "standard" recession could turn into something worse — what Fidelity's Andrew McCaffery calls an "inflation bust" [[sources/pdf-90e7f9350657]].
- Per Fidelity's Andrew McCaffery, the previous norm of central-bank "whatever it takes" intervention during the financial crisis and the pandemic "is going or has gone" [[sources/pdf-90e7f9350657]].
- Per Fidelity's Henk-Jan Rikkerink, signs that hard US data is moderating could be a harbinger of the much-feted Fed pivot; when it arrives, it should boost risky assets such as equities and credit, as well as government bonds [[sources/pdf-90e7f9350657]].
- Per Jim Caron in Morgan Stanley's December 12, 2022 "2023 Investment Outlook," monetary policy will continue to be the key driver of asset prices in 2023, with "long and variable lags" between a monetary policy action and its impact on the economy [[sources/pdf-bf1afc73ac8f]].
- Per Caron, the Fed's mission is to help lower inflation to its 2% to 2.5% target and keep it there, durably [[sources/pdf-bf1afc73ac8f]].
- Per Caron, the Fed has indicated a plan to increase policy rates to around 5% and hold them there for an extended period, even if the economy slips into a mild recession [[sources/pdf-bf1afc73ac8f]].
- Per Caron, with peak inflation likely behind us, high-quality bonds may benefit while riskier assets and lower-quality credit may suffer [[sources/pdf-bf1afc73ac8f]].
- Per Eric Stein in the same outlook, the credit markets started to price in a Fed "pivot" by December 2022 — even though the Fed pivot clearly hasn't happened yet — with the market acting as a forward-looking mechanism and investors now expecting rate increases at a slower pace than most of the second half of 2022 [[sources/pdf-bf1afc73ac8f]].
- Per Stein, the Fed pivot trade will be the major factor in considering fixed-income opportunities in 2023 [[sources/pdf-bf1afc73ac8f]].
- Per Apollo's December 2022 "2023 Economic and Capital Markets Outlook," through November 2022 the FOMC had raised rates six times in 2022, taking the federal funds rate target from 0.25% to 4.0% [[sources/pdf-d196e853ebc0]].
- Per Apollo, as of the paper's writing, the Fed was expected to reach "peak rates" in mid-2023 around 5.5%, with the exact timing of a less aggressive Fed — or a Fed "pivot" — uncertain [[sources/pdf-d196e853ebc0]].
- Per Apollo, the Fed continued to step on the brakes through year-end 2022 because four-out-of-every-five components of the inflation basket were still showing price growth of 5% or more, and "sticky" components of the CPI were continuing to rise [[sources/pdf-d196e853ebc0]].
- Per Apollo, the Fed's 2022 rate hikes were "already showing signs of working," with various inflation measures moderating near year-end — and the fact that inflation is coming down before any deterioration in the labor market is "very important for markets and for the economic outlook" [[sources/pdf-d196e853ebc0]].
- Per Apollo, with less pressure on the Fed to forcefully fight inflation going forward, the likelihood of a soft landing in 2023 "is real" [[sources/pdf-d196e853ebc0]].
- Per Apollo, a less aggressive Fed — or a potential Fed "pivot" in 2023 — should be bullish for asset prices (public and private) ranging from rates to credit to equities [[sources/pdf-d196e853ebc0]].
- Per BII's 2023 Global Outlook, central banks are "on course to overtighten policy as they seek to tame inflation" — a recession is "foretold" [[sources/pdf-won-2022-bii-global-outlook]].
- Per BII, central bankers "won't ride to the rescue when growth slows in this new regime, contrary to what investors have come to expect" — they are deliberately causing recessions by overtightening policy [[sources/pdf-won-2022-bii-global-outlook]].
- Per BII, central bank policy rates are not the tool to resolve production constraints; they can only influence demand — leaving a brutal trade-off between engineering a deep recession or accepting above-target inflation [[sources/pdf-won-2022-bii-global-outlook]].
- Per BII, central banks will eventually back off from rate hikes as economic damage becomes reality, but inflation will cool and stay persistently higher than the 2% target [[sources/pdf-won-2022-bii-global-outlook]].
- Per BII, the damage from overtightening is most evident in rate-sensitive sectors: surging mortgage rates have cratered U.S. new-home sales, with the 2022 decline steeper than past mega Fed hiking cycles including the 1970s, early 1980s, and mid-2000s housing boom unwind [[sources/pdf-won-2022-bii-global-outlook]].
