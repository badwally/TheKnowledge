# Daron Acemoglu

## Summary

Economist at the Department of Economics, Massachusetts Institute of Technology and NBER affiliate; first-listed author of the 2022 NBER working paper "Eclipse of Rent-Sharing: The Effects of Managers' Business Education on Wages and the Labor Share in the US and Denmark," which argues that the rise of CEOs with business-school degrees is a major contributing factor to the decline in the labor share and the slowdown of wage growth in the US and Denmark [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]]; also author of the May 2024 paper "The Simple Macroeconomics of AI" (prepared for Economic Policy), which uses a task-based framework and Hulten's theorem to argue that AI's macro effects over the next 10 years will be modest — no more than 0.53–0.66% TFP growth and 0.93–1.56% GDP growth — far below the large forecasts of Goldman Sachs or McKinsey [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].

## Key facts

- Affiliated with the Department of Economics, E52-446, Massachusetts Institute of Technology, 77 Massachusetts Avenue, Cambridge, MA 02139, and with NBER on the paper [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].
- Contact email daron@mit.edu on the paper [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].
- First-listed author of "Eclipse of Rent-Sharing" (NBER Working Paper No. 29874, March 2022) co-authored with Alex He and Daniel le Maire, with JEL codes G30, J30, J31, J53, M52 [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].
- The paper's headline result: when a CEO with a business degree takes over from a non-business manager, wages decline by 6% and the labor share by 5 percentage points in the US within five years; the Danish results are 3% wages and 3 percentage points labor share [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].
- Argues that changing managerial attitudes and practices toward rent-sharing have been a major contributing factor to the decline in the labor share and the slowdown of wage growth, alongside other proposed drivers like capital accumulation, automation, the rise of superstar firms, growing concentration, and declining union power [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].
- Co-authored Acemoglu et al. (2019) on potentially exogenous variation in country-level democracy based on regional democratization waves — the diffusion-IV strategy that the 2022 paper adapts to identify causal effects of business managers within peer groups [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].
- Also cited in the paper for prior automation work — Acemoglu and Restrepo (2019) and Acemoglu et al. (2020) — that is one of the proposed alternative drivers of the labor-share decline the paper engages with [[sources/pdf-4dbe002c1c7a]].
- Author of "The Simple Macroeconomics of AI" (May 12, 2024), prepared for Economic Policy and presented at the MIT Solow Memorial conference [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- Builds on the task-based framework from Acemoglu and Restrepo (2018, 2019b, 2022), which decomposes AI's effects into four channels: extensive-margin automation, task complementarity, deepening of automation, and new task creation [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- Applies Hulten's theorem to discipline AI macro forecasts: TFP gains equal the GDP share of impacted tasks multiplied by average task-level cost savings [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- Estimates that 20% of US labor tasks are exposed to AI (using Eloundou et al. 2023 data weighted by wage bill share), of which only 23% can be profitably automated within 10 years (per Svanberg et al. 2024), yielding 4.6% of GDP exposed [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- Baseline TFP estimate: 0.66% over 10 years (0.064% per annum); incorporating hard-to-learn tasks, the upper bound drops to 0.53% [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- GDP estimates: 0.93–1.16% over 10 years assuming proportional capital stock growth; up to 1.4–1.56% if a large investment boom materializes [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- Introduces the easy-to-learn vs. hard-to-learn task distinction: easy tasks have reliable observable outcome metrics and a simple action-to-outcome mapping; hard tasks lack both, limiting AI learning to average human performance [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- Classifies 72.6% of wage-bill-weighted exposed tasks as easy-to-learn using a four-step procedure: verb classification, IWA classification, LDA topic modeling, and gradient-boosted tree prediction [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- Argues that even when AI improves the productivity of low-skill workers in certain tasks, this may increase rather than reduce inequality through ripple effects — proving this by example in a task-based model with comparative advantage [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- Finds AI exposure is more equally distributed across demographic groups than pre-AI automation (robotics, software), so wage inequality effects are less adverse — but there is no evidence AI will reduce inequality [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- Predicts AI will widen the gap between capital and labor income, with the capital share of national income increasing by about 0.31 percentage points [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- Estimates low-education women, especially white native-born low-education women, are likely to experience real wage declines from AI [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- Uses estimates from three experimental studies — Peng et al. (2023) at 55.8% faster task completion with GitHub Copilot, Noy and Zhang (2023) at 40% faster with ChatGPT-3.5, and Brynjolfsson et al. (2023) at 14% faster in customer service — to derive a baseline 27% average labor cost saving for easy tasks [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- Warns that new AI-generated tasks with negative social value (deepfakes, addictive social media, malicious IT attacks) could make GDP gains illusory: AI may appear to increase GDP by 2% while reducing welfare by −0.72% [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
- Credits David Autor, Simon Johnson, and Pascual Restrepo as key collaborators, and thanks Pamela Mishkin and Daniel Rock for sharing AI exposure data [[sources/pdf-fb3e28ab676a]].
