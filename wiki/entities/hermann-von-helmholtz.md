# Hermann von Helmholtz

## Summary

Hermann von Helmholtz (cited via Helmholtz 1860) is the 19th-century scientist whose conception of perception as a process of probabilistic, knowledge-driven inference grounds modern predictive-processing accounts of the brain [[sources/pdf-5f41a1d2e45f]]. The "Helmholtzian" insight is that sensory systems are in the tricky business of inferring sensory causes from their bodily effects, computing multiple probability distributions because any single such effect will be consistent with many different sets of causes distinguished only by their relative, context-dependent probability of occurrence [[sources/pdf-5f41a1d2e45f]].

## Key facts

- Helmholtz's view informed mid-20th-century work by MacKay (1956), Neisser (1967), and Gregory (1980), as part of the cognitive-psychological tradition that became known as "analysis-by-synthesis" [[sources/pdf-5f41a1d2e45f]].
- Helmholtz's name is also attached to the "Helmholtz Machine" of Dayan, Hinton, and Zemel (1995), a connectionist architecture that learns multilevel representations by using its own top-down connections to self-supervise its recognition model [[sources/pdf-5f41a1d2e45f]].
