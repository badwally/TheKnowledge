# Joel Greenblatt

## Summary

Value investor, founder of Gotham Capital, and adjunct professor at Columbia Business School who taught the Special Situation Investing class from at least 2002 through 2006; the class notes captured by an auditor are the source under review and present Greenblatt's worldview as a simple, repeatable application of "figure out what businesses are worth, then pay a lot less for them," framed against Mr. Market's volatility and verified by a long-term track record of roughly 40% per year over 20 years at Gotham [[sources/pdf-d0b9b7e97b5c]].

## Key facts

- Taught the Special Situation Investing classes at Columbia University Business School, with the class notes covering 2002 through 2006 [[sources/pdf-d0b9b7e97b5c]].
- Began his first class on September 7, 2005 by framing his goal as "to teach you the course that I never had and that I wish I had" [[sources/pdf-d0b9b7e97b5c]].
- Started in business school 25 years before the 2005 class — i.e., around 1980 — and learned investing on his own through reading and making mistakes [[sources/pdf-d0b9b7e97b5c]].
- Founded and runs Gotham Capital, which has averaged 40% per year for 20 years; per his class, $1,000 invested at inception would have grown to $836,683 [[sources/pdf-d0b9b7e97b5c]].
- Defines his job to his 11-year-old son via a story about a boy named Jason selling gum, concluding: "I sit around trying to figure out what businesses are worth, and then I try to pay a lot less for them" [[sources/pdf-d0b9b7e97b5c]].
- Argues the social value of investing in the stock market is similar to being good at handicapping horses — markets are useful for raising capital but "they just really don't need you" — and asks students who succeed to find some way to give back [[sources/pdf-d0b9b7e97b5c]].
- Argues prices fluctuate far more than underlying business values, and that the gap between the two is the central source of opportunity — without claiming to know or care why this happens, only to take advantage of it [[sources/pdf-d0b9b7e97b5c]].
- Guarantees that "if you do good valuation work and you are right, Mr. Market will pay you back" — the market is inefficient over one to two years but eventually gets it right over two to three [[sources/pdf-d0b9b7e97b5c]].
- Frames his edge as context, not analytical horsepower: "we are not experts in any particular industry, we are not smarter than anybody else, and we are not doing better analysis. The fact that you are here means you can do the analysis" [[sources/pdf-d0b9b7e97b5c]].
- Was down 5% in 1998-1999 worried about a bubble, kept doing what he was doing, and was up 130% in 2000 when the markets came back [[sources/pdf-d0b9b7e97b5c]].
- Won a proxy fight (his first and last foray into activist investing) and concluded the activist path was "not worth the pain" [[sources/pdf-d0b9b7e97b5c]].
- Authored the book "How You Can Be a Stock Market Genius," written for the general public but, in his own assessment, more at an MBA level [[sources/pdf-d0b9b7e97b5c]].
- Co-founded the Value Investors Club, an invitation-only online community for sharing investment write-ups that he calls "a great learning tool" and "a great research archive to build an investment thesis" [[sources/pdf-d0b9b7e97b5c]].
- Is one of only four original investors remaining in Richard Pzena's $14 billion fund, which lost 70% of its investors during the 1995-2000 value drawdown [[sources/pdf-d0b9b7e97b5c]].
