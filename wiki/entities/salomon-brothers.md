# Salomon Brothers

## Summary

Major Wall Street investment bank whose proprietary trading, mortgage innovation, and convertible/equity derivatives businesses made it a recurring participant in every major deleveraging episode from 1987 through 2000; per Andrew Constan's firsthand account, Salomon lost money in equity businesses on Black Monday but the massive fixed-income rally offset the pain, led mortgage market innovation in the late 1980s and early 1990s alongside Goldman Sachs, bid competitively for Drexel Burnham's inventory after the 1990 bankruptcy, housed an arbitrage group that was the firm's "crown jewel" for a decade, and was ultimately acquired by Sandy Weill and merged with Citibank to form Citigroup [[sources/pdf-f139d7bd1924]].

## Key facts

- On Black Monday (October 19, 1987), Salomon and others lost money in equity businesses but the massive rally in fixed income offset much of the pain; banks were willing and able to provide credit to the real economy and to financial asset owners [[sources/pdf-f139d7bd1924]].
- After the Drexel Burnham bankruptcy in February 1990, Salomon and a couple of other desks were asked by Drexel traders to bid competitively for Drexel's inventory [[sources/pdf-f139d7bd1924]].
- Led massive changes in the mortgage market in the late 1980s and early 1990s alongside Goldman Sachs, slicing mortgages into interest-only (IO) and principal-only (PO) strips and developing increasingly sophisticated mortgage derivatives [[sources/pdf-f139d7bd1924]].
- Had a proprietary arbitrage group run by Rob Stavis that was the "crown jewel of Salomon's business for a decade" [[sources/pdf-f139d7bd1924]].
- Sandy Weill bought the firm and merged it with Citibank to form Citigroup; Weill had "no interest at all in risk taking on markets" [[sources/pdf-f139d7bd1924]].
- Weill closed the arbitrage group in July 1998, liquidating its positions into a weak market; losses in the arb group were "likely at least $1 billion" [[sources/pdf-f139d7bd1924]].
- The liquidation of Salomon's arb positions into a weak market contributed to deteriorating conditions leading up to the LTCM crisis in the summer of 1998 [[sources/pdf-f139d7bd1924]].
- Long-Term Capital Management was founded by "a dozen or so ex colleagues from Salomon Brothers" [[sources/pdf-f139d7bd1924]].
- During the 1998 LTCM crisis, every silo at Salomon and across most of Wall Street had some exposure to LTCM [[sources/pdf-f139d7bd1924]].
- Andrew Constan progressed through the firm from corporate finance analyst (1986) to convertible bond trader (1989–1991) to manager of the U.S. convertible trading business (1994) to head of equity derivatives (1998) before leaving to start his own hedge fund [[sources/pdf-f139d7bd1924]].
