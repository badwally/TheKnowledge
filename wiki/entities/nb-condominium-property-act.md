# New Brunswick Condominium Property Act c16.05

## Summary

The New Brunswick Condominium Property Act c16.05 is the province's primary modern condominium statute, having come into effect on January 1, 2010 and replacing the prior Condominium Property Act c-16 (1969) [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]]. The new Act introduced mandatory reserve fund accounts, mandatory reserve fund studies for corporations with more than 10 units, better financial reporting requirements, a 10-day buyer cooling-off period, and a framework administered by the Director of Condominiums — features explicitly intended to bring NB condominium development in line with other Canadian jurisdictions [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]]. Properties under construction before January 1, 2010 that had been issued a building permit are grandfathered under the c-16 (1969) regime [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]]. The Act is the load-bearing regulatory anchor for any New Brunswick expansion arm of the condo-capital-infra Canada-first GTM sequence under ADR-0004 and sits alongside the Nova Scotia Condominium Act and the Newfoundland & Labrador Condominium Act, 2009 + NLR 80/11 in the Atlantic-Canada statutory cluster [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].

## Key facts

### Statutory identification and predecessor regime

- Codified as the *Condominium Property Act* c16.05 [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- Came into effect on January 1, 2010, replacing the prior *Condominium Property Act* c-16 (1969) [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- The 1969 predecessor Act remained in effect until December 31, 2009 [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- Properties under construction before January 1, 2010 that had been issued a building permit are grandfathered under the c-16 (1969) regime [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- The Act is administered by the Director of Condominiums [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- The new Act was framed by Service New Brunswick as bringing NB condominium development more in line with other Canadian jurisdictions [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].

### Material changes from the predecessor regime

- Increased transparency requirements for condominium corporations [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- Mandatory reserve fund accounts (new — absent under c-16 (1969)) [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- Better financial reporting requirements [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- Requirement for reserve fund studies for larger corporations (more than 10 units) [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- A 10-day cooling-off period for buyers to evaluate the documents and rules governing the condominium project they are about to become part of before being bound to a purchase [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].

### Condominium Corporation governance

- A Condominium Corporation manages the condominium's property and business affairs and consists of the owners of all units in the complex [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- Corporation duties include: setting up a Board of Directors; managing and maintaining the common areas; preparing the annual budget; collecting condominium fees for common expenses and directing a percentage into the reserve fund; setting up a reserve fund and ensuring all owners contribute appropriately; adequately insuring the corporation's common property and liability; holding meetings of owners; and ensuring owners comply with the Act, the declaration, rules, and by-laws [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- All owners of condominium units are members of the Condominium Corporation [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].

### Board of Directors composition

- The Board of Directors is made up of volunteer unit owners elected by members of the Condominium Corporation [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- The Board must consist of at least 3 people for corporations with 4 or more units [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- The Board must consist of at least 2 people for corporations with 2-3 units [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- The Board acts on behalf of the Condominium Corporation and is responsible for ensuring the requirements of the declaration and by-laws are met as well as overseeing management of the Corporation [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- The Board reports to the Condominium Corporation at an annual general meeting or extra-ordinary general meeting [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- When a Condominium is first created, a temporary board of directors is appointed by the Declarant; as units are sold to new owners, they eventually elect a permanent board of directors [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].

### Voting rules

- Owners have the right and obligation to vote on matters presented at any general meeting, as well as on changes to common property, regulations, and by-laws [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- At most general meetings, votes are conducted by show of hands; voting may also be done by proxy [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- If more than one person owns a unit, their vote counts as one (the by-laws clarify who has the right to vote in that case) [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- At least 60% of owners must vote in favour to change the by-laws [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- Any by-law amendments must be registered in the Land Title Office to take effect [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- All owners and everyone occupying the units must abide by the by-laws of their Condominium Corporation; in some special cases, by-laws may allow the Corporation to fine its owners for non-compliance [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].

### Reserve fund regime (load-bearing for condo-capital-infra)

- All condominiums are required to have a reserve fund as of January 1, 2010 [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- Condominium Corporations existing before January 1, 2010 have five years to build a reserve fund [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- The reserve fund is used to pay for major repairs and replacement of common property, such as replacing the roof or painting the outside of the building [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- Regulated such that the reserve fund can only pay for major expenses — not for emergencies or unexpected expenses, which must be handled by a separately-established contingency fund [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- All owners must contribute appropriately to the reserve fund [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- Typically, a portion of each owner's condominium fees is directed into the reserve fund on a monthly basis; some Corporations may instead contribute by special assessment on a quarterly or semi-annual basis [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- Condominium Corporations may not mortgage the common elements to raise money for major repairs and replacements; reserve fund obligations are funded usually through reserve fund fees and special assessments only [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].

### Two-tier reserve fund structure (unique to NB within the comparator set)

- For small complexes with 10 units or less, the reserve fund must be equal to the annual budget of the corporation [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- For large Condominium Corporations with 11 units or more, a reserve fund must maintain at least the minimum amount recommended by the reserve fund study for anticipated major repairs and replacements [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- The unit-count-keyed two-tier structure has no analog in BC SPA's uniform 5-strata-lot threshold for triggering the depreciation report requirement, in California Davis-Stirling § 5550's gross-budget-half threshold, or in NL NLR 80/11's prescribed-contents framework, which applies the same physical-and-financial-analysis structure to all reserve-fund-study-eligible corporations regardless of size [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].

### Reserve fund study requirements

- A reserve fund study is an analysis of anticipated major repairs and replacements that the condominium complex will need to undergo in the next 30 years, along with an estimated budget that indicates the needed contributions [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- The qualified person who completes the reserve fund study must prepare a report on the common property including: (i) information on what may need to be repaired or replaced within the next 30 years; (ii) an assessment of the current condition of the common property; (iii) estimates for costs of repair or replacement; and (iv) a recommendation on the amount of money that should be in the reserve fund [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- Corporations with more than 10 units must complete a reserve fund study every 10 years [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- Reserve fund studies must be updated every 3 years [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- Corporations existing before January 1, 2010 had five years to complete the initial reserve fund study [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- As of January 1, 2010, the Declarant must complete the reserve fund study before a project will be approved [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- Reserve fund studies are framed by Service NB as a road map detailing an estimate of future major expenses needed to maintain the common property; should the reserve fund prove inadequate to cover a major repair or replacement, owners may be asked to pay an additional fee through a special assessment, which could be substantial [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].

### Qualified-person requirements (preparer eligibility)

- Only a "qualified" person identified in the Regulations may conduct a reserve fund study; the Service NB FAQ characterizes the qualified set as "usually an engineer or an appraiser" [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- A qualified person must be knowledgeable about: (i) depreciating property; (ii) the operation and maintenance of depreciating property; and (iii) the costs of replacements or repairs to depreciating property [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- The Board or Declarant is responsible for hiring a professional to complete the reserve fund study [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].

### Funding the reserve fund study

- In a new project, the Declarant bears the cost of the reserve fund study (a requirement for project approval) [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- In the case of Corporations existing after the coming of the Act, the owners pay for the reserve fund study, and it qualifies to be paid for from the reserve fund [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].

### Contingency fund (separate from reserve fund)

- A Corporation may set up a contingency fund to be used for purposes specified in the by-laws [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- The contingency fund usually serves to cover unexpected expenses such as emergency repairs not planned for in the operating budget, or to cover shortfalls in the operating budget at the end of the year, if necessary [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- If unforeseen events occur (e.g., dramatic increase in heating costs, need for a major repair) creating a shortfall in the operating budget or reserve fund, each owner may be asked to contribute additional fees through a special assessment charge or increased condominium fees for a set period [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].

### Rights of entry to units

- The Corporation and its agent have the right to enter a unit anytime in cases of emergencies [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- For non-emergency entry, the Corporation may enter between 8 a.m. and 8 p.m. Monday to Friday upon giving 48-hours notice to the owner [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].

### Purchase types and pre-sale disclosure

- The Act distinguishes purchases from a developer (called a Declarant under the Act) — in three sub-types: new sale (unit is part of a new building), phased development (unit is part of a phased development project), and conversion (unit is part of a converted building such as an older apartment building or townhouse complex) — from re-sale (purchase from an existing owner) [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- Before a buyer buys from a Declarant on a pre-sale contract, the Declarant must provide a copy of the agreement of sale and purchase, which in the case of a new sale includes the Condominium plan (or proposed plan), the declaration (or proposed declaration), and the by-laws (or proposed by-laws) [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- In the case of a phased development, the Declarant must provide a disclosure statement about how the project will take place, a statement that the Declarant is not required to continue the project after this phase, an estimated timeline of the phases if applicable, a statement describing the type of buildings and the number of proposed units if applicable, a statement of the proportions of the common interest and common expenses attributable to the units after each phase, and a list of the facilities and services the owners will share [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].

### Cross-jurisdictional comparator framing

- NB's 10-year primary reserve-fund-study cadence with 3-year updates is longer than the BC SPA's 5-year cycle and longer than California Davis-Stirling § 5550's triennial visual-inspection cadence — making NB the cluster's longest-base-cycle jurisdiction among the comparator set [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- NB's unit-count-keyed two-tier reserve fund structure (10 units or less = reserve fund equal to annual budget; 11+ units = full reserve fund study) has no analog in BC SPA's uniform 5-strata-lot threshold, California Davis-Stirling § 5550's gross-budget-half threshold, or NL NLR 80/11's uniform component-inventory-and-financial-analysis prescribed-contents framework [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- NB's "usually an engineer or an appraiser" qualified-preparer description is more permissive than BC SPA's six explicit professional categories but overlaps with two of the four explicit professional categories in NL NLR 80/11 (engineer; AACI member); the binding NB Regulations may name additional categories not surfaced in the Service NB FAQ [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- NB's 30-year forward-forecast horizon for the reserve fund study matches NL NLR 80/11's minimum 30-year recommended funding plan and BC SPA's 30-year financial-forecast horizon [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- NB's prohibition on mortgaging common elements to raise money for major repairs and replacements is a debt-instrument restriction that contrasts with Florida CS/CS/HB 913 (2025)'s explicit authorization of secured-line-of-credit and line-of-credit alternative funding mechanisms — making NB structurally more reliant on accumulated reserve contributions and special assessments than the post-2025 FL regime [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].

### Implication for condo-capital-infra GTM

- The Act is the regulatory floor for any New Brunswick expansion arm of the ADR-0004 Canada-first GTM sequence; a NB reserve fund study engagement must satisfy the 30-year forward-forecast requirement, the 11+ unit threshold for full study requirement, the qualified-preparer regulatory specification, the 10-year primary cadence with 3-year updates, the Declarant-vs-owner funding-source split for new versus existing corporations, and the mortgage-of-common-elements prohibition that constrains corporation-level financing options when reserves are short [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
- The two-tier structure (10-or-less units bound to annual-budget-equivalent reserve; 11+ units bound to study-recommended minimum) materially shapes the NB addressable market: only 11+ unit corporations are reserve-fund-study customers, while 10-or-less unit corporations represent annual-budget-equivalent reserve targets without the study product attached [[sources/web-2017-07-26-1f3]].
