# Salman Ahmed

## Summary

Global Head of Macro and Strategic Asset Allocation at Fidelity International; author of the Macro chapter of the firm's Investment Outlook 2023, where he argues that the financial system cannot tolerate positive real rates for any material length of time and that the Fed's continued tightening risks turning a "standard" recession into something worse [[sources/pdf-90e7f9350657]].

## Key facts

- Listed as Global Head of Macro and Strategic Asset Allocation at Fidelity International on the Investment Outlook 2023 Macro chapter [[sources/pdf-90e7f9350657]].
- Frames the Macro chapter as "What happens next depends on the Fed" [[sources/pdf-90e7f9350657]].
- Cites Fidelity's proprietary activity trackers as indicating a continuing slowdown through Q4 2022, with a recession likely in the US and near certain in Europe and the UK [[sources/pdf-90e7f9350657]].
- Argues the Fed appears set on raising rates significantly beyond neutral and that a pivot won't come until there is meaningful deterioration in hard data — especially inflation and the labour market [[sources/pdf-90e7f9350657]].
- Identifies the US housing market as already showing signs of stress, with higher mortgage rates and reduced affordability stifling transactions [[sources/pdf-90e7f9350657]].
- Argues the Fed is too focused on backward-looking data, especially the labour market — by the time labour-market weakness shows up it may already be too late for the US economy [[sources/pdf-90e7f9350657]].
- Argues real rates have been positive for some time and parts of the yield curve are pushing toward pre-GFC levels [[sources/pdf-90e7f9350657]].
- States Fidelity has "repeatedly argued that the financial system cannot take positive real rates for any material length of time (due to high levels of debt) before financial stability becomes an issue" [[sources/pdf-90e7f9350657]].
- Argues that if the Fed stays true to its current word and doesn't stop until inflation is back near 2 per cent, a "standard" recession could turn into something worse [[sources/pdf-90e7f9350657]].
- Treats the UK as a "canary in the coal mine" — the September 2022 UK fiscal-policy episode demonstrates that combinations of monetary tightening and mis-judged fiscal decisions have the potential to turn into financial-stability risks [[sources/pdf-90e7f9350657]].
- Argues Europe's 2023 will be decided by the direction of energy prices, the nature of fiscal support to consumers, and the weather — a warmer winter (early indications from ECMWF and the UK Met Office support this) reduces the chance of gas rationing or blackouts [[sources/pdf-90e7f9350657]].
- Identifies decarbonisation, deglobalisation, and the process of dealing with high debt levels as structural trends likely to keep inflationary pressure elevated over the coming years [[sources/pdf-90e7f9350657]].
- Reads the post-20th Communist Party Congress signals as tentatively pointing to relaxed anti-Covid measures (though slower than expected) and continued loose monetary and fiscal policy in China [[sources/pdf-90e7f9350657]].
- Believes the People's Bank of China is willing to accept some renminbi depreciation to support export growth, especially given relatively low Chinese inflation [[sources/pdf-90e7f9350657]].
- Concludes that "markets rarely follow economics in straight lines" and the appearance of value across asset classes — especially in fixed income and parts of equities — will be an important trend to assess alongside macro developments [[sources/pdf-90e7f9350657]].
