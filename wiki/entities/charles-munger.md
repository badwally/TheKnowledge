# Charles T. Munger

## Summary

Vice chairman of Berkshire Hathaway and longtime intellectual partner of Warren Buffett; author of "The Psychology of Human Misjudgment," a revised talk originally written for *Poor Charlie's Almanack* in which he enumerates twenty-five standard causes of human misjudgment — a self-assembled checklist synthesized from three psychology textbooks, Robert Cialdini's *Influence*, and decades of real-world observation, intended as a practical tool for avoiding cognitive errors in business and investing [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].

## Key facts

- Educated at Caltech and Harvard Law School, neither of which required any coursework in psychology — a gap he calls a form of institutional pride in avoiding "fuzzy" subjects [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- Developed his own system of psychology "more or less in the self-help style of Ben Franklin" after finding that his generalized, evolution-based theory structure was inadequate to cope with the extreme irrationality he encountered in law practice and investing [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- Credits two turns of mind as central to his method: (1) inversion, following the algebraist Jacobi's counsel "Invert, always invert" — seeking good judgment by collecting instances of bad judgment, and (2) ignoring boundaries between professional territories when collecting instances of stupidity [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- Identifies Robert Cialdini's *Influence* as the catalyst that pushed him to skim three introductory psychology textbooks; gave Cialdini a share of Berkshire Class A stock in gratitude [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- Revised the talk at age eighty-one in 2005, from memory, unassisted by any research, producing a full-scale rewrite with new material for *Poor Charlie's Almanack*, republished with permission of Peter Kaufman [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- Enumerates twenty-five psychological tendencies that, while generally useful, often mislead — ranging from Reward and Punishment Superresponse Tendency (#1) through Lollapalooza Tendency (#25) [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- Places incentive-caused bias first in his framework, stating: "Never a year passes but I get some surprise that pushes a little further my appreciation of incentive super-power" [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- Emphasizes that psychological tendencies are "both numerous and inseparably intertwined" and that the complex parsing out of effects from intertwined tendencies was usually avoided by psychology textbook writers — a gap his checklist is designed to fill [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- Advocates a checklist-based approach to psychological tendencies, arguing that any other mode of operation will cause a thinking person to miss much that is important [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- Recounts a personal investing error caused by Deprival-Superreaction Tendency: he bought 300 shares of Belridge Oil at $115/share but declined 1,500 more at the same price because it would have required selling something or borrowing $173,000; Belridge later sold to Shell at ~$3,700/share, costing him approximately $5.4 million [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
- Names Warren Buffett's uncle Fred Buffett as a shared formative influence who "changed me for the better" through the Liking/Loving Tendency's feedback mode between admiration and love [[sources/pdf-munger-2021-revised-psychology-of]].
