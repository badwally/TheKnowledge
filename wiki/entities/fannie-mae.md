# Fannie Mae

## Summary

Fannie Mae is one of the two US government-sponsored enterprises (GSEs) whose project standards and property insurance requirements determine whether condominium unit loans qualify for the conventional secondary mortgage market. On March 18, 2026, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac jointly issued a significant update to project standards and property insurance requirements designed to simplify processes, respond to insurance market challenges, and strengthen the financial health of condominium communities; Fannie Mae's changes are outlined in Lender Letter LL-2026-03 [[sources/web-2026-03-18-a4e]]. The March 2026 update is characterized by industry commentators as the most significant round of condominium lending standard updates in several years, with some changes taking effect immediately and others rolling out through the rest of 2026 and into early 2027 [[sources/web-2026-04-15-f75]]. Industry coverage in the NYC condo/co-op market has emphasized that the 15% reserve-funding floor and the retirement of Limited Review are likely to drive substantial demand for reserve-study services and to cascade ineligibility designations into adjacent jumbo and commercial lending markets [[sources/web-2026-04-21-260]]. Community-association legal commentary frames Fannie Mae as the primary gatekeeper of US condominium mortgage eligibility, governing approximately 75% of US condominium mortgages, with non-warrantable designations producing 5-30% downward pressure on unit market values [[sources/web-2026-03-24-bb6]].

## Key facts

- Full legal name: Federal National Mortgage Association [[sources/web-2026-04-15-f75]].
- Is a government-sponsored enterprise that purchases mortgages from lenders after those loans are made; the eligibility standards Fannie Mae imposes effectively govern most conventional residential mortgages in the United States [[sources/web-2026-04-15-f75]].
- Characterized by community-association legal commentary as one of three semi-governmental agencies — alongside Freddie Mac and Ginnie Mae — that establish minimum standards for mortgage lending in order to stabilize the housing market and make homeownership affordable in the U.S. [[sources/web-2026-03-24-bb6]].
- Plays a critical role in condominium mortgage eligibility, impacting both buyers and associations; approximately 75% of U.S. condominium mortgages are governed by Fannie Mae standards, making FNMA a primary gatekeeper of mortgage eligibility [[sources/web-2026-03-24-bb6]].
- Mortgage approval depends not only on the buyer, but also on whether the condominium association meets FNMA eligibility guidelines; if an association does not satisfy FNMA guidelines, buyers may be unable to obtain conventional financing for units in that community [[sources/web-2026-03-24-bb6]].
- Approximately 80%-90% of mortgages are sold on the secondary mortgage market shortly after closing; mortgages tied to ineligible associations cannot be sold on the secondary market, reducing lender willingness to issue loans [[sources/web-2026-03-24-bb6]].
- Associations with a negative FNMA status (e.g., "unwarrantable" or "ineligible," collectively aka "blacklisted") may experience a 5% to 30% decrease in market value [[sources/web-2026-03-24-bb6]].
- Evaluates the entire condominium project, not just the borrower; if an association's finances, reserves, or insurance fail Fannie Mae's standards, buyers in that community cannot obtain affordable conventional financing [[sources/web-2026-04-15-f75]].
- A loan on a property meeting Fannie Mae's standards is called "warrantable"; non-warrantable loans typically require larger down payments, higher interest rates, and harder qualification requirements [[sources/web-2026-04-15-f75]].
- Practical implications of FNMA ineligibility per community-association legal commentary include: prospective purchasers far less likely to obtain conventional mortgage financing; low down payment options (5%-10%) largely disappear; buyers often required to put 20%-30% down with higher interest rates; reduced financing options place immediate downward pressure on unit market values; buyer pool shifts from owner-occupants to cash investors or higher-risk borrowers; higher-risk buyer profiles may increase assessment delinquencies; overall market demand declines; pending transactions more likely to fall through mid-contract; units may need to be re-listed with longer time on market; sellers often forced to offer concessions or accept below-market pricing — producing a compounding "domino" effect across the association [[sources/web-2026-03-24-bb6]].
- Began implementing new condominium underwriting standards in late 2021 in response to the June 2021 partial collapse of Champlain Towers South in Surfside, Florida [[sources/web-2026-04-15-f75]].
- Issued Lender Letter LL-2026-03 on March 18, 2026 in coordination with Freddie Mac, updating project standards and property insurance requirements for condominium loans [[sources/web-2026-03-18-a4e]] [[sources/web-2026-04-15-f75]] [[sources/web-2026-03-24-bb6]].
- Stated rationale for the update: simplify certain processes, respond to insurance market challenges, and strengthen the financial health of condominium communities [[sources/web-2026-03-18-a4e]].
- Fannie Mae/Freddie Mac project-review criteria are explicitly characterized by CAI Advocacy as not always aligned with actual financial stability and performance of condominium communities [[sources/web-2026-03-18-a4e]].
- Retired the limited review process for loans with application dates on or after August 3, 2026 [[sources/web-2026-03-18-a4e]].
- The Limited Review retirement applies specifically to established condominium projects with more than 10 units; every loan in those communities must now go through Full Review [[sources/web-2026-04-15-f75]].
- Full Review requires a comprehensive evaluation of the association's budget, reserve funding, insurance, delinquency rates, pending litigation, special assessments, and inspection reports [[sources/web-2026-04-15-f75]].
- Limited review historically represented roughly 40% of all project reviews [[sources/web-2026-03-18-a4e]]; NYC industry coverage characterizes the limited review process as having been used by "at least 40% of loans in the U.S." [[sources/web-2026-04-21-260]].
- Industry commentary characterizes the pre-March-2026 limited-review regime as having allowed "many condominium communities [to skirt] through without reserving at all for future repairs," per Strategic Inspections president and CEO Orest Tomaselli [[sources/web-2026-04-21-260]].
- Expanded eligibility for a waiver of project review to include projects with up to 10 units [[sources/web-2026-03-18-a4e]].
- The previous project-review-waiver threshold was 4 units; the March 2026 update raised it to 10 units [[sources/web-2026-04-15-f75]].
- For projects in the five-to-ten-unit range, the community cannot be part of a master association or larger development in order to qualify for the project-review waiver [[sources/web-2026-04-15-f75]].
- Eliminated the long-standing 50% investor concentration limit [[sources/web-2026-03-18-a4e]] [[sources/web-2026-04-15-f75]].
- Retired Florida-specific review requirements for new, attached condominium projects [[sources/web-2026-03-18-a4e]].
- Increased the minimum reserve funding requirement from 10% to 15% of the annual budget, effective January 4, 2027 [[sources/web-2026-03-18-a4e]] [[sources/web-2026-04-15-f75]] [[sources/web-2026-03-24-bb6]]; industry coverage frames the change as adding "an additional 5% to be added to operating costs" on top of inflationary cost pressure facing boards [[sources/web-2026-04-21-260]].
- The 15% baseline applies specifically to associations that do not have an updated (2-3 year-old) professional reserve study; an association without an updated reserve study must contribute at least 15% of its annual budget toward reserve funding starting January 4, 2027, and even with the 15% contribution will remain subject to strict eligibility reviews (budget review, deferred-maintenance check, structural-issue check, special-assessment check, and possibly engineer's reports or recent financials for older buildings) [[sources/web-2026-03-24-bb6]].
- The 15% budget allocation is not required if a condominium association has a reserve study that has been conducted or updated within the last three years and the association is following the highest recommended level of funding (baseline funding is not allowed as a substitute) [[sources/web-2026-03-18-a4e]] [[sources/web-2026-04-15-f75]]; NYC market coverage characterizes the alternative window as "a reserve study completed within the past 36 months" [[sources/web-2026-04-21-260]].
- Effective August 3, 2026, the legacy 10% Baseline Funding Method is no longer acceptable to FNMA even for associations relying on an updated professional reserve study; lenders must confirm the association's budget reflects the highest recommended allocation from the updated reserve study, and the association can no longer rely on the Baseline Funding Method percentage (10% pre-2027 or 15% from 2027 onward) [[sources/web-2026-03-24-bb6]].
- If the reserve study indicates that the highest annual contribution is less than 10%, the association is generally considered to be in a strong financial position and the lower amount may be used [[sources/web-2026-03-24-bb6]].
- Reserve studies in the NYC condo/co-op market cost between $7,500 and $9,500, per Strategic Inspections president and CEO Orest Tomaselli [[sources/web-2026-04-21-260]].
- Industry commentators note that a reserve study can require a contribution far higher than 15%, making the actual reserve-funding requirement a community-by-community determination, per Tomaselli [[sources/web-2026-04-21-260]].
- Buildings will "eventually run out of that reserve study benefit," per Moritt Hock & Hamroff partner David Fitzhenry, meaning the reserve-study alternative is a time-limited workaround for buildings that do not choose to hold the full 15% [[sources/web-2026-04-21-260]].
- Without holding the 15% minimum in capital reserves, boards will increasingly find loans are not approved for their building, per Fitzhenry [[sources/web-2026-04-21-260]].
- Fitzhenry has characterized the impact of the 15% requirement as "another sticker shock" for boards already absorbing inflationary cost pressure, while acknowledging the policy is "well intentioned" [[sources/web-2026-04-21-260]].
- The baseline funding method (which essentially targets a near-zero reserve balance) is no longer permitted under any circumstances [[sources/web-2026-04-15-f75]].
- When a condominium building is flagged as ineligible for Fannie Mae financing, other lenders — including jumbo and commercial — often follow suit, per Tomaselli [[sources/web-2026-04-21-260]].
- Tomaselli has characterized the ineligibility designation as "an almost automatic reason not to lend in any capacity in the community," producing impact felt equally from affordable units to luxury condos [[sources/web-2026-04-21-260]].
- For properties that had not been compliant with the prior rules on reserves, critical repairs, or special assessments, "there is nowhere to hide" under the post-March-2026 full-review regime, per Tomaselli [[sources/web-2026-04-21-260]].
- Community-association legal commentary frames the GSE update as imposing on boards new responsibilities around budgeting (potentially higher monthly assessments and reallocation from operating funds), reserve-study currency and comprehensiveness (engineering-based recommendations fully implemented in the budget), warrantability (failure to follow the highest recommendation may render the condo non-warrantable), property values, and legal risk exposure (boards that fail to plan for foreseeable funding and maintenance needs may face owner disputes, special-assessment challenges, and potential fiduciary-duty claims) [[sources/web-2026-03-24-bb6]].
- Eliminated strict replacement cost documentation requirements for property insurance [[sources/web-2026-03-18-a4e]].
- Removed the requirement to insure roofs at full replacement cost; roofs may now be covered on an actual cash value basis, reversing a 2024 requirement that had driven premiums higher for many communities [[sources/web-2026-03-18-a4e]] [[sources/web-2026-04-15-f75]]; industry commentary characterizes this rollback as "spectacular" relief, particularly for buildings previously denied residential mortgage financing because insurance policies could not be aligned with the prior guidelines, per Tomaselli [[sources/web-2026-04-21-260]].
- Introduced greater flexibility in how coverage sufficiency is determined [[sources/web-2026-03-18-a4e]].
- Removed the inflation guard requirement for condominium association property insurance [[sources/web-2026-03-18-a4e]] [[sources/web-2026-04-15-f75]].
- Imposed a new maximum deductible cap of $50,000 per unit on master property insurance policies, effective July 1, 2026 [[sources/web-2026-03-18-a4e]] [[sources/web-2026-04-15-f75]].
- Individual unit owners still need interior coverage, typically in the form of an HO-6 policy [[sources/web-2026-04-15-f75]].
- Introduced new loan-servicer obligations: annual verification of insurance coverage, monitoring for reductions in coverage, and a new requirement to remind borrowers annually to maintain insurance, fully effective by January 1, 2027 [[sources/web-2026-03-18-a4e]].
- Maintains a published ineligible project list that condominium communities are encouraged to consult to confirm eligibility status [[sources/web-2026-03-18-a4e]].
- The full text of Lender Letter LL-2026-03 is published at singlefamily.fanniemae.com/news-events/lender-letter-ll-2026-03-updates-project-standards-property-insurance-requirements [[sources/web-2026-04-15-f75]]; the source PDF is mirrored at singlefamily.fanniemae.com/media/44986/display [[sources/web-2026-03-24-bb6]].
- Maintains the Fannie Mae Condo Project Manager at singlefamily.fanniemae.com/originating-underwriting/mortgage-products/condo-co-op-project-review as the project-review tool used by lenders [[sources/web-2026-04-15-f75]].
